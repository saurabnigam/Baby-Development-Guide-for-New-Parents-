# Module 09: Age 2 to 3 — Becoming a Person

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: selfhood, language explosion, toilet learning, and the limits of a 2-year-old's self-control
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

---

## Key Takeaways

- "Me", "mine", and "no" are not rudeness. They are the sound of a self being built.
- Your child can now **understand a rule and still be unable to follow it.** Knowing and stopping are different systems, and the stopping one is barely started.
- **Playing alongside other children, not with them, is normal at this age.** So is not sharing.
- **Toilet learning: readiness beats age, and there's no prize for early.** Evidence suggests no benefit to intensive training before about 27 months.
- Picky eating peaks now. Pressure reliably makes it worse.

---

## What's Happening Now

Between two and three, language, memory, movement, and feelings start working together much more efficiently. Your child remembers what happened yesterday, anticipates what's coming, and has genuine plans of their own.

What hasn't arrived is **inhibitory control** — the ability to stop a wanted action. That's why a two-year-old can tell you the rule about not touching the plug socket and touch it thirty seconds later, while looking at you.

They weren't lying. They knew. They just can't stop yet.

### Selfhood arrives

"Me." "Mine." "No." "I do it."

These aren't behaviour problems. They're a child working out that they are a separate person with their own wants — which is a prerequisite for everything from empathy to independence.

**A child with no "mine" has no self to share from.** The ownership phase comes first for a reason.

---

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Vocabulary grows very fast
- 2–4 word phrases, then real sentences
- Sorts by shape, colour, or type
- Follows a simple two-step instruction
- Starts counting and noticing quantity — often reciting numbers before understanding them
- Richer pretend play, with sequences
- Remembers routines and gets upset when they change

**Speech clarity:** familiar adults understand roughly **three-quarters** of what a 3-year-old says. Strangers understand less. That's normal, and it improves fast.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- Strong ownership and strong preferences
- **Parallel play** — alongside other children, occasionally interacting
- Big feelings when blocked, tired, or overloaded
- Early empathy: bringing a toy to someone who's crying, looking concerned
- Uses words for wants — but loses that ability completely under stress
- Still needs an adult to help them come down from a big feeling

**They are not manipulating you.** Manipulation needs you to model someone else's mind well enough to plan against it. That machinery isn't built yet.

What looks like manipulation is usually simpler: a child has noticed that one behaviour reliably produces one result. That's just learning.

## The Sharing Problem

**Two-year-olds do not share, and this is not a moral failing.**

Real sharing needs three things at once. You have to know the other child wants something. You have to feel their wanting is as real as yours. And you have to trust that giving it up now means getting it back later.

None of that is available yet.

### What to do instead

**Turn-taking with an external timer.**

> "It's Amir's turn now. When the timer goes, it's your turn."

Why this works and "share nicely" doesn't:

- **"When he's finished" is unbearable** — there's no end in sight, and a two-year-old cannot hold a hope that long
- **"When the timer goes" is bearable** — it's visible, it's finite, and it isn't your arbitrary decision
- The timer becomes the authority, which takes the conflict out of your relationship

**Other things that help:**

- Duplicates of the most-fought-over things, where you can
- Let them put two special things away before a friend arrives. Nobody has to share everything
- Don't force it. A child made to hand over a toy learns that bigger people take things, not that giving is good

**Real sharing usually shows up around 4 or 5**, once perspective-taking comes online. Practise turn-taking now and it arrives on schedule.

## Toilet Learning

The most pressure-laden topic of this stage, and the one where pressure works worst.

### Readiness, not age

Most guidance says start **after 18 months**. Most children finish between **24 and 36 months**.

But research suggests there is **no benefit to intensive training before about 27 months**. Starting earlier tends to mean training for longer — not finishing sooner.

**There is no prize for early.** A child trained at 2 and a child trained at 3½ end up in exactly the same place.

### Signs they're actually ready

Look for several together, not one:

- Stays dry for a couple of hours, or through a nap
- Knows when they're weeing or pooing, and tells you — even if only afterwards
- Dislikes a dirty nappy
- Can walk to the potty, and pull clothes up and down with a bit of help
- Follows simple instructions
- Shows interest in the toilet or in copying you
- **Wants to.** This one does a lot of work

### The approach that works

The AAP recommends a **child-led** approach and is explicit that children should not be forced before they are behaviourally, emotionally, and developmentally ready.

A long-running prospective study of 482 children using this approach found **61% were trained by 36 months and 98% by 48 months** — with no pushing. ([AAFP review](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2019/1015/p468.html) · [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/toilet-training/Pages/default.aspx))

**How to run it:**

1. Get a potty out and let it just exist for a while. No expectations
2. Let them sit on it clothed, then without a nappy, whenever they want
3. Let them watch you or a sibling. Children learn this by copying
4. Once they're interested, try nappy-free time at home
5. **Praise the trying, not the result.** Praise for output makes failure feel like failure
6. Expect accidents for months. Respond with a shrug and a change of clothes

### What makes it go wrong

- **Starting because of a deadline** — a nursery place, a new baby, a relative's comment
- **Pressure, bribes, or disappointment.** The one thing you cannot control is what your child's body does, so a power struggle here is one you will lose
- **Punishment for accidents.** The fastest route to withholding, constipation, and months of misery
- **Pushing on through constipation.** Painful poos teach a child to hold on. Holding on makes it more painful. If pooing hurts, fix that first — with a doctor if needed — before you continue

### Regression is normal

New baby, house move, starting nursery, illness. Step back without comment or consequence, and let them climb back up.

## Six Things to Do

### 1. Play alongside them

Sit nearby with the same materials — blocks, cars, crayons, dolls — and do your own version.

**Why:** it matches where they actually are socially, and it creates openings for copying without any pressure to interact.

### 2. Sort and match real things

Socks, spoons, lids, leaves, buttons, toy animals.

**Why:** categories are the foundation of both maths and logic — and this uses things you already own.

### 3. Pretend the ordinary

Kitchen, bedtime, doctor, bus, shop.

**Why:** symbolic thinking, sequencing, and — importantly — a way to process things that were confusing or frightening. A child who plays "doctor" for a week after an injection is digesting it.

### 4. Emotion words, all day

> "You're disappointed."
> "You wanted it now."
> "You're proud you did that."

Add "frustrated", "disappointed", "excited", and "worried" to the core four this year.

### 5. Movement plus listening games

"Jump and stop." "Find something red." "Bring me the big spoon."

**Why:** this is the earliest form of self-control practice. Hold a rule in your head. Make your body obey it. And it's a game, which is the only reason a 2-year-old will do it.

### 6. The same books, again and again

**Why:** repetition is how the words go in. A child who wants the same book forty times is not stuck — they're consolidating. Pause before the last word of a familiar line and let them fill it in.

## Feeding: Age 2 to 3

### The rule that governs everything

**The Division of Responsibility**, developed by dietitian Ellyn Satter and widely used in paediatric practice:

> **You decide** *what* food is offered, *when*, and *where*.
> **Your child decides** *whether* they eat, and *how much*.

*([Ellyn Satter Institute](https://www.ellynsatterinstitute.org/how-to-feed/division-of-responsibility/))*

Almost every mealtime battle is one side crossing into the other's half.

**You crossing into theirs looks like:** one more bite · finish your plate · three more peas · you can't get down until · eat this and you can have that · aeroplane spoons · a screen on so they'll eat more without noticing.

**What that produces**, consistently: fussier eating, worse recognition of their own fullness, and a worse long-term relationship with food. **Pressure is the single most reliably counterproductive feeding practice there is.**

### Picky eating peaks now

It's developmentally normal. A wariness of new foods probably kept toddlers alive for most of human history. It is not something you caused.

**What works:** repeated neutral exposure. There's a tested protocol in [Module 07](../07-toddler-12-18-months/README.md#the-14-day-taste) — one taste a day for fourteen days, with no comment at all.

**Always include one thing you know they'll eat.** It makes a refusal survivable for both of you and stops meals becoming a standoff.

### Drinks

For ages 2–3, AAP recommends:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Milk** | 16–24 oz (2–3 cups) a day, now low-fat or non-fat |
| **Water** | 8–40 oz (1–5 cups) a day |
| **Juice** | Up to 4 oz a day, only if whole fruit isn't available. None is better |
| **Avoid** | Sugary drinks, flavoured milk, caffeine, artificial sweeteners |

### Also worth knowing

- Appetite swings wildly day to day. Judge by the week
- Growth has slowed a lot since infancy, so they eat less than you expect
- **Involve them in food** — washing, stirring, tearing, choosing at the shop. Children eat what they helped make. They resist what simply appeared
- Watch for constipation. It affects mood, sleep, appetite, and toilet learning all at once

## Toys

| Thing | What it builds |
|-------|---------------|
| Playdough with a roller and cutters | Fine motor, creativity, and it's genuinely calming |
| Large connecting bricks | Building, planning, spatial thinking |
| Balance bike or trike | Big movement, coordination, independence |
| Dress-up clothes and props | Pretend play, trying on identities |
| Puzzles, 6–12 pieces | Problem-solving, persistence |
| Toy animals, vehicles, or small figures | Storytelling, imaginative play |
| Art supplies — crayons, paint, stickers, paper | Expression, fine motor |
| Sand and water play | Sensory regulation, pouring, early physics |
| Picture books with simple stories | Vocabulary, comprehension, feelings |

**Rotate.** Keep 5–8 things out and put the rest away. Fewer choices reliably produce deeper, longer play — try it for a fortnight and watch what happens.

## Common Worries

### "They won't share"

See above. They're not supposed to yet. Use turn-taking and a timer.

### "Everything is no"

Autonomy. Reduce yes/no questions — "time for the bath" rather than "shall we have a bath?" Offer two acceptable choices instead. Save your own "no" for things that matter, so it keeps its force.

### Tantrums in public

The problem is usually the audience, not the child.

Get low, get quiet, ignore everyone else, and leave if you need to — calmly, without shaming them on the way out. Other people's opinions are not a factor in your child's development.

Full playbook in [Module 24](../24-behaviour-and-discipline/README.md#part-6-the-playbook).

### "Everyone else's child is toilet trained"

They're mostly not, and the ones who are were often ready earlier. See above. **There is no advantage to early.**

### Speech that's hard to understand

Familiar adults understanding around three-quarters of a 3-year-old's speech is normal.

**Worth checking if:** *you* often can't understand them. They aren't putting words together at all by around 30 months. They've stopped using words they had. Or you have any doubt about their hearing.

Ask for a hearing test. It's quick. Glue ear is common at this age and quietly affects everything.

---

## Red Flags: Call a Doctor If

- Very limited language progress, or **no two-word phrases by 30 months**
- **No pretend play**
- Very little interest in other people, adults or children
- **Loses skills** they previously had — always, at any age
- Extreme distress at everyday sounds, textures, clothing, or food, enough to affect daily life
- Behaviour that is extreme and persistent across every setting, not just at home
- You suspect a hearing or vision problem

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Pick one feeling word and use it all week.**

Choose "frustrated". Then use it every single time you see it — in your child, in yourself, in a book, in a stranger's toddler at the park.

> "That's frustrating."
> "I'm frustrated — this jar won't open."
> "He looks frustrated. His tower fell."

One word, many repetitions, attached to real moments. That's how emotional vocabulary actually gets built — not by teaching a list, but by naming the same thing enough times that the word arrives with the feeling.
