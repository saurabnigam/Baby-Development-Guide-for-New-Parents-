# Module 05: Infant — 6 to 9 Months

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: exploring, object permanence, starting solids, and a baby who suddenly moves
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md). If your baby was born early, use their **corrected age**.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Your baby switches from watching the world to **poking it**. Dropping a spoon forty times is an experiment, not naughtiness.
- **Object permanence** arrives — they work out that things still exist when hidden. This is why peekaboo suddenly becomes hilarious and why you leaving the room suddenly becomes a problem.
- They start **checking your face** before deciding whether something is safe. Your expression is now information.
- Solids usually start around 6 months. **Iron is the reason**, more than calories.
- Introducing common allergens early **prevents** allergies. Delaying makes them more likely. This reversed the old advice completely.
- Once they're mobile, your home needs a fresh safety pass — done at floor level.

---

## What's Happening Now

Three big things are arriving at once, and they interact.

**Memory improves.** Your baby can now hold a picture of something in mind that isn't in front of them.

**The body gets useful.** Sitting frees up both hands. Then comes crawling, shuffling, or rolling toward things they want.

**People sort into categories.** Familiar. Safe. Unfamiliar. Uncertain.

Put those together and you get a baby who can want a specific thing, remember where it went, travel to it, and check your face on the way.

### Why object permanence changes everything

Before it, "gone" means gone. After it, "gone" means *somewhere else*.

This is why:

- Peekaboo becomes the funniest thing ever — they now know your face is behind your hands, so the reveal is a joke rather than a surprise
- They look for a toy you hide under a cloth instead of losing interest
- **Separation gets harder.** They can now hold you in mind after you've left the room, and miss you. That's a cognitive achievement showing up as a hard evening

### Social referencing: your face is now data

Something new happens: your baby encounters something unfamiliar, and **looks at you before deciding how to feel about it.**

This is a big deal. They've worked out that you know things they don't.

> **A worked example — the dog**
>
> A friendly labrador walks up to the pram. Your baby stares at it, then turns and looks straight at you.
>
> **If your face says alarm** — eyes wide, sharp intake of breath, pulling the pram back — they will usually be frightened, and may well be frightened of dogs next time too.
>
> **If your face says interest** — relaxed, smiling, leaning slightly toward it — they will usually be curious.
>
> **What to do:** say the calm thing out loud, because your voice is part of the message. *"That's a dog. He's saying hello. Shall we look at him?"*
>
> **And when something genuinely is dangerous, use it deliberately.** A clear, serious face and a firm "hot — no" carries far more weight now than at any earlier age. You have a channel you didn't have before. Spend it on the things that matter and stay relaxed about the rest.

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Looks for a dropped or half-hidden object
- Recognises familiar routines and games
- Explores by banging, shaking, passing things hand to hand, and dropping them
- Notices differences in size, sound, and texture
- Starts doing one thing **on purpose** to make something else happen
- Solves small problems by trying things

**Why the dropping matters.** A baby dropping a spoon from the high chair for the fortieth time is running an experiment: *does it fall every time? Does it sound the same? Does she pick it up every time?*

That last one is a study of you, and it's why it's so hard to stop. Give them a legal outlet — a basket of things they're allowed to drop — and it gets much easier.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- Strong preference for familiar people
- **Stranger wariness** often appears
- Looks at your face when unsure
- Clearer joy, frustration, caution, and anticipation
- Enjoys repeated social games and starts to initiate them

**Why stranger anxiety is good news.** A baby who is wary of strangers has built a reliable category of *my people*. It's evidence the attachment system is working, not evidence of a shy or difficult temperament.

## Six Things to Do

### 1. Hide and find

Cover a toy partly with a cloth. Then fully. Wait.

**Why:** builds object permanence, working memory, and persistence.

**Do:** don't rescue it too fast. Let them work. If they lose interest, uncover a corner and try again.

### 2. Containers

A bowl and a few large safe objects to take out and put in.

**Why:** in and out, full and empty, and a lot of hand control. Filling and emptying is one of the most reliably absorbing activities at this age.

**Note:** taking *out* comes months before putting *in*. Don't expect both.

### 3. A floor space that's genuinely theirs

A safe area with a few interesting things at floor level.

**Why:** self-directed movement is how babies learn about space, distance, and their own bodies. A baby who crawls to a thing learns more than a baby handed the thing.

**Do:** floor time beats time in bouncers, seats, and walkers — those hold a baby in a position they can't get into or out of themselves.

### 4. Cause and effect

Anything where a clear action produces a clear result. Shakers, crinkle books, a single big button that pops something up.

**Why:** teaches consequence and prediction, which is the root of experimental thinking.

**Do:** simpler beats fancier. A toy that does everything by itself teaches nothing.

### 5. Answer the look

When they look at something new and then at you, respond clearly and calmly. See the dog example above.

### 6. Name the feeling

Long before they understand the words:

> "That surprised you."
> "You're cross because it rolled away."
> "You're pleased with yourself."

**Why:** builds a map between body states and language, and — genuinely useful — it helps *you* stay regulated, because naming a feeling makes it easier to be around.

## Starting Solids

The big event of this stage.

### When: readiness, not a date

Around **6 months**, and **never before 4 months**. But the calendar is the second thing to check. The first is your baby.

**Look for these together, not just one:**

- Sits with support, with good steady head and neck control
- Opens their mouth when food comes
- Can move food back and swallow it, rather than pushing it all out
- Brings things to their mouth
- Interested in reaching for and grabbing things

**Being interested in your food is not a readiness sign on its own.** Babies are interested in everything you do.

Your paediatrician may advise a different timing for your baby — follow that over any general guidance. ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infant-and-young-child-feeding) · [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/feeding-nutrition/Pages/Starting-Solid-Foods.aspx))

### Milk is still the main event

For the whole of this stage, breast milk or formula remains the primary nutrition. Solids are practice, plus specific nutrients — not a replacement.

**Offer solids after a milk feed**, once a day at first. A baby who is starving will not be interested in learning.

### Why iron is the actual reason

Babies are born with an iron store. Around 6 months, it runs low — and breast milk is low in iron.

Iron matters for brain development specifically: oxygen transport, myelination, and the chemistry of attention and learning. Early iron deficiency is associated with worse cognitive and behavioural outcomes, and some effects persist even after it's corrected.

So: **iron-rich foods first and often.** Not baby rice for three weeks.

**Good iron sources:**

- Well-cooked **whole** egg, mashed *(yolk alone gives you less iron and skips the allergen exposure)*
- Lentils, dal, beans, mashed and thinned
- Meat, chicken, or fish, pureed or very soft — if your family eats them
- Iron-fortified infant cereal
- Tofu

**Pair plant-based iron with vitamin C** — tomato, citrus, guava, amla, capsicum, berries. It meaningfully improves how much iron gets absorbed.

### A worked example — the first spoonful

Most parents expect this to go better than it does. Here's what normal looks like.

> **Setup:** mid-morning, after a milk feed, when everyone's in a decent mood. Not 5pm. Not when they're overtired.
>
> Baby sits upright in a high chair. Bib on. Phone away.
>
> **You:** offer half a teaspoon of smooth dal on a soft spoon. Hold it near their mouth and **wait for them to open.** Don't push it in.
>
> **What happens:** they take it. Then they make a face like you have poisoned them. Then most of it comes back out.
>
> **What that means:** nothing bad. That face is a reaction to a completely new texture and taste, not a verdict. And the tongue-push reflex is still fading — food coming back out is mechanical, not a refusal.
>
> **You:** stay neutral. Don't laugh, don't perform, don't say "yummy!" in a big voice. Just: *"That's dal."* Offer another half-teaspoon.
>
> **Two or three spoons in, they turn their head away.** Meal over. That's a completely successful first session — you were aiming for two teaspoons, not a bowl.
>
> **What not to do:** don't chase the head with the spoon. Don't do the aeroplane. Don't try to get one more in. All of those teach that eating is something done *to* them, and every one of them makes the next meal harder.

**Repeat, boringly, for days.** Acceptance comes from calm repetition, and the calm is doing as much work as the repetition.

### The first two weeks

| When | What |
|------|------|
| Days 1–3 | 1–2 teaspoons of a smooth, thin puree, once a day. Iron-rich if you can |
| Days 4–7 | Still once a day. Add one new single-ingredient food every few days |
| Week 2 | Slightly more, a bit more variety. Keep iron-rich foods frequent |
| By 8–9 months | 2–3 small meals a day alongside milk feeds, moving to lumpier textures |

**Texture progression:** smooth puree → thicker and lumpier → soft mashed pieces by around 8–9 months. Don't stay on smooth purees too long; babies who never meet lumps often struggle with them later.

### Purees or baby-led weaning?

Both work. Honestly:

- Studies find **baby-led weaning doesn't increase choking risk** compared with spoon-feeding purees, provided food is prepared safely
- But nutrient intake is more variable with baby-led weaning, and **iron in particular is easier to miss**
- Most families end up doing both — some finger foods, some loaded spoons, some purees. That's fine and probably sensible

**Whichever you choose, the iron question is the same.** Make sure iron-rich food is actually going in, not just being explored.

### Foods to avoid entirely

| Avoid | Why |
|-------|-----|
| **Honey** | Risk of infant botulism. Nothing before 12 months, including in cooked food |
| **Cow's milk as a drink** | Not before 12 months. In food is fine |
| **Added salt** | Their kidneys can't handle it |
| **Added sugar** | Shapes taste preference and damages teeth |
| **Whole nuts, whole grapes, hard raw pieces** | Choking |
| **Rice drinks** | Arsenic content |
| **Juice** | Not needed. Nothing before 12 months |

## Allergens: The Advice Has Reversed

For years parents were told to **delay** peanuts, eggs, and other allergens. That advice was wrong, and delaying appears to have made things worse.

### The study that changed it

The **LEAP trial** enrolled 640 infants aged 4–11 months who were at high risk — they had severe eczema, egg allergy, or both. Half were given peanut protein regularly; half avoided peanut entirely until age 5.

At 5 years old ([Du Toit et al., 2015, *New England Journal of Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705822/)):

| Group | Avoided peanut | Ate peanut regularly |
|-------|---------------|---------------------|
| Babies with a negative initial skin test (n=530) | **13.7%** had peanut allergy | **1.9%** |
| Babies with a positive initial skin test (n=98) | **35.3%** had peanut allergy | **10.6%** |

That's roughly an **81% relative reduction**, in the children at highest risk. Guidelines changed within two years.

### What to actually do

The common allergens: **peanut, egg, dairy, wheat, soy, sesame, tree nuts, fish, shellfish.**

1. **Start once your baby has managed a few basic foods** — usually a week or two in.
2. **One new allergen at a time**, with a few days between.
3. **Start tiny.** A quarter-teaspoon. Increase over following days if all is well.
4. **At home, earlier in the day**, when you can watch them for a few hours and get help if needed. Not at nursery, not on holiday, not at bedtime.
5. **Keep it in the diet** — roughly twice a week once introduced. Tolerance can be lost if a food disappears again.

**Safe forms:**

- **Peanut:** smooth peanut butter thinned with warm water, breast milk, formula, or puree until it's runny. Or peanut powder stirred in. **Never whole nuts. Never a thick blob** — thick nut butter is a genuine choking hazard.
- **Egg:** well-cooked, whole, mashed.
- **Sesame:** tahini, thinned the same way as peanut butter.
- **Tree nuts:** smooth nut butters, thinned.

### Talk to a doctor first if

Your baby has **severe eczema**, an existing food allergy, or a strong family history. These are exactly the babies who benefit most from early introduction — and also the ones who may need it done under supervision or after testing.

Don't skip it out of fear. Get it planned. ([AAP, 2023 — *Updates in Food Allergy Prevention in Children*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37818612/))

### Signs of a reaction

**Mild:** a few hives, a rash around the mouth, mild vomiting.

> Redness just around the mouth from acidic foods like tomato or citrus is usually contact irritation, not allergy.

**Call emergency services immediately for:** difficulty breathing, wheezing, swelling of the lips, tongue, or face, repeated vomiting, floppiness, or pale or blue colour.

## Choking: The Part People Skip

Hazards get listed everywhere. What to *do* rarely does.

### Gagging is not choking

You will see a lot of gagging. It is loud, alarming, and **protective** — it's the reflex pushing food back forward. Babies gag frequently when learning to eat, and the reflex sits further forward on the tongue in infancy, which is why it triggers so easily.

| | Gagging | Choking |
|---|---------|---------|
| Sound | Noisy — coughing, retching, spluttering | **Silent**, or a high-pitched squeak |
| Face | Red | Turning blue or grey |
| What to do | **Stay calm. Don't intervene.** Let them work it out | **Act immediately** |

**Resist the urge to reach into their mouth.** A blind finger sweep can push an object further down.

### The rules

- **Always upright**, in a high chair or on a lap. Never lying down, never in a moving car or pram
- **Always supervised.** Never leave them alone with food
- **Never prop a bottle**
- No eating while crawling, walking, or being bounced

### The hazard list

Whole nuts · whole grapes and cherry tomatoes (**quarter them lengthways**) · popcorn · raw hard carrot or apple · chunks of meat or cheese · hard sweets · marshmallows · **thick blobs of nut butter** · sausage rounds (cut lengthways, never in coins)

### Learn what to do — properly

**Take an in-person infant choking and CPR class.** This is the single highest-value hour available to you at this stage. Back blows and chest thrusts on an infant are different from the adult manoeuvre, and this is not something to learn from a page or a video while it's happening. ([AAP — choking prevention](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/Choking-Prevention.aspx))

## What They Need: 6 to 9 Months

| Marker | Typical |
|--------|---------|
| First solids | Around 6 months; never before 4 |
| Starting amount | 1–2 teaspoons once a day, building gradually |
| By 8–9 months | 2–3 small meals a day, plus milk feeds |
| Priority foods | **Iron-rich** — whole egg, lentils, beans, meat, fortified cereal, tofu |
| Texture | Smooth → lumpier → soft mashed pieces by 8–9 months |
| Milk | Still the main nutrition source |
| Vitamin D | 400 IU a day, unless taking over ~27oz/800ml of formula |
| Water | Small amounts in an open cup with meals is fine now |
| Never | Honey, cow's milk as a drink, added salt or sugar, whole nuts or grapes |

Expect mess, refusal, and outraged faces. All normal.

## Toys

Babies now sit, use both hands, and explore on purpose.

| Thing | What it builds |
|-------|---------------|
| Stacking cups | Nesting, size, filling and emptying |
| Soft cloth blocks | Inspecting, early stacking, safe to mouth |
| Textured balls | Rolling, chasing, different feels |
| A single-button pop-up toy | Clear cause and effect |
| Wooden rattle or egg shaker | Sound, rhythm, grip |
| Board books, one clear picture a page | Naming, shared attention |
| Suction bowl and textured spoon | Joining in at mealtimes |
| Bath pouring cups | Water, volume, early physics |
| Soft toy with a clear face | Comfort and early social play |

**The principle:** things that respond clearly to what the baby does. A toy that performs by itself while the baby watches is doing the interesting part for them.

## Safety: Everything Changes When They Move

Do a fresh safety pass now — and do it **on your hands and knees**, at their eye level. You will see things you have never noticed.

- **Stair gates**, top and bottom
- **Furniture and TVs anchored to the wall.** Tip-overs are a leading cause of serious household injury in under-5s
- **Cupboard latches** on anything with cleaning products or medicines. High up is not enough once they climb
- **Blind cords** cut short or secured
- **Button and coin-cell batteries locked away.** These cause severe internal burns within hours and are a genuine emergency — check remotes, thermometers, greetings cards, scales, toys
- **Water:** buckets, toilets, pet bowls, paddling pools. A baby this age can drown in a few centimetres and cannot right themselves. Empty everything after use, keep the bathroom door shut. See [Module 23](../23-life-skills-critical-moments/README.md#part-3-water--swimming)
- **The toilet-roll test:** if an object fits through a toilet roll tube, it's a choking hazard
- **Never leave them on a raised surface**, ever

## Common Worries

### Stranger anxiety

Normal, healthy, and often worst with people who try hardest.

**What helps:** let new people approach slowly and sideways rather than head-on. Stay physically close. Let your baby watch from your arms for a few minutes before anyone tries to hold them. **Don't hand them over to prove they're friendly** — that teaches that their signals don't count.

Tell relatives in advance. "She needs ten minutes to warm up, then she's great" saves everyone's feelings.

### Separation getting harder

This is the cost of object permanence. They can now miss you.

**What helps:** short predictable goodbyes, the same words every time, and a warm reunion. **Don't sneak out.** It avoids one bad minute and buys you a baby who now has to monitor you constantly.

Practise tiny separations — leave the room for thirty seconds, call out cheerfully, come back.

### Throwing everything

Testing gravity, enjoying the noise, and studying whether you always pick it up.

**What helps:** a basket of things they're allowed to throw or drop. Turn it into a game sometimes. Remove the food tray when the meal has clearly become a physics lab.

### "They won't eat anything"

At this stage food is practice. **Milk is still the nutrition.** Weight gain over weeks matters; any single meal does not.

Get help if wet nappies drop, weight is a concern, they cough or choke often during feeds, or feeding is consistently distressing.

## Red Flags: Call a Doctor If

- Little interest in people or in back-and-forth games
- Doesn't sit with support, or moves noticeably more on one side than the other
- Doesn't reach for or explore objects
- Rarely makes sounds, or doesn't respond to sound
- Doesn't look at you when something surprising happens
- **Loses a skill they had before**
- Persistent difficulty swallowing, or frequent coughing and choking during feeds

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Play hide-and-find, and don't rescue.**

Take a favourite toy. Cover it half-way with a cloth while they watch. Then wait.

Say nothing for ten seconds. Let them look, reach, fail, try again. If they get frustrated, uncover a bit more — don't hand it over.

When they get it: *"You found it!"*

Five minutes, no equipment, and you've worked memory, persistence, and the discovery that a problem can be solved by not giving up.
