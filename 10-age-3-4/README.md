# Module 10: Age 3 to 4 — Imagination & Other Minds

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: pretend worlds, the discovery that other people think differently, and the first real friendships
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Something big happens this year: your child works out that **other people can know different things than they do.** Almost everything social follows from that.
- The endless "why" is not a stalling tactic. It's a child building a model of how the world works.
- Cooperative play starts — badly, with a lot of conflict. That conflict is the practice, not a sign it's going wrong.
- Imaginary friends and imagination-based fears are normal and often arrive together.
- **This is the window to sort out swimming.** Drowning is a leading cause of death for young children, and formal lessons work well from around this age.

---

## The Big One: Other People Have Minds

The defining change between 3 and 4 is that your child starts to understand that **what someone believes can be different from what's actually true.**

### The test researchers use

It's beautifully simple. A child watches this happen:

> Maxi puts his chocolate in the **blue** cupboard and goes out to play.
> While he's gone, his mum moves the chocolate to the **green** cupboard.
> Maxi comes back and wants his chocolate.
>
> **"Where will Maxi look?"**

**Most 3-year-olds say the green cupboard.** They know where the chocolate is, so they assume Maxi knows too. They can't yet hold the idea that someone else has a picture of the world that's out of date.

**Most 4- and 5-year-olds say the blue cupboard** — and often laugh, because they can see the joke.

### How solid is this?

Very. Researchers pooled **178 separate studies** and found the same developmental pattern held up across different countries and many variations of the task: preschoolers move from getting it wrong more often than chance to getting it right more often than chance.

Their conclusion was that this is **a genuine change in how children understand minds** — not just children getting better at a confusing question. ([Wellman, Cross & Watson, 2001, *Child Development*](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11405571))

### Why it matters to you at 4pm on a Tuesday

Almost everything you want socially depends on this:

- **Lying** starts around now — and it requires knowing your mind holds different information from theirs. Unnerving, but a genuine cognitive milestone
- **Empathy** deepens, because "how would you feel?" becomes answerable
- **Persuasion, negotiation, and keeping secrets** all become possible
- **Real friendship** becomes possible, because you can now think about what your friend wants

It also explains why your 3-year-old tells you a story you cannot follow. They don't yet grasp that you weren't there and don't have the background. That's not poor storytelling — it's the same skill still under construction.

**What to do:** ask questions that make them supply what you're missing. *"Wait — who is Bella?"* You are teaching them to model your knowledge.

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Longer sentences and much bigger vocabulary
- Understands "now", "later", "after", "yesterday" — roughly
- Counts, names some colours and shapes
- Builds pretend scenarios that run for a while and have plots
- Follows multi-step routines with reminders
- **Asks "why" and "how" constantly**
- Better memory, sequencing, and sorting

### The "why" phase, and how not to kill it

The questions aren't random and they aren't (usually) stalling. Your child is building causal models.

**Rotate your answers.** Don't only ever explain.

| Response | When |
|----------|------|
| **Answer it** | It's factual and you know it |
| **"What do you think?"** | Most of the time. Their theory is usually interesting |
| **"I don't know. Good question."** | Say this often. It teaches that not knowing is fine |
| **"Let's find out."** Then actually do | Once or twice a week |

**Why the third one matters:** a parent who always has an answer teaches that not knowing is embarrassing. A parent who cheerfully doesn't know teaches that the edge of knowledge is an interesting place.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- Moves from playing alongside to short bursts of playing *with*
- Negotiates with other children, badly at first
- Understands and uses more feeling words
- Can wait briefly, with support
- **Fears based on imagination** — monsters, the dark, things under the bed
- **Imaginary friends are common and completely healthy**
- Starting to notice fairness

## Coaching Peer Conflict

Cooperative play means conflict. Your instinct will be to find out who was wrong. Resist it.

**Adjudicating teaches children to build a case against each other. Narrating teaches them to consider another mind** — which is exactly the skill that just came online.

### A worked example

> **Two 4-year-olds. One digger. Loud screaming.**
>
> **What usually happens**
>
> "What happened? Who had it first? Sam, did you snatch? Say sorry. Right, if you can't share it, nobody gets it."
>
> **What that taught:** get your story in first, and adults resolve things by taking stuff away.
>
> **What to do instead**
>
> **1. Stop the action, not the feeling.** Get between them, low, calm.
> > "Stop. Everyone's safe. Hands down."
>
> **2. Narrate both sides. Don't judge either.**
> > "Sam, you were building and you weren't finished. Aria, you'd been waiting and it felt like forever."
>
> *[Pause here. Let both of them feel heard before either has to give anything up. This is the step everyone skips, and it's the one that works.]*
>
> **3. Name the actual problem.**
> > "You both want the same digger. That's a real problem."
>
> **4. Hand it back to them.**
> > "What could work for both of you?"
>
> At 3 they'll have nothing. At 4 you'll sometimes get something workable — and that's the skill being born.
>
> **5. If they can't, you decide — but say why.**
> > "Right. Timer for five minutes, then it's Aria's turn. Sam, I'll tell you when it's coming."
>
> **6. Skip the forced apology.** Try instead:
> > "Look at her face. What do you see?"
> > "What would help right now?"

**A child who fetches a tissue has done more real repair than a child who said the word.** More on this in [Module 21](../21-building-eq/README.md#move-4-stop-forcing-apologies-teach-repair-instead).

## Imagination-Based Fears

Monsters, the dark, shadows, drains, dogs, being flushed away. These usually arrive at exactly the same time as the flowering of pretend play — because they're made of the same material.

**What helps:**

- **Take the feeling seriously, even though the monster isn't real.** "There's no such thing" is true and useless. "That sounds frightening. I'm here" works
- Keep bedtime predictable and boring
- One or two rituals — a night light, a door left open a specific amount, a soft toy on guard
- **Don't invent elaborate anti-monster machinery.** "Monster spray" confirms that monsters are the kind of thing that needs spraying. Better: "monsters aren't real, and I'm right here, and I'll always come"
- Cut scary media, including things that seem mild. A 3-year-old cannot reliably tell a story from a fact

**Don't shame it.** Fear at this age is a sign of a working imagination, not of weakness.

## Six Things to Do

### 1. Open-ended pretend worlds

Boxes, blocks, scarves, dolls, animals, kitchen bits.

**Why:** planning, perspective-taking, and flexible problem-solving. Also, a plain box genuinely outperforms most electronic toys for sustained play, because the child has to supply everything.

### 2. Retell the story

After a book: *"What happened first? What happened next? How did she feel?"*

**Why:** memory, sequencing, comprehension, and emotional understanding at once. Shared book reading with discussion is repeatedly linked to social and emotional skills as well as language ([review](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40893858)).

### 3. Turn-taking games

Rolling a ball, simple board games, cooperative building.

**Why:** waiting, frustration tolerance, and losing. **Losing gracefully is the actual skill** — coach the recovery, not the winning.

### 4. Perspective questions

In books and in life:

> "Why do you think she's sad?"
> "What do you think he wants?"
> "What would help?"

**Why:** this is the direct exercise for the ability that's just arriving.

### 5. Regulation tools, taught when calm

- Breathe out slowly like a dragon
- Push against a wall
- Jump ten times
- Squeeze your hands

**Teach these when nothing is wrong.** A tool introduced mid-meltdown gets rejected, and then it's contaminated.

### 6. Talk during real tasks

Cooking, washing up, walking, shopping, gardening.

**Why:** vocabulary in real context, and this is where "decontextualised" talk starts — talking about things that aren't in front of you, which is the language school runs on.

## Swimming: Do This Now

**Drowning is among the leading causes of death for children aged 1–4.** It is fast and it is silent — a drowning child doesn't splash or shout.

Ages 3 to 5 is when most children can start formal lessons and actually build water competence.

**Full guidance, including the layers of protection that matter more than lessons, is in [Module 23, Part 3](../23-life-skills-critical-moments/README.md#part-3-water--swimming).** The short version:

- Lessons reduce risk. **They do not make a child drown-proof** — no such thing exists
- **Barriers and supervision matter more than skill.** Four-sided pool fencing, and an adult whose only job is watching
- Armbands and inflatable rings are toys, not safety equipment
- Learn CPR

## Feeding: Age 3 to 4

**Structure over persuasion.** Regular meals and snacks, water in between, and one familiar food on every plate.

### What they need

| | |
|---|---|
| Energy | Roughly 1,200–1,400 kcal a day |
| Iron | **7mg/day at age 3; 10mg/day from age 4** |
| Calcium | **700mg/day at age 3; 1,000mg/day from age 4** |
| Vitamin D | 600 IU a day |
| Protein | ~13g/day at 3; ~19g/day from 4 |
| Milk | 16–24 oz (2–3 cups), low-fat or non-fat |
| Water | 8–40 oz (1–5 cups) |
| Juice | Up to 4 oz a day at most. None is better |

### Two things that work at this age

**Cook with them.** Washing, stirring, tearing, sprinkling, pouring. Children eat what they helped make.

**Peers do what you can't.** Nursery and playdates change food acceptance more than any parental strategy — a child who won't touch a pepper at home will eat one because Priya did.

### Watch constipation

Common, and it drives poor appetite, bad sleep, and irritable behaviour all at once, usually without anyone connecting them.

## Toys

| Thing | What it builds |
|-------|---------------|
| Art supplies — paint, scissors, glue, collage | Creativity, fine motor, planning |
| Pretend sets — doctor, shop, tools | Role play, vocabulary, social understanding |
| Magnetic tiles or wooden building sets | Space, structure, persistence |
| Simple board games — roll-and-move, colour matching | Turn-taking, rules, losing |
| Puzzles, 12–24 pieces | Problem-solving, sustained focus |
| Outdoor kit — slide, sandpit, balance beam | Big movement, calibrated risk, social play |
| Simple instruments | Rhythm, coordination |
| Books with real emotional content | Vocabulary, empathy |
| Magnifier, bug pot, nature bits | Curiosity, close observation |

**The best toys leave room for the child.** Feature count is a poor guide; open-endedness is a good one.

## Common Worries

### "They lie"

Lying starts now and it's a cognitive milestone, not a character flaw.

**What helps:** don't set traps. Asking "did you break this?" when you know they did forces a lie and then punishes it. Say instead: *"I see the cup is broken. Let's clear it up."*

Make truth pay: *"If you tell me what really happened, the consequence gets smaller. Always."* Then hold to it exactly, every time.

### "They're bossy"

Usually emerging social planning without the flexibility to go with it.

Teach the scripts — *"Can I have a turn when you're done?"*, *"Do you want to play the shop game?"* — and shape it rather than shaming it. This is leadership that hasn't learned manners yet.

### "They have an imaginary friend"

Completely normal, associated with rich imagination, and often a way of practising social scenarios or offloading blame. Play along. It passes.

### "Nobody understands them"

By around 4, strangers should follow most of what your child says. If **you** frequently can't, or a stranger rarely can, ask for a speech and hearing check.

## Red Flags: Call a Doctor If

- Speech is hard for familiar adults to understand
- Play is very repetitive with little or no pretend
- Consistently avoids interacting with other children or adults
- Emotional outbursts are extreme, constant, and not improving with support
- **Loses language, social skills, or play skills** — always, at any age
- Not answering simple "what/where" questions by around 4

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**After your next story, ask exactly two questions:**

> "How do you think she felt?"
> "What do you think happens next?"

That's it. One feeling question and one thinking question, on a book you were reading anyway.

Do it every night for a fortnight and you'll notice them starting to volunteer the answers before you ask — which means the habit has moved inside.
