# Module 02: Milestones — What They Mean and What They Don't

> **Time**: 2 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **For**: All parents and caregivers
>
> A map of what usually develops when, from birth to 18 — and, just as importantly, how to read it without losing sleep.

---

## Key Takeaways

- A milestone is a skill **most** children have by a certain age. It is not a deadline, a test, or a score.
- In 2022 the CDC rewrote its milestone checklists so they now show what **75% of children** can do — not the average. This changed a lot, and most parenting content online hasn't caught up.
- **Two-thirds of the milestones they moved were moved to older ages.** The old lists were flagging too many perfectly typical children.
- Watch the **pattern over months**, not a single skill on a single day.
- One thing overrides everything else on this page: **losing a skill they already had is always worth a phone call**, at any age.

---

## What Milestones Are For

Doctors use them for **surveillance** — a rough check that development is heading the right way, so that children who need extra help get spotted early.

That's the whole job. They are not a scoring system, and a child hitting things early is not being ranked above one hitting them late.

**The most common mistake** is reading a milestone as a deadline and starting a countdown. The second most common is comparing two children who are both completely normal.

---

## The 2022 Rewrite: Why the Old Numbers Were Scaring People

This matters, because most milestone charts you'll find online are still using the old system.

**The old checklists showed what an *average* child could do** — the 50th percentile. Think about what that means: if the chart says babies walk at 12 months, then **half of all normal babies are "behind" on their first birthday.** By design.

That's a machine for generating unnecessary panic.

**The new checklists show what 75% of children can do.** If your child hasn't got there, they're in the smaller group, and that's genuinely worth mentioning to a doctor — rather than being one of the fifty percent who were always going to arrive later.

### What actually changed

A working group of the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics went through every milestone ([Zubler et al., 2022, *Pediatrics*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35132439/)):

| Change | Amount |
|--------|--------|
| Milestones removed | **26.4% fewer** overall |
| Milestones replaced with new ones | **40.9%** |
| Retained milestones moved to a different age | about one third |
| **Of those moved — how many went to an OLDER age** | **67.7%** |
| New checklists added | 15 months and 30 months |

Read the fourth row again. **When they checked the evidence, two out of three moved milestones turned out to happen later than the old charts said.**

### Two specific changes worth knowing

**Crawling was removed entirely.** Not because crawling doesn't happen, but because there was no good evidence about when 75% of children do it — and because plenty of healthy children never crawl on hands and knees at all. The WHO's study of 816 children found **4.3% skipped it completely**, going from sitting to pulling up or shuffling on their bottoms.

If your baby doesn't crawl, nothing is wrong. Crawling is a common route, not a required one.

**"Walks alone" moved from 12 months to 15 months.** And "rolls over" moved from 4 months to 6.

## The Big Ideas Behind the Map

You don't need these to use this guide. They're here because you'll meet the names, and it helps to know which parts have held up.

### Piaget — children think differently, not just less

Jean Piaget's insight was that children aren't small adults with less information. They reason in genuinely different ways at different ages.

| Stage | Age | What it's like |
|-------|-----|---------------|
| **Sensorimotor** | 0–2 | Learning by touching, moving, mouthing. Works out that hidden things still exist |
| **Preoperational** | 2–7 | Symbols and language explode. Pretend play. Still sees the world mostly from their own viewpoint |
| **Concrete operational** | 7–11 | Real logic, about real things. Understands that a squashed ball of clay is still the same amount |
| **Formal operational** | 12+ | Abstract thinking. "What if?" Arguments, ethics, hypotheticals |

> **Where Piaget was wrong.** His *sequence* has held up well. His *ages* were too pessimistic, because his tests required babies to reach and grab — so a baby who understood something but couldn't coordinate a reach looked like they didn't.
>
> Later researchers used a cleverer method: show a baby something impossible and see if they stare longer. Renée Baillargeon's studies found babies as young as **3½ to 5 months** appear to know that a hidden object still exists — over a year earlier than Piaget concluded. ([Overview of the method](https://infantcognition.web.illinois.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Margoni-F.-Surian-L.-Baillargeon-R.-2024.-The-violation-of-expectation-paradigm-A-conceptual-overview.-Psychological-Review-1313-716-748.pdf))
>
> Also: not everyone reaches formal operational thinking in every subject. Most adults reason abstractly about things they know well and concretely about things they don't.

### Erikson — the question a child is working on

Erik Erikson described development as a series of questions, each one the emotional job of that stage.

| Age | The question | If it goes well |
|-----|-------------|----------------|
| 0–18 months | "Can I trust this world?" | Hope and security |
| 18 months–3 years | "Can I do things myself?" | Will and independence |
| 3–5 years | "Is it okay to try things?" | Purpose and initiative |
| 5–12 years | "Am I any good at anything?" | Competence and confidence |
| 12–18 years | "Who am I?" | A sense of identity |

This is a framework rather than a tested theory, but it's a genuinely useful lens. When a two-year-old fights you over putting on their own shoe, they are working on question two. That reframe alone changes how you respond.

### Vygotsky — learning happens just past the edge

Lev Vygotsky's idea: there's what a child can do alone, and what they can do **with a bit of help.** The gap between them is where learning happens — the **zone of proximal development**.

Too easy, and nothing is learned. Too hard, and they shut down. Just past the edge, with support, is the sweet spot.

The practical version is **scaffolding** — help just enough, then take the help away. Like scaffolding on a building: temporary, and removed once the structure stands up.

## Attachment: An Honest Look at the Numbers

Attachment describes the pattern of a child's bond with a specific caregiver. It's usually measured with a lab procedure that watches how a toddler reacts to a brief separation and, crucially, the **reunion**.

| Pattern | What the caregiving usually looks like | How the child tends to behave |
|---------|--------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Secure** | Warm, fairly consistent, responsive | Explores freely, seeks comfort when upset, settles |
| **Avoidant** | Emotionally distant, uncomfortable with distress | Doesn't seek comfort, seems independent early |
| **Ambivalent / resistant** | Inconsistent — sometimes responsive, sometimes not | Clingy and anxious, hard to settle |
| **Disorganized** | Frightening or frightened caregiving; unresolved loss or trauma | Contradictory behaviour — approaches and freezes |

### About the percentages you'll see quoted

You will find confident figures everywhere — often "65% secure, 20% avoidant, 10% resistant, 5% disorganized." **Treat all of these with suspicion, including these ones.**

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled **97 samples covering 8,186 children** and found, for child–mother attachment: **53.5% secure, 14.0% avoidant, 11.0% ambivalent, 21.5% disorganized or controlling** ([Deneault et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36897065/)).

That's a long way from the commonly quoted figures — particularly for disorganized attachment, which comes out around four times higher.

**Why the numbers move around so much:**

- Which population was studied. Security is lower and disorganization higher in at-risk families, especially where there's been maltreatment.
- Which version of the procedure was used, and who coded it.
- Which culture. 89% of the children in that meta-analysis were North American or European. Distributions differ elsewhere, and the whole method was developed in one cultural context.

**What to take from this:** the categories are real and useful. The percentages are not stable enough to quote at anyone.

### Two things people get wrong about attachment

**It describes a relationship, not a child.** A child can be securely attached to one parent and not another. It isn't a personality trait and it isn't stamped on them.

**It isn't fixed.** Classifications change over time, in both directions, as circumstances and caregiving change.

Attachment history in the long-running Minnesota study was related to later self-reliance, emotional regulation, and social competence — but as one strand among many, not a destiny ([Sroufe, 2005 — a 30-year longitudinal study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16332580/)).

## The Milestone Map

Ranges, not deadlines. Every band covers thinking, feeling, moving, and talking.

### 0–3 months — waking up to the world

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Prefers faces to objects. Follows a slow-moving face with the eyes. Likes strong patterns and contrasts. Starts to recognise the shape of the day |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Social smile, often 6–8 weeks. Calms when held. Brings hand to mouth to self-soothe. Knows familiar people |
| **Moving** | Lifts head briefly on tummy. Grasps reflexively. Brings hands to the middle |
| **Talking** | Coos and gurgles. Goes quiet at a familiar voice |

### 3–6 months — becoming sociable

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Reaches for things. Follows objects across the whole field of view. Explores with the mouth. Starts to grasp cause and effect — shake it, it rattles |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Laughs. Tells familiar from unfamiliar people. Shows clear joy, upset, and frustration. Enjoys back-and-forth games |
| **Moving** | Rolls over. Sits with support. Grabs and passes things hand to hand |
| **Talking** | Babbles with consonants — "ba", "ma". Turns toward sounds |

### 6–9 months — the explorer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Looks for a dropped or half-hidden object. Understands "no". Copies simple actions. Bangs, drops, and tests things on purpose |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Wary of strangers. Separation upset begins. **Checks your face** before deciding if something is safe |
| **Moving** | Sits alone. Gets mobile — crawling, bum-shuffling, rolling, or straight to pulling up. All normal |
| **Talking** | More varied babble. May say "mama"/"dada" without meaning them yet. Understands some words |

### 9–12 months — communicating on purpose

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Finds fully hidden things. Uses one object to get another. Puts things in and out of containers. **Points to show you things** |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Clearly seeks one particular person when upset. Separation upset often peaks. Delights in shared attention |
| **Moving** | Pulls to stand. Cruises along furniture. May take first steps. Neat finger-and-thumb pincer grip |
| **Talking** | First real words. Understands far more than they say. Follows a simple instruction with context |

> **The single most important item on this list is pointing.** Pointing to *show* you something — not to ask for it — means your child has understood that you have a mind that can be directed. It's one of the strongest early predictors of language. If it hasn't appeared by 12 months, mention it.

### 12–18 months — walking and words

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Solves small problems by trial and error. **Pretends** — feeds a doll, holds a block to their ear like a phone. Knows themselves in a mirror |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Uses you as home base — goes off, comes back. Shows pride, jealousy, frustration. First flickers of concern when someone else is upset |
| **Moving** | Walks. Starts climbing. Stacks 2–3 blocks. Scribbles |
| **Talking** | Vocabulary starts building. Points to named objects. Roughly 10–50 words, with huge variation |

### 18–24 months — big feelings

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Sorts by shape or colour. Longer pretend sequences. Simple puzzles. Follows two-step instructions |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | "Mine." Tantrums arrive — the wanting is fully online, the braking system isn't. Embarrassment and pride appear. Plays alongside other children rather than with them |
| **Moving** | Runs. Kicks a ball. Stairs with help. Uses a spoon. Towers of 4–6 blocks |
| **Talking** | Roughly 50–200+ words. Two words together — "more milk", "daddy go" |

### 2–3 years — becoming a person

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Counts a few things. Understands big/small, in/out. Sorts into groups. Memory improves a lot |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | "I", "me", "mine". Names some feelings. Starts to notice when others are upset. Can sometimes be settled with words alone |
| **Moving** | Pedals a trike. Draws a circle. Dresses partly. Better balance |
| **Talking** | Hundreds to over a thousand words. 3–4 word sentences. Endless "why". Understood by familiar adults about 75% of the time |

### 3–4 years — imagination and other people

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Today/tomorrow. Counts to ten. Names colours. Draws a person with a few parts. Tells a story |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Cooperative play begins. Takes turns, with reminders. **Starts to grasp that other people know different things.** Negotiates. Imaginary friends are common and healthy |
| **Moving** | Hops on one foot. Catches a bounced ball. Big buttons |
| **Talking** | 1,000–2,000 words. Complex sentences. Rhymes and word play. Understood by strangers most of the time |

### 4–5 years — getting ready for school

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Counts past 20. Knows some letters. Understands order and sequence. Adds and subtracts with real objects. Concentrates 15+ minutes |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Genuinely understands others can believe different things. Manages some feelings alone. Conscience developing. More stable friendships |
| **Moving** | Skips. Writes some letters. Draws recognisable pictures. Laces later in the range. Handedness settled |
| **Talking** | 2,000–5,000 words. Grown-up grammar. Gets jokes. Can explain what they think and feel |

### 5–7 years — school and friends

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Reads simple books. Basic arithmetic. Follows rules. Understands that an amount stays the same when you change its shape |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Real friendships. Strong sense of fairness. Compares self to others. Recovers from disappointment, with support |
| **Moving** | Rides a bike. Ties laces. Writes legibly. Loses first teeth |
| **Talking** | 5,000–10,000 words. Understands humour. Shifts from learning to read to reading to learn |

### 7–12 years — competence

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Real logical reasoning about concrete things. Plans ahead. Starts thinking about their own thinking |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Stable friendship groups. Intense concern with fairness and belonging. Can hide feelings — which is not the same as managing them. Wants to feel capable |
| **Moving** | Refined coordination. Sport-specific skill. Puberty may start (girls from ~8–13, boys from ~9–14) |
| **Talking** | Vocabulary grows mostly through reading. Understands sarcasm and figures of speech. Can build an argument |

### 12–18 years — who am I

| | |
|---|---|
| **Thinking (IQ)** | Abstract and hypothetical thinking. Systematic problem-solving. Questions everything, including you. Judgement systems still maturing into the mid-20s |
| **Feeling (EQ)** | Identity work. Intense need to belong. Deep friendships, first romances. Emotions run high. Privacy matters enormously |
| **Moving** | Puberty completes. Adult height. Body clock naturally shifts later — which is a biological fact, not laziness |
| **Talking** | Adult vocabulary and grammar. Talks differently to friends than to teachers than to you. All three are real |

## How to Actually Track This

### A worked example

> Sam is 22 months. His mum notices he has about 15 words, and a chart says "50 words by 24 months." She starts counting words every day and feeling worse.
>
> **A better approach — look at four things instead of one:**
>
> 1. **Is he understanding?** Does he follow "get your shoes", find things you name, respond to his name? *Understanding runs ahead of speaking, and it's the more important signal.*
> 2. **Is he communicating at all?** Pointing, showing, taking your hand, gestures, sounds with meaning? *A child with no words but rich gesture is in a very different position from one with neither.*
> 3. **Is it moving?** Compare to three months ago, not to a chart. Five words then, fifteen now, is a trajectory.
> 4. **Is anything else off?** Hearing, eye contact, pretend play, social interest?
>
> **If understanding is good, gesture is rich, and it's climbing** — keep talking, keep reading, and mention it at the next check-up.
>
> **If he doesn't point or show, doesn't respond to his name, has lost words he used to have, or you've been worried for a while** — ask for a hearing test and a referral now. Don't wait for 24 months. Don't wait for anything.

That's the skill: **understanding, communicating, trajectory, everything else.** Not one number.

### Do

- Jot down what's new every month or so. Any notebook works
- Bring your notes to check-ups — organised observation is genuinely useful to a clinician
- Use milestones to decide what to offer next
- Notice and enjoy things as they arrive

### Don't

- Compare with a specific other child
- Panic at one item on one day
- Push a skill your child isn't ready for
- Treat the chart as the whole picture of who your child is

## When to Call

**Call your doctor about any of these:**

| Age | Concern |
|-----|---------|
| 3 months | No social smile |
| 12 months | Doesn't respond to their name |
| 12 months | Doesn't point, show, or gesture to communicate |
| 12 months | No babbling with varied consonants |
| 16 months | No words |
| 30 months | No two-word phrases |
| **Any age** | **Loses a skill they used to have** |
| **Any age** | **You've been worried for a while** |

> **On the last two.** Regression always warrants a call — no waiting, no matter how mild. And "I keep worrying about this" is a legitimate reason on its own. Parents notice things before tests do.

> **A note on the 30-month line.** Many charts still say two-word phrases by 24 months. The CDC deliberately moved it to 30, because at 24 months it was flagging large numbers of children who turned out to be fine. A 24-month-old with single words and strong understanding is very often typical. A 30-month-old still not combining words is worth checking.

## Mostly Follow, Sometimes Stretch

A working idea for how to spend time with your child — deliberately not a number, because no research supports a specific ratio, whatever you may have read:

- **Mostly follow them.** Go where their interest already is. Join what they're doing rather than redirecting them to what you'd prefer.
- **Sometimes stretch them.** Offer something a bit harder than they'd pick, and help just enough.

This is Vygotsky's zone of proximal development in practice. Below the edge, nothing new is learned. Above it, they give up. Just past it, with you nearby, is where growth happens.

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Start a one-page milestone diary.**

Any notebook. Write today's date and your child's age. Then write **three things they can do now that they couldn't do a month ago.**

That's it. Do it monthly.

Two reasons this works better than a chart:

1. **You'll see progress you'd otherwise miss.** Development is invisible day to day and obvious across months. Most parents badly underestimate how much has changed.
2. **If you ever do need a doctor**, you'll arrive with dated observations instead of "I think maybe he's a bit behind?" That difference genuinely changes the appointment.
