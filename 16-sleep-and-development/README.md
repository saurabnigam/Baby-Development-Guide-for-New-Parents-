# Module 16: Sleep Science & Development

> Time: 2 hours | Difficulty: Beginner | Focus: sleep, memory, mood, routines, common disruptions

---

## Key Takeaways

- Sleep is not downtime; it is active brain work for memory, regulation, growth, and learning.
- Children who are under-slept often look dysregulated, inattentive, or “difficult.”
- Early sleep depends heavily on co-regulation and routine, not strict independence.
- Sleep needs change by age, but consistency and emotional safety matter at every stage.
- Fixing sleep often improves cognition, mood, and parent-child relationships at the same time.

---

## Why Sleep Matters for IQ and EQ

Sleep supports:
- memory consolidation
- attention and learning
- emotional regulation
- growth hormone release
- immune health
- stress recovery

Poor sleep affects both parent and child. A tired parent is less regulated and responsive. A tired child is more likely to melt down, resist, and struggle with focus.

---

## Typical Sleep Needs by Age

These are broad ranges, not mandates.

| Age | Typical Total Sleep in 24 Hours |
|-----|---------------------------------|
| 0 to 3 months | about 14 to 17 hours |
| 4 to 12 months | about 12 to 16 hours including naps |
| 1 to 2 years | about 11 to 14 hours including naps |
| 3 to 5 years | about 10 to 13 hours including naps |
| 6 to 12 years | about 9 to 12 hours |
| 13 to 18 years | about 8 to 10 hours |

Sleep need varies. Behavior and daytime function matter as much as totals.

---

## Sleep and the Developing Brain

### Infancy

Newborn sleep is fragmented and biologically normal. Circadian rhythm is immature early on. Night waking is not a sign of bad habits in the newborn stage.

### Toddlerhood

As memory and autonomy grow, bedtime resistance, separation concerns, and night waking can increase.

### Preschool and School Age

Sleep supports executive function, attention, and emotional stability. Poor sleep can look like hyperactivity, irritability, or school difficulty.

### Adolescence

Circadian rhythm naturally shifts later, but school schedules often do not. Chronic sleep debt is common and harmful.

## Evidence-Based Sleep Supports

### 1. Predictable Routine

Use the same sequence most nights.

Examples:
- bath
- pajamas
- teeth
- story
- cuddle
- lights down

Why it works:
- reduces uncertainty
- lowers arousal
- creates strong sleep cues

### 2. Consistent Wake Time

Often more powerful than a rigid bedtime.

Why it works:
- anchors circadian rhythm
- improves predictability across the day

### 3. Daytime Light and Movement

Why it works:
- strengthens body clock
- increases sleep pressure naturally
- supports mood regulation

### 4. Emotional Safety at Bedtime

Why it works:
- children settle better when they feel secure
- harsh bedtime battles can increase arousal and resistance

### 5. Screen Control Before Bed

Why it works:
- content and light can increase arousal and delay sleep
- bedtime should cue down-regulation, not stimulation

## Common Sleep Challenges

### Newborn Day-Night Confusion

What helps:
- light exposure in daytime
- calm, dark nights
- avoid treating the newborn like a machine on a fixed schedule too early

### Infant Night Waking

Often normal, especially with growth, teething, illness, or developmental leaps.

What helps:
- consistent response pattern
- age-appropriate expectations
- watch overtiredness

### Toddler Bedtime Battles

What helps:
- predictable routine
- clear limit with warm follow-through
- enough daytime movement
- no long negotiations after lights down

### Preschool Nightmares or Fears

What helps:
- validate feelings
- keep routines calm and boring
- avoid scary media
- offer one or two predictable comfort measures

### Early Waking

Check:
- bedtime too late
- light entering room
- hunger
- naps out of balance
- habit patterns

---

## Parent Behaviors That Matter Most

- Stay calm and boring at bedtime
- Avoid turning bedtime into a stage for power struggles
- Notice whether behavior problems track with sleep debt
- Support independent skills gradually, not punitively
- Ask for help if your own sleep deprivation is affecting mental health

---

## Red Flags: Talk to a Pediatrician If

- loud chronic snoring
- breathing pauses or labored breathing during sleep
- extreme daytime sleepiness
- persistent insomnia with major daytime impairment
- sleep disruption tied to pain, reflux, or medical concern
- severe behavioral change with poor sleep

---

## Research Notes

- Sleep supports memory consolidation and learning across childhood.
- Chronic short sleep is associated with worse attention, mood, and behavior.
- Bedtime routines are associated with improved sleep outcomes and family functioning.
- Adolescent sleep deprivation is linked to depression, anxiety, and worse academic outcomes.

---

## 5-Minute Action Plan

Choose one part of the routine to make consistent every night this week. Start small. A reliable last 10 minutes before sleep is more useful than a perfect 60-minute routine you cannot maintain.
