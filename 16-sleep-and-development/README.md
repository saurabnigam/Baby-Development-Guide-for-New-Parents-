# Module 16: Sleep

> **Time**: 2 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: keeping babies safe asleep, how much sleep children need, and what actually fixes bad nights
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

## Key Takeaways

- **If your baby is under one, safe sleep comes before everything else in this module.** It's first for that reason.
- Sleep isn't downtime. It's when memories get consolidated and emotional regulation gets restored.
- **An under-slept child doesn't look sleepy.** They look inattentive, impulsive, and difficult.
- A consistent bedtime routine works, and it works **fast** — most of the improvement shows up in the first three nights.
- The long-term worry about sleep training has been studied. At five-year follow-up, researchers found **no difference** between trained and untrained children on any outcome they measured.

# Safe Sleep for Infants — First, and Non-Negotiable

Everything else here is about sleep *quality*. This is about sleep *safety*.

It applies to every sleep in the first year. Naps, nights, at home, at grandparents', in a hotel — and by every person who ever puts your baby down.

## The ABCs

- **A — Alone.** Nothing else in the sleep space. No pillows, blankets, bumpers, positioners, wedges, or soft toys
- **B — Back.** On the back, every sleep, until they can roll both ways unaided. Once they roll reliably both ways you don't need to flip them back all night — but you always *place* them on their back
- **C — Cot.** A firm, **flat, non-inclined** surface with a fitted sheet and nothing else

## The two numbers worth knowing

| Situation | Risk |
|-----------|------|
| Sleeping on a **sofa or armchair** with a baby | Up to **67 times** higher risk of sleep-related death |
| **Bed-sharing** with a baby under 4 months | Roughly **5–10 times** higher risk |

*(AAP 2022 — [parent guide](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx) · [recommendations](https://www.alpqc.org/files/2025/09/AAP-2022-Safe-Sleep-Recommendations.pdf))*

**The sofa figure is the one to remember.** Dozing off on the sofa during a 3am feed feels like the careful choice compared with the bed. It is the most dangerous thing on this page.

## The rest

| Rule | Why |
|------|-----|
| **Room-share, don't bed-share** — baby on their own surface in your room, at least the first 6 months | Room-sharing lowers risk. AAP doesn't recommend bed-sharing under any circumstances |
| **Never on a sofa or armchair** | See above |
| **Don't overheat** | One more layer than you'd want. No hats indoors, head uncovered |
| **Stop swaddling at the first sign of rolling** | A swaddled baby who rolls face-down can't get back |
| **Move them out of car seats and swings** | Not safe sleep surfaces; inclined seats carry a positional risk |
| **Dummy at sleep**, once feeding is established | Associated with reduced risk. Optional. Don't re-insert once asleep |
| **Smoke-free**, in pregnancy and after | One of the largest things you can change |
| **Breastfeeding**, if it works for you; **vaccinations up to date** | Both associated with reduced risk |

## Products to avoid, whatever the box says

**Weighted blankets, weighted sleep sacks, weighted swaddles** — AAP says don't use them. **Inclined sleepers, sleep positioners, cot bumpers** (including "breathable"). **Hammock-style sleepers.** Several categories have been recalled after infant deaths.

**The test:** if a product adds something to the sleep surface, or changes your baby's position, the answer is no.

**Home breathing or oxygen monitors** are not medical devices, aren't validated to prevent SIDS, and produce both false alarms and false reassurance.

## If your baby has reflux or was premature

Ask your doctor before changing anything. The answer is almost always still back-sleeping on a flat surface. **Reflux is not a reason for inclined or side sleeping.**

## "We ended up bed-sharing and I feel terrible"

Almost every exhausted parent has, at some point, fallen asleep somewhere they didn't plan to. Shame isn't useful here; information is.

The safest arrangement is a separate flat surface in your room.

But if bed-sharing is going to happen — planned or not — **prepare the bed in advance rather than deciding at 4am on a sofa.** Firm mattress. No duvet or pillows near the baby. No gap between mattress and wall. No other children or pets. Baby on their back.

Risk climbs sharply if anyone in the bed smokes, has drunk alcohol, or has taken anything sedating. Same if they are extremely sleep-deprived, or if your baby is under 4 months, was premature, or was small at birth.

Talk to your midwife, health visitor, or doctor about your actual situation rather than working it out alone at night.

# How Much Sleep, and Why

## What sleep is doing

- **Consolidating memory** — the day's learning gets moved into longer-term storage
- **Restoring emotional regulation** — this is why tired children fall apart
- **Growth hormone release**
- **Immune function**
- **Clearing the effects of the day's stress**

## How much

Recommended totals per 24 hours, from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and endorsed by the AAP:

| Age | Hours per 24 |
|-----|-------------|
| 4–12 months | 12–16 (including naps) |
| 1–2 years | 11–14 (including naps) |
| 3–5 years | 10–13 (including naps) |
| 6–12 years | 9–12 |
| 13–18 years | 8–10 |

*([AASM consensus](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27250809) · [AAP summary](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sleep/Pages/healthy-sleep-habits-how-many-hours-does-your-child-need.aspx))*

**Newborns aren't on this list** because their sleep is fragmented and irregular by design, and that's normal.

## The thing most parents don't know

**A short-sleeping child does not look tired.**

They look inattentive, impulsive, irritable, emotionally fragile, and "hyperactive". Short sleep in a school-age child is routinely mistaken for a behaviour problem or an attention problem.

**So: before you conclude anything about your child's behaviour, count backwards from their wake time.** If they need 10–11 hours and they're up at 7, they need to be asleep by 8 or 8:30 — asleep, not starting the bath. Most families find their real bedtime is an hour later than they thought.

## The Bedtime Routine — and How Fast It Works

A consistent bedtime routine is the single best-supported thing you can do about sleep, at any age.

**And it works quickly.** Researchers randomly assigned 134 mothers and their infants, aged 8–18 months, to start a consistent bedtime routine — bath, massage, quiet activities. Then they tracked what happened night by night.

**Most of the improvement appeared in the first three nights**, across:

- how long it took to fall asleep
- how often they woke in the night
- how long those wakings lasted
- how consolidated the sleep was
- how easy bedtime felt to the mother
- the mother's rating of her baby's mood

Sleep-onset time didn't improve much further after those first three nights; the other measures kept improving slowly over the fortnight ([Mindell et al., 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28985580)).

**Why this matters if you're exhausted:** you don't have to commit to a month of misery to find out whether it helps. **Start tonight and you'll usually know by Thursday.**

A wider review links bedtime routines to more than sleep — to broader development and wellbeing too. That makes sense: a routine bundles the things that help anyway. Contact, language, predictability, and calm ([Mindell & Williamson, 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29195725)).

### Building one

Four to six steps, same order, most nights, 20–45 minutes:

> dim the lights → bath → pyjamas → teeth → two books → song → into bed → "night night, I'm nearby"

**What makes it work:**

- **Same order every time.** The sequence is the signal, not the length
- **Same ending.** The last thing before sleep should be the same last thing
- **Get shorter, not louder, when it's going badly.** A rushed 10-minute version beats an abandoned 45-minute one
- **Dim the lights early.** Light is a genuine input to the body clock
- **Screens off well before**, and out of the bedroom for older children

### The two other levers

**A consistent wake time** anchors everything, and matters more than a rigid bedtime. It sets the body clock for the whole day.

**Daylight and movement in the day.** Both strengthen the body clock and build up sleep pressure so that being tired at bedtime is genuine rather than negotiated.

## Sleep Training: An Honest Section

This is the most emotive question in early parenting, and the guide owes you a straight answer rather than a dodge.

### What it is

A group of behavioural approaches that reduce how much help a baby needs to fall asleep. They're used from around 6 months, and it's worth checking with your doctor first.

They range from gradual, parent-present methods through to leaving longer gaps before responding.

### What's actually known

**Short term, they work.** Randomised trials show behavioural approaches improve infant sleep and maternal mood.

**The long-term worry has been tested.** The most common fear is that these methods damage attachment, stress regulation, or mental health. Researchers followed up a randomised trial when the children were **6 years old**. That's 326 children, roughly half of whom had the intervention as babies.

They measured child mental health, sleep, psychosocial functioning, stress regulation via cortisol, the child–parent relationship, maternal mental health, and parenting style.

**They found no evidence of differences between the groups on any outcome.** Not better, not worse ([Price, Wake, Ukoumunne & Hiscock, 2012, *Pediatrics*](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22966034)).

### What that means for you

**It means this is genuinely your choice**, and neither answer is the wrong one.

- If sleep deprivation is damaging your health, your mental health, or your ability to parent the way you want to — **behavioural sleep support is a legitimate option. The long-term fears are not supported.**
- If you don't want to, you don't have to. Night waking in the first year is normal, most children sleep through eventually regardless, and there is no developmental prize for early independent sleep.
- **Don't do it because someone made you feel judged**, in either direction. This debate generates a lot of heat and very little of it is about your family.

**Whatever you choose, safe sleep rules don't change.**

## Sleep by Stage

**Newborns.** Fragmented, irregular, no body clock yet. Night waking is not a habit you've created. Day-night confusion is normal — bright and sociable by day, dim and boring at night.

**Infants.** Night waking is common and can increase with teething, illness, and developmental leaps. Watch for overtiredness, which paradoxically makes settling harder.

**Toddlers.** Memory and autonomy arrive together, so you get bedtime resistance, separation protest, and the fourth request for water. Predictable routine, one clear limit, warm follow-through, and no long negotiations after lights out.

**Preschool.** Imagination-based fears turn up. Take the feeling seriously without building elaborate anti-monster machinery. Naps drop somewhere between 3 and 5 — messily.

**School age.** Sleep now competes with homework, clubs, and screens. This is where quiet chronic sleep debt starts, and where it gets mistaken for behaviour.

**Adolescence.** The body clock genuinely shifts later at puberty. They're not tired at 10pm because their biology says it isn't night. School start times don't move, so chronic debt is common. Phones out of bedrooms is the highest-value rule you have.

## Common Problems

### Night waking

Normal, especially in the first year. Check the obvious first: hungry, teething, ill, too hot or cold, or overtired.

**Be consistent in how you respond**, whatever you choose. Inconsistency is harder for a child than any particular approach.

### Bedtime resistance

Predictable routine · enough daytime movement · a clear limit warmly held · and be boring. **Handle the fourth request for water without engagement** — attention is the reward being sought, and even irritated attention works.

Pre-empt: put the water there, do the last wee, say goodnight to the toys, before lights out.

### Early waking

Check: bedtime too late (overtiredness causes early waking), light coming into the room, hunger, naps out of balance, or noise at a predictable hour.

### Nightmares and night terrors — different things

| | Nightmare | Night terror |
|---|---------|-------------|
| When | Second half of the night | First few hours |
| Are they awake? | Yes, and frightened | **No** — eyes may be open but they're asleep |
| Do they remember? | Yes | No |
| What to do | Comfort, reassure, stay | **Don't wake them.** Keep them safe and wait. It passes |

Night terrors are alarming to watch and harmless. Waking them makes it worse.

## Red Flags: Talk to a Doctor If

- **Loud habitual snoring**, pauses in breathing, gasping, or laboured breathing during sleep — this can be obstructive sleep apnoea, it's treatable, and it's frequently missed
- Persistent daytime sleepiness in a child who appears to be getting enough hours
- Insomnia with real daytime impairment
- Sleep disrupted by pain, reflux, itching, or breathing
- A marked change in sleep alongside changes in mood, appetite, or functioning
- Excessive restlessness or leg discomfort at night

**Snoring is the one most often shrugged off.** A child who snores loudly most nights deserves a conversation with a doctor.

---

## Your Own Sleep Counts

Parental sleep deprivation is not a side issue. It directly affects your patience, your mood, and your ability to be the calm adult everything else in this guide depends on.

- Split nights where you can, so at least one adult gets an unbroken block
- Lower your standards on everything else while sleep is bad
- Ask for practical help rather than sympathy
- **If your own sleep deprivation is affecting your mental health, that's a medical conversation** — with your doctor, not something to endure

---

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Pick the last ten minutes and make them identical, starting tonight.**

Not the whole routine. Just the final ten minutes before sleep — same order, same words, same ending, every night.

Then watch what happens over the next three nights. Based on the research, that's when most of the change shows up. If nothing has shifted by the weekend, you've lost very little and you know to look elsewhere.
