# Module 23: Life Skills & Critical Moments

> Time: 5 hours | Difficulty: Beginner to Intermediate | Focus: how to actually teach a skill — and the specific skills that matter most, with age gates and safety limits
>
> *This is the "how do I teach my kid to do X" module: swimming, crossing roads, using a knife, handling money, keeping their body safe, calling for help.*

---

## Safety note — read this first

Several skills in this module sit on top of genuine physical risk. For those, **teaching the skill is never the primary safety measure.** A child who can swim can still drown. A child who knows road rules can still be hit. The skill is one layer among several, and it is not the strongest one.

Every high-risk section below is written as **layers of protection**, with the skill in its correct place in the stack. Please don't lift the teaching progression out of a section and leave the layers behind.

This module is general parent education, not certification. For water, road, fire, and first-aid skills, in-person instruction from a qualified instructor beats anything written down — including this.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Every skill is taught with the same five-step loop. Learn the loop once and you can teach anything.
- **Readiness beats age.** The age columns here are typical windows, not schedules — and for safety skills, being early is worse than being late.
- For anything dangerous, the child's skill is the *last* layer of protection, never the first.
- The hardest part of teaching is not explaining. It is **shutting up and letting them be slow and bad at it.**
- Teach in calm moments, always. A skill introduced during the emergency is not a skill; it's a lecture.

# Part 1: The Teaching Loop

The same five steps work for holding a spoon, tying a lace, crossing a road, or managing a bank account. The only thing that changes is the size of the step.

```
   1. MODEL          → they watch you do it, narrated
   2. BREAK DOWN     → split into steps small enough to succeed at
   3. DO IT TOGETHER → hand-over-hand, then side-by-side
   4. THEY DO, YOU WATCH → you are present and silent
   5. FADE           → you leave, in stages
```

### 1. Model — do it out loud

Show the whole skill first, narrating your thinking, not just your actions.

> "I'm looking left first, because that's where the cars come from on our side. Now right. Now left again — because a car could have come while I was looking right."

The narration is what makes it teachable. Silent demonstration teaches the motion; narrated demonstration teaches the *decision*.

### 2. Break it down — small enough to win

Most teaching fails here. Adults hold the whole skill in their head and forget it's twelve steps.

**"Wash your hands" is not one skill.** It is: turn on tap → wet hands → get soap → rub palms → rub backs → between fingers → thumbs → count to twenty → rinse → turn off tap → dry. A 3-year-old will fail at "wash your hands" and succeed at "you do the soap part, I'll do the rest."

**Two ways to sequence it:**

- **Forward chaining** — they do step 1, you do the rest. Good for most skills.
- **Backward chaining** — you do everything and they do the *last* step. Better for anything long or frustrating, because they always end on success. Use it for shoelaces, getting dressed, and making the bed.

### 3. Do it together — then get your hands off

Hand-over-hand, then a light touch, then just your hand hovering, then nothing. Move down this ladder faster than feels comfortable.

### 4. They do, you watch — and say nothing

This is the step adults ruin. You will want to correct, hurry, and fix. Don't.

**Sit on your hands.** Let it be slow, backwards, and clumsy. A child who is corrected mid-attempt learns that the skill belongs to you.

If you must speak, ask instead of tell: *"What comes next?"* beats *"Now the soap."*

### 5. Fade — in stages, out loud

Say it explicitly so the handover is visible:

> "This week I'm going to watch you do it. Next week I'll be in the kitchen while you do it. After that it's just yours."

Naming the fade turns it into a promotion. Silently withdrawing feels like abandonment.

## The three rules that make it stick

**1. Teach when calm, never during.** Never teach road-crossing while late for school. Never teach knife safety while cooking dinner for guests. Practise on Sunday so it works on Tuesday.

**2. Let them be bad at it.** The first twenty attempts will be worse than you doing it. That is what the twenty attempts are for. Build extra time into the day rather than taking the task back.

**3. Praise the process, name the step.**
> Not: "Good job!"
> But: "You looked both ways *twice* before you stepped out. That's the part that matters."

# Part 3: Water & Swimming

**This is the highest-stakes section in the guide.** Drowning is the leading cause of death for children aged 1–4 in the United States and among the leading causes of injury death for children worldwide. It is fast — a child can drown in under a minute — and, crucially, **it is silent.** There is no splashing and no shouting. A drowning child is vertical, quiet, and looks like they are treading water. Most parents nearby do not notice.

## Layers of protection — in order of strength

Swim lessons are layer 4. Do not treat them as layer 1.

| # | Layer | Why it matters |
|---|-------|----------------|
| **1** | **Barriers** | Four-sided isolation fencing around a pool, self-latching and self-closing, at least 4ft/1.2m, that separates the pool from the *house* — not just from the street. Studies find this substantially reduces drowning risk compared with three-sided fencing. Door alarms and pool covers add to it |
| **2** | **Touch supervision** | For any child under 5, or any weak swimmer: an adult in the water, within arm's reach. Not "watching from a lounger." Not "there are lots of adults here" |
| **3** | **A designated Water Watcher** | One named adult whose only job is watching the water for a set period, no phone, no conversation, then handing over explicitly. Diffusion of responsibility is how drownings happen at busy parties |
| **4** | **Swim lessons / water competence** | Reduces risk. Does not eliminate it |
| **5** | **Life jackets** | Properly fitted, certified. **On boats, always, for everyone** |
| **6** | **Adult CPR training** | Because layers 1–5 sometimes all fail |

### The things that are not safety devices

**Armbands, water wings, inflatable rings, floaty seats, and pool noodles are toys, not safety equipment.** They deflate, slip off, and — worse — they hold a child upright in a position that makes it harder to learn to swim, and they teach both child and parent a false confidence. If flotation is needed, use a properly fitted, certified life jacket.

## Where children actually drown, by age

| Age | Most common site | The specific hazard |
|-----|-----------------|--------------------|
| Under 1 | **Bathtub** | Left alone "for a second." Bath seats are not restraints |
| 1–4 | **Home swimming pools** | Unsupervised access — often the child was last seen inside the house |
| 1–4 | Buckets, toilets, ponds, pet bowls | A toddler can drown in a few centimetres. Their head is heavy and they can't right themselves |
| 5–9 | Pools, open water | Overestimating ability |
| 10–17 | **Open water** — rivers, lakes, sea | Currents, cold shock, depth misjudgement, peers, and (older) alcohol |

**Two rules that cover most of this:**
- Never leave a child under 5 alone near any water, including a bathtub, for any length of time. If the doorbell rings, take the child with you.
- Empty buckets, paddling pools, and baths immediately after use. Keep toilet lids down; consider a lid latch during the toddler years.

## What "water competence" means

Swimming is not one skill. The internationally used definition breaks it into five, and a child who can do all five in a pool is meaningfully safer. They are still not "drown-proof" — **that concept does not exist.**

1. **Enter water over their head and return to the surface**
2. **Float or tread water for one minute**
3. **Turn around in a full circle and find an exit**
4. **Swim 25 metres**
5. **Exit the water** — including climbing out without a ladder

Ask any swim programme how they teach these five, especially #1, #3 and #5. Programmes that only teach forward strokes in shallow water are teaching sport, not safety.

## Teaching progression by age

### 0–12 months: comfort, not lessons

There is **no evidence that formal swimming instruction reduces drowning risk under age 1**, and infant programmes that claim to "drown-proof" babies should be avoided outright.

What you actually do at this age is build comfort:
- Bath time: pour water gently over the head, from the forehead back. Say "ready — water!" first, every time, so it becomes predictable rather than startling.
- Hold them in the water chest-to-chest, warm pool, short sessions (10–20 minutes; babies chill fast).
- **Never submerge an infant deliberately.** Beyond distress, infants can swallow enough pool water to cause hyponatraemia.
- Keep sessions positive and short. A baby who cries through swim class is learning that water is frightening.

### 1–3 years: parent-and-child classes

AAP guidance is that swim lessons **may be considered from age 1**, based on evidence that lessons in the 1–4 range are associated with reduced drowning risk. Choose based on your child's readiness, your pool access, and their comfort.

Skills at this stage:
- Blowing bubbles (this is the foundation of everything — breath control comes before strokes)
- Kicking while held
- **Back float with support** — the single most important survival position
- Holding the wall and moving along it
- Climbing out — teach the actual exit, at the actual pool edge, many times
- "Wait for me" at the edge: **never enter the water without an adult.** Practise this constantly. It is a rule, not a skill

### 4–6 years: real lessons

Most children are developmentally ready for formal instruction here. Look for:
- Small groups, warm water, in-water instructor
- Explicit teaching of the five water-competence skills
- Clothed-swim practice (falling in wearing clothes feels completely different)
- A programme that never promises drown-proofing

Home reinforcement:
- Back float, unassisted, working up to 60 seconds
- Roll from front to back — this is what saves a child who falls in face-down
- Jump in, turn around, swim back to the wall
- Practise the **"if you fall in" sequence**: don't panic → roll onto your back → float → shout → kick towards the edge

### 7–12 years: competence and judgement

The skill is mostly there. The risk shifts to **judgement**, and this is where boys in particular start to be over-represented in drowning statistics.

Teach explicitly:
- Never swim alone, ever, at any level of skill
- Open water is a different sport: currents, cold shock (which causes involuntary gasping within seconds), no clear bottom, changing depth
- What a rip current is and what to do — **swim parallel to shore, not against it**
- Never dive into unknown water; head-first entry into shallow water causes spinal injury
- Reach or throw, don't go — how to help someone else without becoming a second casualty
- Life jacket on boats, non-negotiable, including for strong swimmers

### 13–18 years

The risks are now peers, showing off, unfamiliar water, and alcohol. Talk about them directly, without moralising, because this is exactly the age when your influence is indirect:

> "The thing that kills strong swimmers isn't ability. It's cold water, currents, and being somewhere nobody knows the water. And alcohol — which makes people overestimate what they can do and slows the reaction if it goes wrong."

## If your child is frightened of water

Never force it. Fear learned by force is durable and generalises.

Go backwards until they're comfortable and rebuild:
bath → paddling pool → sitting on the pool step → holding you in shallow water → walking in.

Let them control the pace and the entry. Give them a rule they own — *"I'll never put your face under without telling you first"* — and then never break it. Trust is the whole mechanism here.

## Also learn CPR

If you have a pool, or spend time around water, take an infant/child CPR and rescue-breathing class. Bystander CPR meaningfully changes drowning outcomes, and the window is minutes. This is genuinely one of the highest-value hours a parent can spend.

## After any submersion incident

If a child was submerged and had any trouble breathing, coughed persistently, or was unusually sleepy afterwards — get medical assessment. So-called "dry" or "secondary drowning" is heavily overstated online, but respiratory distress after a submersion event is real and worth checking the same day.

# Part 4: Roads & Traffic

## The developmental reality

Children are not small adults on roads. Until around age 9–10, most children genuinely cannot:

- accurately judge the speed of an approaching vehicle
- accurately judge how far away it is
- reliably localise sound direction
- see far into their peripheral vision (a young child's usable field is roughly two-thirds of an adult's)
- resist an impulse when something interesting is on the other side
- attend to traffic and a conversation at the same time

This is not carelessness. It is developmental fact, and it is why children get hit.

**Practical conclusion: hold hands until at least 8, and don't expect independent crossing before 9–10.**

## Teaching progression

| Age | What you teach | How |
|-----|---------------|-----|
| 1–3 | "We hold hands near roads." Non-negotiable, every time | Never negotiate this one. Consistency is the lesson |
| 3–4 | Stop at the kerb, automatically | Practise: they stop, then look at you for permission |
| 4–5 | Look left, right, left. Listen. Narrate what you see | You narrate first, then they narrate for you |
| 5–7 | Find the safe place to cross; what makes a crossing safe | Walk a familiar route and ask them to choose crossing points |
| 7–9 | Judging gaps — with you standing right there | "Is that car far enough? …Let's watch and see how long it took" |
| 9–11 | Crossing alone on a known, quiet route | Walk it 20+ times together first. Then follow behind |
| 11+ | New routes, busier roads, junctions | Discuss route choice before, not during |

## The rules that actually matter

- **Stop at every kerb.** Make it automatic, like a full stop at the end of a sentence.
- **Look, listen, and look again.** The second look catches the car that arrived during the first look.
- **Never cross between parked cars.** They hide the child from the driver and the driver from the child. If you must, edge out to the outer line of the cars and look again from there.
- **Eyes up, ears free.** No phone, no headphones, near roads. Model this yourself.
- **Make eye contact with drivers** before crossing in front of them. A stopped car is not a safe car until the driver has seen you.
- **Never run to a road, ever** — not for a ball, not for a friend, not for a dog. Practise the alternative: stop, tell an adult.
- **Reverse the perspective:** "The driver can't see you if you can't see their face." This single sentence does a lot of work for 5–8 year olds.

## The commentary walk

The best road-safety exercise there is, and it takes no extra time:

For ages 4–7, **you** narrate every decision on the walk to school:
> "I'm stopping here. I'm looking left — there's a car but it's far. Right — clear. Left again — it's closer now, so we'll wait. …Now it's passed, and I can see all the way down. Now we go, walking not running."

From around 6, **swap roles.** They narrate, you follow their instructions exactly, including their mistakes (safely — with your hand ready). Being wrong out loud teaches faster than being corrected.

## Car safety

- Correct child restraint for weight/height, correctly installed, every trip. Rear-facing as long as the seat allows.
- Nobody moves until everyone's buckled — make this a family ritual the children enforce on adults.
- Get out on the pavement side.
- **Never leave a child alone in a car.** Heatstroke deaths happen fast, including on mild days.
- **Check behind you before reversing on a driveway.** Driveway run-overs of toddlers are a real and awful category, and the child is almost always invisible from the driver's seat.

# Part 5: Cycling

**Balance first, pedals second.** A balance bike (no pedals, feet on the ground) teaches the actual hard skill — balance and steering — while the child can stop themselves at any time. Most children who use one skip stabilisers entirely and transition to a pedal bike in an afternoon.

Stabilisers/training wheels teach pedalling, which is easy, while actively preventing the learning of balance, which is hard. They also teach a lean pattern that has to be unlearned.

| Age | Stage |
|-----|-------|
| 2–3 | Balance bike. Feet down, walking it, then gliding |
| 3–4 | Long glides, feet up, steering around obstacles |
| 4–5 | Pedal bike, no stabilisers. Often takes one session |
| 5–7 | Braking (both brakes, front-and-rear), starting and stopping on a slope, looking behind while riding |
| 7–10 | Traffic-free paths. Hand signals. Riding one-handed to signal |
| 10–12 | Formal cycle training if available in your area |
| 12+ | Roads, with training and a route you've ridden together |

**Helmet, every ride, from the first ride.** The habit is set in the first month. Wear one yourself — a parent riding bare-headed makes the rule unteachable.

# Part 6: Kitchen, Knives & Fire

The kitchen is the best skills classroom in the house: sequencing, measurement, patience, following instructions, and a tangible result. It also teaches food ownership better than any nutrition advice — a child who cooked it usually eats it.

## Progression

| Age | Can do | Adult does |
|-----|--------|-----------|
| 18 mo–2 | Wash vegetables, tear leaves, stir cold things, put things in a bowl | Everything else |
| 2–3 | Spread with a blunt knife, pour from a small jug, mash, sprinkle, press cutters | Heat, sharp things |
| 3–4 | Cut soft foods with a child-safe serrated knife (banana, cooked potato, cheese) | Heat, hard foods |
| 4–5 | Crack eggs, measure, peel with a Y-peeler, set the table, use a timer | Heat, sharp knives |
| 6–7 | Use the toaster and microwave. Cut firmer foods with a small sharp knife, supervised | Stovetop, oven |
| 8–10 | Stovetop with supervision. Follow a simple recipe start to finish | Oven, deep heat, alone-time |
| 10–12 | Oven with supervision. Cook a full simple meal | |
| 12+ | Cook independently. Plan and shop for a meal | |

## Knife skills — teach properly, early

Counterintuitive but true: children taught knife technique properly cut themselves less than children who first pick up a knife unsupervised at ten.

Teach in this order, on a banana:

1. **The bridge grip** — fingers arched over the food like a bridge, cut between them.
2. **The claw grip** — fingertips curled under, knuckles guide the blade. Say it as: *"Fingers are the frightened crab. They hide."*
3. **Blade away, always. Nobody walks with a knife pointed out.**
4. **Non-slip board** — damp cloth underneath.
5. **A falling knife has no handle.** You never catch it. You step back and let it land.
6. **Cut on a flat surface** — halve round things first so they can't roll.

## Fire and heat

- From 3: "hot" as a category. Let them feel *warm* things so the word has meaning. Steam burns before anything looks hot.
- From 5: how the stove works, why pan handles turn inwards, why you never put water on an oil fire.
- From 6: what the smoke alarm sounds like and exactly what to do. **Practise a fire drill twice a year** — including the meeting point outside and "never go back in for anything."
- From 7: candles and matches, supervised, treated as a skill rather than a forbidden mystery. Forbidden things get experimented with in secret.
- **Burns:** cool running water for 20 minutes. No ice, no butter, no toothpaste. Teach this to every child over 6.

# Part 7: Body Safety & Consent

This belongs in every parenting guide and is missing from most. It is EQ, IQ, and safety at once — and the evidence is clear that children who have body-safety language disclose sooner and are targeted less often.

Two facts that shape how it's taught:

- **Most child sexual abuse is by someone the child knows** — a family member, family friend, or someone in a position of trust. "Stranger danger" is the wrong model and it leaves children unprotected against the actual risk.
- Perpetrators rely on secrecy and on the child lacking words. Both of those you can remove.

## What to teach, by age

### 2–3 years

- **Correct anatomical names for body parts**, said as neutrally as "elbow." This matters for two reasons: it removes shame, and it means a child's disclosure is understandable to a doctor or a police officer.
- "Your body belongs to you."
- Bodily autonomy in ordinary life: **never force hugs or kisses**, including for grandparents. "You don't have to hug. You can wave, high-five, or blow a kiss." This is where consent is actually taught — not in a conversation at 14.
- You stop tickling *immediately* when they say stop, every single time. This is a live demonstration that "stop" works.

### 3–5 years

- **The private parts rule:** "The parts covered by your swimsuit are private. Nobody looks at them or touches them except to keep you clean or healthy — and even then, you can say what feels okay."
- **Secrets vs surprises:** "A surprise is something happy that we tell later — like a present. A secret is something you're told never to tell. **We don't keep secrets in this family.** If a grown-up asks you to keep a secret from me, come and tell me straight away — you will never be in trouble for it."
- **Safe adults:** name 3–5 specific people they could tell anything to. Write them down. Update the list yearly.
- **Uh-oh feeling:** "Sometimes your tummy feels funny or wrong when something isn't right. That feeling is worth listening to."

### 5–8 years

- "No, go, tell": say no, get away, tell a safe adult — **and keep telling until someone does something.** That last clause is critical, and children need to hear it explicitly.
- **"You will never be in trouble for telling me something."** Say this often. Mean it. The single biggest barrier to disclosure is the child's fear of the consequences.
- Their own rules for their own body — including that they don't have to let a friend hug them either.
- Online: never share pictures of your body, never meet someone from the internet, tell me if anyone asks you to keep an online conversation private.

### 8–12 years, and into adolescence

- Puberty, in advance of it happening, not after
- Consent in friendships and, later, relationships — both directions: yours and other people's
- Pornography exists, it is not what real intimacy looks like, and you can tell me if you see something that bothered you
- Grooming behaviour: adults who single you out, give gifts, want privacy with you, or treat you like an adult
- **Keep the door open.** The goal is not a lecture at 12. It is a child who at 15 still believes you would react well.

## Two things that make it work

**Little and often beats one big talk.** Five sentences in the bath, once a month, for years.

**Your reaction is the whole thing.** If a child tests you with something small and you overreact, panic, or get angry, they have learned not to bring you the big thing. Practise the calm response now: *"Thank you for telling me. You did the right thing. You're not in trouble. Tell me more."*

# Part 8: Money

| Age | Concept | Activity |
|-----|---------|----------|
| 3–4 | Money is exchanged for things | Let them hand over the cash and take the change |
| 4–5 | Coins have different values | Sorting and counting. A clear jar so savings are *visible* |
| 5–7 | You have to choose — you can't have both | £/$ 2 at a shop, their choice, no rescuing the bad choice |
| 5–7 | Saving means waiting | Save for one specific wanted thing. A visual chart |
| 7–9 | Regular allowance, three jars: **spend / save / give** | Give them full control of the spend jar, including to waste it |
| 8–10 | Things cost different amounts; comparing value | "Which is better value — and why do you think so?" |
| 10–12 | Earning beyond allowance; simple budgeting | Extra paid jobs distinct from unpaid family chores |
| 12–14 | Bank account. Tracking. Delayed purchases | A rule: anything over a threshold waits a week |
| 14–16 | Real earning; understanding tax and pay | A first job, or a paid project |
| 16–18 | Full budget for a category they own (clothes, phone, transport) | Give them the money and let them run out |

**The three principles:**

1. **Let them lose money.** A £5 mistake at eight teaches what a £5,000 mistake at twenty-eight would. Do not rescue it, and do not say "I told you so."
2. **Separate contribution from payment.** Family chores are unpaid, because you're a member of this household. Extra jobs can be paid. Blurring these teaches that helping is a transaction.
3. **Talk about your own money decisions out loud** — trade-offs, not amounts. *"We're not doing the holiday this year because we're fixing the roof, and the roof matters more."*

# Part 9: Self-Care, Chores & Contribution

## Self-care

| Skill | Introduce | Independent | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Handwashing | 2 | 4–5 | Sing a 20-second song |
| Toothbrushing | 6 mo | 7–8 | **Adult brushes or checks until ~7–8** — they lack the dexterity. First dental visit by age 1 |
| Bathing | 4 | 8–10 | Never unsupervised under 5 (see Part 3) |
| Dressing | 2 | 4–5 | Lay clothes out in order; let them wear the mismatched outfit |
| Hair | 5 | 8–10 | |
| Blowing nose | 2–3 | 4 | Practise blowing a feather off a hand first |
| Packing own bag | 5 | 8 | Use a picture checklist, then a written one |
| Morning routine unprompted | 6 | 10–12 | Visual checklist on the wall beats nagging, permanently |

## Chores — why they matter more than they look

Household contribution is one of the few things linked in longitudinal work to later self-efficacy and competence. The mechanism is probably simple: a child who is genuinely needed learns they are genuinely capable.

| Age | Real jobs |
|-----|-----------|
| 18 mo–2 | Put toys in a box. Carry their plate to the counter. Throw something in the bin |
| 2–3 | Feed a pet with help. Wipe a spill. Put clothes in the basket |
| 3–4 | Set the table. Match socks. Water plants |
| 4–5 | Make their bed (badly). Sort laundry. Clear their place |
| 6–8 | Load/unload dishwasher. Take out rubbish. Simple food prep |
| 8–10 | Own laundry with supervision. Sweep. Cook simple food. Look after a pet |
| 10–12 | Own laundry fully. Cook a meal. Basic cleaning. Help with younger siblings |
| 12+ | Meal planning, shopping, real share of household running |

**The rule that decides whether this works:** *do not redo their work in front of them.* The badly made bed stays badly made. Redoing it says the standard matters more than the contributor, and the contributor stops contributing.

# Part 10: Emergencies — What Your Child Should Know

Teach these calmly, as ordinary competence, not as a source of fear. Practise annually, like a fire drill.

| Age | Should know |
|-----|-------------|
| 3 | Own first and last name. Parent's first and last name |
| 4 | Home address or a strong landmark. That a shouted "NO!" is allowed and encouraged |
| 4 | **If lost: stay still and shout. Don't wander.** If you must find someone — a parent with children, or someone in a uniform behind a till |
| 5 | Parent's phone number, memorised (set it to a tune — it works) |
| 5 | How to call the emergency number. What to say: *what's happened, where I am, my name* |
| 6 | What the smoke alarm means. The escape route. The outside meeting point |
| 6 | Burns: cool running water, 20 minutes. Never go back into a fire |
| 7 | Basic first aid: pressure on a bleeding wound. When to get an adult |
| 8 | Choking: what it looks like, and to get an adult immediately |
| 10 | Basic CPR concept. How to unlock the door for paramedics |
| 12 | Formal first-aid course if available |

### Teach "if you get lost" properly — because the instinct is wrong

A lost child's instinct is to move and search, which is exactly wrong: two moving people rarely find each other.

Practise it, out loud, before every big outing:
> "If we get separated — you **stop**. Stay exactly where you are and shout my name loudly. I will find you. If a long time passes, look for a mum or dad with children, or someone working behind a till, and say 'I've lost my grown-up.'"

Do the drill in the actual shopping centre. It takes two minutes and it works.

# Part 11: Digital Independence

Handled fully in [Module 18](../18-screen-time-technology/README.md). The skill-teaching layer:

| Age | Skill |
|-----|-------|
| 4–6 | Screens have an end. The timer decides, not a negotiation |
| 6–8 | Not everything on a screen is true. Ads exist and want something from you |
| 8–10 | Passwords are private. Never share your address, school, or photos of yourself |
| 8–10 | Tell me if anything online made you uncomfortable — **you'll never lose the device for telling me** |
| 10–12 | Digital footprint: things don't get deleted. Group chats are never private |
| 12–14 | Recognising manipulation: rage-bait, engagement design, "why does this app want me to keep scrolling?" |
| 14–18 | Their own boundaries; noticing how specific apps affect their mood; sleep and phones |

**The one rule to get right:** never make telling you about a problem cost them their device. If disclosure costs them access, you have guaranteed they won't disclose.

## Common Challenges

### "My child refuses to even try"

Usually the step is too big, or a previous attempt went badly. Break it smaller — much smaller than feels reasonable — and use backward chaining so they end on success. Also check whether you've been correcting during attempts; that's the most common reason a child stops trying.

### "It's so much faster if I do it"

It is. For about two years. Then it's permanently slower, forever, because you now have a twelve-year-old who can't make toast. Build ten extra minutes into the routine rather than taking the task back.

### "My child can do it at home but not at school / not for anyone else"

Normal. Skills are context-bound in early childhood. Practise the skill in a second location deliberately.

### "They learned it and then stopped doing it"

Regression after a new sibling, a house move, illness, or starting school is extremely common and almost always temporary. Step back a rung on the ladder without comment or consequence, and let them climb back up.

## Research & Guidance Notes

- **Drowning epidemiology and prevention:** AAP policy statement *Prevention of Drowning* (2019) — swimming lessons may be considered from age 1; four-sided pool fencing is the most effective single environmental intervention; there is no such thing as drown-proofing. WHO's *Global Report on Drowning* covers the international picture.
- **Pool fencing:** systematic reviews consistently find isolation fencing that separates pool from house substantially outperforms perimeter fencing.
- **Child pedestrian development:** children's ability to judge vehicle speed and distance, and their peripheral visual field, do not approach adult levels until roughly age 10 — the basis for the "hold hands to 8, no solo crossing before 9–10" guidance.
- **Balance bikes:** motor-learning research and practice both support learning balance before pedalling; stabilisers do not transfer to independent riding.
- **Body safety education:** reviews of school-based child sexual abuse prevention programmes find improvements in knowledge and in disclosure. Using correct anatomical terminology is recommended by child-protection bodies specifically because it supports credible disclosure.
- **Household contribution:** longitudinal work associates early participation in household tasks with later self-efficacy and adjustment; causality is not established, but the association is consistent.
- **Button batteries:** ingestion causes caustic injury within around two hours and is treated as a medical emergency by paediatric bodies worldwide.
