# Module 07: Toddler — 12 to 18 Months

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: walking, "I do it", pretend play, and the start of the will
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Walking changes everything at once — what they can reach, choose, refuse, and discover.
- **"I do it" is development, not defiance.** The drive to do things themselves is the engine of competence, and it arrives before the skill does.
- **Pretend play begins.** When a block becomes a phone, your child has started using symbols — the same machinery that later runs language and reading.
- They understand far more than they can say, and the gap causes most of the frustration at this age.
- They still need you to do their emotional regulation for them. Independence in the body arrives years before independence in the feelings.

---

## What's Happening Now

Three things arrive together, and it's the combination that makes this stage feel intense.

**They can go places.** Walking turns a baby who was brought to things into a person who goes to them.

**They have opinions.** Strong ones, about cups, socks, doors, and who carries them.

**They can't say much.** Understanding runs way ahead of speech. They know exactly what they want and have almost no way to tell you.

Put those together and you get enormous motivation, almost no self-control, and very little language. That's not a difficult child. That's the design of this stage.

---

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Walking, or nearly — the normal range for walking alone runs to about **17.6 months**
- Solves small problems by trying things: pulling a blanket to get the toy on it
- **Pretend play starts** — feeding a doll, holding a block to their ear
- Understands far more words than they use
- Follows a simple one-step instruction when there's context
- Recognises themselves in a mirror

### Why pretend play is a bigger deal than it looks

When your child holds a banana to their ear and says "hello", something significant has happened: **one thing is standing in for another.**

That's symbolic thinking. It's the same operation that lets a squiggle on a page stand for a sound, and a sound stand for an object. Pretend play is early literacy that doesn't look like literacy.

**What to do:** join in and take it seriously. Answer the banana phone. Eat the invisible cake and say it's delicious.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- Uses you as a base — goes off to explore, comes back to check in
- Very strong preferences, and protests when blocked
- Shows pride, frustration, and jealousy clearly
- **First flickers of empathy** — looking concerned when someone cries
- Wants control over routines and objects

**Why the intensity.** Their wanting has fully arrived. Their braking system hasn't. That mismatch is the whole story of this stage, and it isn't a behaviour problem — it's a construction schedule.

## "I Do It": A Worked Example

This is the defining conflict of the year, and how you handle it shapes whether your child becomes someone who tries things.

> **The situation.** You're late. Your 16-month-old wants to put on her own shoes. She cannot put on her own shoes. She will not let you help.
>
> **What usually happens**
>
> You give her ten seconds. She fails. You say "let Mummy do it" and do it. She screams and pulls the shoe off. You put it back on. She screams harder. You leave with a crying child, five minutes later than if you'd just waited.
>
> **What she learned:** wanting to do things ends badly.
>
> **What to do instead**
>
> The trick is to **give the wanting somewhere to go**, without giving up the deadline.
>
> 1. **Change the situation, not the child.** Start shoes five minutes earlier. That's the single most effective intervention and it's not really about her at all.
> 2. **Give her a real part of the job.** Not the whole thing — one part she can actually do. *"You do the Velcro. I'll do the heel."*
> 3. **Narrate what she's doing so the effort gets noticed.** "You're pushing hard. It's going in."
> 4. **When you do have to take over, acknowledge it first.** "This bit's tricky. I'm going to help. You can do the Velcro."
> 5. **When you genuinely have no time**, be honest and quick: "No time today. I'm doing shoes. You can be cross." Then carry the cross child. Don't negotiate — that takes longer and teaches that the limit was never real.
>
> **What she learns instead:** wanting to do things gets me a job.

### Why this matters more than it seems

The urge to do things yourself is where competence comes from. Every time it's efficient to override it — and it always is, in the moment — a small amount of that drive gets spent.

**The practical rule: build ten extra minutes into whatever routine causes the most fighting.** Not into all of them. One. That usually does it.

## Six Things to Do

### 1. Name what they're inspecting

As they wander, name whatever they stop at — not what you'd like them to look at.

**Why:** vocabulary sticks best when it's attached to something the child has already chosen to care about.

### 2. Simple pretend play

Cups, spoons, dolls, soft toys, a bag, a toy phone, a box.

**Why:** symbolic thinking, sequencing, and rehearsing daily life. Also, this is where empathy starts — a child who puts a doll to bed is practising taking care of someone.

### 3. Two acceptable choices

> "Blue cup or red cup?"
> "Walk to the door, or shall I carry you?"
> "Coat on inside, or coat on outside?"

**Why:** gives real control inside a limit you've already set. It prevents an enormous number of fights.

**The rule: only offer choices you can live with.** "Do you want to get in the car?" is not a choice. "Do you want to climb in yourself or shall I lift you?" is.

### 4. Set up a solvable problem

Put something desirable slightly out of reach, with a safe way to get it — a low stool, a blanket to pull.

**Why:** planning, persistence, and the feeling of having worked something out.

**Wait longer than is comfortable.** If frustration climbs, make it slightly easier rather than solving it.

### 5. Real jobs

Carrying a nappy to the bin. Putting socks in the basket. Wiping a spill. Passing you things from the shopping.

**Why:** competence, belonging, and a legal outlet for "I do it". A toddler who is genuinely useful is a much easier toddler.

### 6. Name the feeling, then repair

> "You're mad the tower fell."
> *[stay close, say little]*
> "I'm here. Shall we try again?"

**Why:** they can't manage a big feeling alone yet. Doing it with them, hundreds of times, is how they eventually do it themselves.

## Feeding: 12 to 18 Months

### The big change: milk becomes a drink, not a meal

From 12 months, whole cow's milk is fine (if there's no allergy). But the amount matters more than most parents realise.

**AAP recommends, for 12–24 months:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Milk** | **16 oz (about 480ml / 2 cups) a day** of whole milk |
| **Water** | 8–32 oz (1–4 cups) a day |
| **Juice** | **None recommended at this age** |
| **Avoid** | Added sugars, flavoured milk, plant-based milks other than soy |

*([AAP — Recommended Drinks for Children Age 5 and Younger](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/nutrition/Pages/recommended-drinks-for-young-children-ages-0-5.aspx))*

### Why the milk cap matters

This is one of the most common and most fixable problems at this age.

Too much milk fills a toddler up so they eat less actual food, **and** it interferes with iron absorption. The result is a well-fed-looking toddler who is iron deficient. Around **10% of 1–3 year olds** have iron deficiency, and roughly half of those go on to iron-deficiency anaemia.

**If your toddler drinks a lot of milk and eats very little, look at the milk first.** It is very often the whole answer.

### Appetite falls off a cliff, and that's normal

Growth slows sharply after the first birthday, so appetite drops with it. A toddler who ate everything at 11 months and almost nothing at 14 months has not developed a problem — their growth rate halved.

**Judge by the week, not the meal.** And by growth, energy, and nappies.

### What to aim for

- 3 meals and 2–3 snacks at roughly predictable times
- Iron, zinc, protein, and healthy fats prioritised — eggs, beans and lentils, meat or fish, nut butters in safe forms, avocado, full-fat dairy
- Varied textures. Family food, cut safely
- **No pressure.** You decide what's offered, when, and where. They decide whether and how much

### The 14-day taste

If there's a food your child refuses, here's a protocol that has actually been tested.

Researchers took 156 parents of children aged 2–6 and picked a vegetable each child disliked. One group gave their child **a taste of it every day for 14 days** — nothing else, no pressure, no rewards. Another group got nutrition information and a leaflet. A third group did nothing.

The daily-taste group showed the greatest increases in **liking, ranking, and how much the child actually ate.** ([Wardle, Cooke, Gibson, Sapochnik, Sheiham & Lawson, 2003, *Appetite*](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12781165))

**How to run it:**

- One small taste. Not a portion. A single piece
- Every day, for two weeks
- **Say nothing about it.** No praise, no encouraging, no "just try it"
- Accept refusal completely and without comment
- Eat it yourself, visibly, without making a point of it

The neutrality is doing as much work as the repetition. The moment it becomes a negotiation, you've lost the mechanism.

> **A note on the number you may have seen.** Various sources quote "it takes 10 to 15 exposures." Those figures are widely repeated but hard to trace to a specific study, so this guide gives you the protocol that was actually trialled: **one taste a day for fourteen days.**

## Toys

Toddlers are walking, climbing, and moving fast. Sturdy and open-ended.

| Thing | What it builds |
|-------|---------------|
| Push wagon or walking trolley (weighted) | Walking confidence, loading and unloading |
| Low foot-propelled ride-on | Balance, coordination, independence |
| Wooden peg puzzles, 3–6 pieces | Fine motor, shapes, problem-solving |
| Large soft ball and a basket | Throwing, aiming, big movement |
| Stacking blocks and a simple shape sorter | Space, construction, hand-eye |
| Board books with familiar scenes | Naming, vocabulary, routine |
| Simple play kitchen and a few props | Pretend play, language, copying you |
| Chunky crayons and big paper | Mark-making, self-expression |

**Household objects usually beat toys at this age.** A wooden spoon, a set of measuring cups, an old phone, a handbag, a cardboard box. These get used more because they're the things they see you using.

**Choose things that survive being dragged, thrown, and stood on.** They will be.

## Safety: A Toddler Who Climbs

Walking and climbing arrive together, and the risks change shape.

- **Everything moves up, then gets anchored.** Furniture and TVs bolted to the wall. Once they climb, "out of reach" stops existing
- **Stair gates**, and start teaching stairs deliberately — sitting, backwards, one at a time, with you
- **Windows.** Locks or guards above ground level. Furniture moved away from windows
- **Kitchen.** Pan handles turned in. Hot drinks nowhere near an edge. Oven doors get touched
- **Roads.** Hand-holding starts now and is **non-negotiable, every time**. Don't negotiate it once. A child under about 9 cannot reliably judge how fast a car is coming — see [Module 23](../23-life-skills-critical-moments/README.md#part-4-roads--traffic)
- **Water.** Bath, buckets, paddling pools, garden ponds, toilets. Never alone near any of it
- **Button and coin-cell batteries** locked away — a same-day emergency if swallowed
- **Medicines and cleaning products** in a locked cupboard, not a high one
- **Car seat** still rear-facing for as long as the seat allows

## Common Worries

### "They say no to everything"

Usually autonomy rather than opposition. "No" is the first really powerful word they get, and they're testing what it does.

**What helps:** offer choices instead of yes/no questions. Save your own "no" for things that genuinely matter — if everything is a no, none of them carry weight. Use playfulness where you can; silliness defuses more standoffs than firmness does at this age.

### Meltdowns over nothing

Not nothing. The banana broke, and they wanted it whole, and they have no way to explain that and no ability to get over it.

**What helps:** stay close, say very little, wait. Teach afterwards, never during. Most of the win here is prevention — food, sleep, and a warning before transitions.

Full playbook in [Module 24](../24-behaviour-and-discipline/README.md#part-6-the-playbook).

### Climbing on everything

This is how they learn what their body can do, and stopping it entirely isn't possible or useful.

**What helps:** give them somewhere they *are* allowed to climb — sofa cushions, a low frame, the park. Then anchor the furniture and supervise the rest. Teaching how to get *down* is more useful than forbidding going up.

### "Barely any words yet"

At 12–18 months the spread is huge. Check the other things first: do they understand you, respond to their name, point or show, follow simple instructions, babble with lots of different sounds?

**Worth raising if:** no pointing or showing, no response to name, no attempts to communicate at all, or **they've lost something they used to do.**

---

## Red Flags: Call a Doctor If

- No words and few attempts to communicate by the end of this stage
- **No pointing, showing, or bringing you things to share**
- No copying of simple actions
- Little interest in interacting, or no pretend play at all by around 18 months
- **Loses language, social interest, or physical skills** — at any age, always
- Not walking, or not weight-bearing at all, by around 18 months
- You suspect a hearing problem

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Give them one real job today.**

Not a pretend job. A real one, however small: carrying a nappy to the bin, putting a spoon in the drawer, handing you clothes from the basket, pressing the lift button.

Then say what happened, specifically:

> "You carried that all the way. That helped me."

Real participation is one of the fastest routes to confidence, language, and cooperation at this age — and it takes something off your list rather than adding to it.
