# Module 08: Toddler — 18 to 24 Months

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: tantrums, two-word phrases, and the beginning of self-control
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Tantrums arrive because **wanting** is fully built and **stopping** is not. It's a construction schedule, not a behaviour problem.
- Most tantrums are **much shorter than they feel** — the research says half a minute to a minute is typical, and three-quarters are over within five.
- A tantrum contains **two different feelings**, and they need opposite responses. Getting this right is the single most useful thing in this chapter.
- Words explode in this window, and "more milk" is a bigger cognitive leap than it sounds.
- Hitting and biting at this age are almost always frustration plus no language. Block it, then **teach the replacement.**

---

## What's Happening Now

Your toddler understands nearly everything and can say almost none of it. They have strong desires, good memory, and growing independence — and the part of the brain that applies the brakes is years from finished.

That mismatch is the whole stage. It's why the same child can seem impressively capable one minute and completely overwhelmed the next.

---

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Two words together by the end of this period, for many children — "more milk", "daddy go"
- Follows two-step instructions with support
- Sorts by one feature — shape, or colour
- Longer pretend sequences: feed the doll, wipe its face, put it to bed
- Simple puzzles and matching
- Remembers where things live and what comes next

**Why "more milk" is a big deal.** Single words label things. Two words together express a *relationship* between ideas. That's the beginning of grammar, and it's a genuine cognitive jump — not just more vocabulary.

> **A note on timing.** Many charts say two-word phrases by 24 months. CDC deliberately moved this to **30 months**, because at 24 months it was flagging huge numbers of children who turned out fine. If your 23-month-old has single words and understands you well, that's usually normal.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- "Mine." Strong sense of ownership
- Tantrums when blocked, tired, or overloaded
- Starts naming a feeling or two, in themselves or others
- Small acts of empathy — patting someone who's crying
- Plays **alongside** other children rather than with them. This is normal and lasts a while yet
- Tests limits — repeatedly, and often while looking straight at you

**Why limit-testing is not defiance.** They're finding out where the edges are, because edges make the world predictable. A toddler who tests the same limit forty times and gets the same answer forty times is being reassured, not thwarted.

## Tantrums: What the Research Actually Found

Two researchers collected detailed parent accounts of tantrums from **335 children aged 18 to 60 months** and coded what happened in consecutive 30-second chunks. What came out is genuinely useful.

### Finding 1: they're much shorter than they feel

| | |
|---|---|
| Most common tantrum length | **30 seconds to 1 minute** |
| Proportion over within 5 minutes | **75%** |

*([Potegal, Kosorok & Davidson, 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12806226))*

If that seems wrong, it's because a two-minute tantrum in a supermarket is one of the longest experiences available to a human being. **Knowing the real number helps.** You are not managing an hour. You are usually managing ninety seconds.

### Finding 2: there are two feelings, not one

The analysis found two separate processes running inside a tantrum, overlapping but independent: **Anger** and **Distress**.

They behave differently:

- **Anger** — screaming, yelling, stamping, hitting, throwing. It **spikes early**, at or near the very start, and then falls away.
- **Distress** — crying, whining, sad sounds, wanting to be held. It runs **underneath the whole thing** and is what's left at the end.

*([Potegal & Davidson, 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12806225) · [Green, Whitney & Potegal, 2011](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21707157))*

### Why this is the most useful thing in the chapter

Anger and sadness need **opposite responses**, and if you get the timing wrong it goes badly:

| Phase | What you see | What to do | Why |
|-------|-------------|-----------|-----|
| **Anger peak** (first ~30–60 sec) | Screaming, stamping, throwing, pushing you away | **Stay close. Say almost nothing.** Keep everyone safe | They cannot hear you. Comfort offered now is usually pushed away, and words add fuel |
| **Distress** (what's left after) | Crying softens, they sag, they may reach for you | **Now comfort.** Open arms, low voice, few words | The anger has burned out. What's left is a sad child who wants you |

**This explains the thing that confuses every parent:** you try to cuddle them, they scream harder and shove you off, and you conclude they don't want you. They do. **You just arrived thirty seconds early.**

Wait for the shift. You'll start to hear it — the sound changes from sharp and angry to loose and sad. That's your cue.

### A worked example — the supermarket

> **2:40pm.** Nap was short. You're doing "just three things".
>
> **2:52pm.** She wants the packet of biscuits. You say no.
>
> **2:52:15.** Full tantrum. On the floor. Screaming. People looking.
>
> **What not to do:** explain about dinner. Offer a different snack. Threaten to leave. Give in and buy the biscuits. All four make the next one more likely, and none of them work in the next sixty seconds, because she can't process any of it.
>
> **What to do:**
>
> **2:52 – 2:53 (anger).** Get down to her level. Move her away from feet and trolley wheels. Say one short thing, once: *"You really wanted those."* Then nothing. Don't look at the other shoppers — they are not part of this.
>
> **2:53 – 2:54 (the shift).** The screaming loosens into crying. She looks at you.
>
> **Now:** open your arms. *"That was a big one. Come here."* Hold her. Say very little.
>
> **2:55.** She's back. *"We're getting bread and bananas. Do you want to hold the bananas?"*
>
> **Later, at home, calm:** *"You were so cross in the shop. When you want something and I say no, you can tell me 'I want it.' It'll still be no sometimes, but I'll always listen."*
>
> **The answer never changed.** You didn't buy the biscuits. You just stopped talking for sixty seconds — and taught her, at the end, what to do instead.

### Prevention is most of the win

Track when they happen for a week — the [Meltdown Pattern Tracker](../parent-toolkit/worksheets.md) exists for this. Nearly everyone finds the same things: late afternoon, hungry, tired, transitions, shops, or the end of screen time.

Then fix the pattern rather than the tantrum. Snack before you leave. Shop earlier. Warn twice before you change activity.

## Hitting and Biting

Almost always frustration plus no language. Occasionally sensory, or copying.

**In the moment:**

1. **Block it physically.** Move the body, catch the hand.
2. **Six words maximum:** *"I won't let you hit."*
3. **Go to the child who was hurt** — warmly and visibly. This does two things: it looks after them, and it removes the dramatic reaction that sometimes keeps the behaviour going.

**Later, when calm — this is the part that changes anything:**

> "When you're that cross, you can stamp your feet, or come and get me. Hitting isn't one of the choices. Let's practise. Show me stamping."

**Never remove a behaviour without giving them a replacement.** A child who's only told "no hitting" still has the original problem and one fewer option.

**Biting specifically:** give them something they *are* allowed to bite — a teether, a cloth — and keep it accessible.

## Six Things to Do

### 1. Feeling words, out loud, constantly

> "You're mad."
> "You're sad we stopped."
> "You're so excited to go out."

Stick to the core four at this age — **happy, sad, mad, scared** — plus "frustrated". Don't add nuance yet.

**Why:** a child who can say "I'm mad" doesn't need to throw the block. Vocabulary turns an overwhelming body state into something with a handle on it.

### 2. Two real choices

> "Walk, or shall I carry you?"
> "Banana or yoghurt?"

**Why:** control inside a limit. Prevents an enormous number of standoffs.

### 3. Sorting and matching

Socks, spoons, lids, blocks, laundry.

**Why:** categories, attention, and early executive function — using household objects you already own.

### 4. Pretend sequences

Feed the doll, wipe its face, tuck it in, say goodnight.

**Why:** memory, sequencing, symbolic thought — and empathy practice, because they're rehearsing looking after someone.

### 5. Move them, hard, every day

Music, jumping, climbing, pushing, pulling, carrying heavy things, outdoors.

**Why:** genuinely affects regulation, mood, and sleep. A toddler who hasn't moved enough is a toddler who falls apart at 5pm.

### 6. Repair, every time

After the storm has passed:

> "That was hard."
> "You were really upset."
> "We're okay."

**Why:** teaches that a bad moment doesn't damage the relationship. That belief, built now, is what makes conflict survivable for the rest of their life.

## Feeding: 18 to 24 Months

### Drinks — the part most often got wrong

AAP's recommendations for **12–24 months**:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Milk** | **16 oz (about 480ml) a day** of whole milk |
| **Water** | 8–32 oz (1–4 cups) a day |
| **Juice** | **None recommended** at this age |
| **Avoid** | Added sugars, flavoured milk, plant milks other than soy |

*([AAP — Recommended Drinks for Children Age 5 & Younger](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/nutrition/Pages/recommended-drinks-for-young-children-ages-0-5.aspx))*

**Too much milk is the single most common fixable feeding problem at this age.** It fills them up and blocks iron absorption at the same time. If your toddler drinks a lot and eats little, cap the milk before you change anything else.

### Structure beats persuasion

- 3 meals and 2–3 snacks at roughly the same times
- **Nothing but water between them.** All-day grazing is why nobody's hungry at meals
- Keep nutrient density up, because intake is uneven
- Iron, protein, healthy fats, zinc, and fibre are the priorities

### Picky eating peaks about now

It is developmentally normal and it is not something you did.

**What works:** repeated neutral exposure — one small taste, no comment, over and over. There's a tested protocol in [Module 07](../07-toddler-12-18-months/README.md#the-14-day-taste): one taste a day for fourteen days, with **no** pressure, praise, or persuasion.

**What doesn't:** "one more bite", bribes, dessert as payment, separate meals cooked to order, or any commentary at all on how much they ate.

**Always put one thing on the plate you know they'll eat.** It makes refusal survivable for both of you.

### Constipation — check this before you conclude "behaviour"

Very common at this age, and it makes children miserable, badly behaved, and off their food, often without anyone connecting it.

Fluids, fibre, movement, and a doctor if it persists. Worth ruling out whenever behaviour changes for no obvious reason.

### Check the body before the behaviour

Toddlers look "naughty" when they are actually:

hungry · tired · constipated · over-stimulated · under-moved · coming down with something · or in pain from teeth

**Run that list first.** It resolves a surprising proportion of what looks like a discipline problem.

## Toys

Pretend play is taking off, and they need to move.

| Thing | What it builds |
|-------|---------------|
| Knob puzzles, 3–6 pieces | Fine motor, shapes, persistence |
| Pretend food and a few kitchen bits | Symbolic play, copying you |
| Doll or soft toy with a blanket | Nurturing play, early empathy |
| Chunky crayons, washable paint, big paper | Expression, fine motor |
| Wooden blocks in different sizes | Building, space, cause and effect |
| Ride-on or low balance bike | Big movement, coordination |
| Simple colour or shape sorting board | Categories, visual discrimination |
| Ball and a basket | Throwing, aiming, turn-taking |
| Drum or xylophone | Rhythm, making something happen |

**The most-used items are usually not toys.** An old phone, measuring spoons, a handbag, scarves, and a cardboard box. Provide them deliberately.

## Safety

Everything from [Module 07](../07-toddler-12-18-months/README.md#safety-a-toddler-who-climbs) still applies, plus:

- **Climbing is now serious.** They can get onto tables, worktops, and windowsills. Anchor everything; move furniture away from windows; fit window locks or guards
- **Roads: hold hands, every single time**, no negotiation. Children under about 9 can't reliably judge how fast a car is coming
- **Water:** never alone near baths, buckets, ponds, or paddling pools. Drowning is the leading cause of death for 1–4 year olds — see [Module 23](../23-life-skills-critical-moments/README.md#part-3-water--swimming)
- **Button and coin-cell batteries** locked away. Same-day emergency if swallowed
- **Start teaching, not just preventing.** "Hot", "we hold hands near roads", "we sit to eat". They can learn rules now, and taught rules travel to places you haven't childproofed

## Common Worries

### "The tantrums are constant"

Check frequency against the pattern, not the day. Then check the state list above — sleep and food explain most clusters.

**Worth raising with a doctor if:** tantrums routinely last well beyond 15 minutes, happen many times a day across every setting, involve them hurting themselves or others badly, or aren't easing at all as language grows.

### "They're not sharing"

They're not supposed to be. Real sharing needs perspective-taking, which arrives years from now.

**Use turn-taking with a timer instead of sharing.** "When the timer goes" is bearable. "When he's finished" is unbearable, because it has no end.

### Bedtime resistance

Predictable routine, consistent wake time, plenty of daytime movement, and a calm boring response to the fourth request for water.

### Everything is "no"

Autonomy, again. Reduce the number of yes/no questions you ask. "Time for the bath" is a statement. "Shall we have a bath?" is an invitation to say no.

## Red Flags: Call a Doctor If

- **No meaningful words** by the end of this stage
- Very limited eye contact or shared attention
- No copying, and no pretend play
- **Loses language, social interest, or physical skills** — always, at any age
- Extreme, persistent feeding or sleep difficulty alongside developmental concerns
- Tantrums that involve serious self-injury, or that never de-escalate
- You suspect a hearing problem, especially after ear infections

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Pick your worst recurring moment** — leaving the park, getting in the bath, coming off a screen — and add exactly two things:

1. **A warning.** "Two more minutes, then bath." Then again at one minute. Then do it.
2. **A feeling sentence, said before it happens.** "You might feel cross when park time ends. I'll help you."

That's it. You've removed the surprise and pre-named the feeling — the two things that turn a transition into a fight.
