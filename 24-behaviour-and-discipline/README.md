# Module 24: Behaviour & Discipline — Teaching, Not Punishing

> Time: 4 hours | Difficulty: Beginner to Intermediate | Focus: a single coherent model for behaviour, plus a playbook for the specific problems — including food and mealtime behaviour
>
> *The word "discipline" comes from the Latin for "to teach." That is not a nice etymological aside — it is the entire method.*

---

## Key Takeaways

- Behaviour is a symptom, not the problem. **Ask what it is telling you** before deciding what to do about it.
- Most "misbehaviour" in the first five years is a **skill gap or a state problem**, not a motivation problem. Punishment is the wrong tool for both.
- Every correction has three parts: stop the behaviour, connect, **teach the replacement**. Almost every parent does the first, many do the second, very few do the third — and the third is what changes anything.
- Physical punishment is the one area where the evidence is genuinely unambiguous, and it is unambiguously negative.
- Feeding is the clearest case in parenting where **the harder you push, the worse the outcome.**

# Part 1: The Behaviour Equation

Before responding, run this. It takes about five seconds and it changes the response completely.

```
BEHAVIOUR = capacity  +  state  +  skill  +  payoff
```

| Factor | The question | If this is the cause… |
|--------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Capacity** | Can a child this age actually do what I'm asking? | Change the expectation, not the child |
| **State** | Tired, hungry, overstimulated, unwell, constipated, over-scheduled? | Fix the state. Nothing else will work |
| **Skill** | Do they know *what to do instead*? | Teach the replacement behaviour |
| **Payoff** | Is this reliably getting them something? | Change what it gets — calmly and consistently |

Most parents jump straight to payoff ("they're doing it for attention") and reach for a consequence. In the under-5 years, capacity, state, and skill explain the overwhelming majority of it.

### Capacity: what is actually realistic

| Age | Genuinely cannot yet | So stop expecting |
|-----|---------------------|-------------------|
| 0–12 mo | Any impulse control. Any understanding of "no" as a rule | Anything. Rearrange the environment instead |
| 12–24 mo | Remember a rule while excited. Wait. Share | "He knows he's not supposed to" — he doesn't, not in that moment |
| 2–3 yr | Regulate a big feeling. Stop mid-impulse. Genuinely share | Instant obedience. Sharing without support |
| 3–4 yr | Manage disappointment alone. Hold a rule under temptation | Consistency. They'll do it Monday and not Tuesday |
| 4–5 yr | Regulate when tired or hungry. Think before acting when excited | Adult-level self-control in a stimulating environment |
| 5–7 yr | Handle losing gracefully every time. Resist peer pressure | Perfect emotional control at the end of a school day |
| 7–12 yr | Prioritise long-term over immediate. Regulate in social conflict | Adult judgement |
| 12–18 yr | Consistently override reward-seeking in emotional or social moments | Reliability under peer pressure |

**A child failing to do something they are not yet capable of is not misbehaving.** The correct response to a capacity gap is to change the situation — and to keep changing it until the capacity arrives.

### The state check: HALTS+

Before any correction:

- **H**ungry
- **A**ngry / overwhelmed
- **L**onely / disconnected
- **T**ired
- **S**ick, or in pain
- **+ Over-stimulated, under-moved, constipated, or transitioning**

A striking share of "behaviour problems" in the 2–6 range dissolve when a child is fed, slept, moved, and given twenty minutes of unhurried attention. If you fix nothing else, fix these.

# Part 2: The Three-Part Response

Every correction, every age:

## 1. Stop — briefly, physically, without a speech

> "I won't let you hit."

Then physically prevent it if needed — move a body, block a hand, remove the object. Six words maximum. **Do not explain during dysregulation.** The part of the brain that processes explanation is not available, and adding words adds arousal.

## 2. Connect — before you correct

This is the step that feels wrong and works.

> "You were so angry. You really wanted that turn."

You are not endorsing the behaviour. You are naming the feeling that drove it, which is the only thing that brings a child back to a state where teaching is possible. **A dysregulated child cannot learn.** Connection is not a reward for bad behaviour; it is the precondition for the lesson.

## 3. Teach — after they're calm, and specifically

Most parenting stops at step 1 or 2. The teaching step is where behaviour actually changes.

> "When you're that angry and you want a turn, you can come and get me, or you can say 'I need help.' Hitting isn't one of the choices. Let's practise it — pretend you want the truck. What do you say?"

**The rule: never remove a behaviour without installing a replacement.** A child who is only told what not to do still has the original problem and now has one fewer option.

### The replacement table

| Instead of | Teach explicitly | Practise when calm |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Hitting | "I'm angry" + walk away, or come to me | Role-play with a toy |
| Biting | Bite a teether / cloth; come to me | Give them a designated "bite thing" |
| Grabbing | "Can I have a turn when you're done?" | Rehearse the sentence daily |
| Screaming for attention | Tap my arm and wait | Practise the tap; respond fast at first |
| Throwing | Throw *these* (soft balls) *there* | Set up a legal throwing zone |
| Whining | "Ask me in your regular voice" | Model it; respond instantly when they do |
| Running off | Hold my hand / stay where you can see me | Practise in a safe car park |
| Slamming doors | "I need space" + go to their room and close it normally | Name it as the mature version |
| Lying | Tell me the truth and the consequence gets smaller | Prove this is true, every time |

# Part 3: Limits That Actually Hold

## What makes a limit work

**Few.** A household with forty rules has no rules. Pick the ones that matter: safety, not hurting people, and a small number of family non-negotiables. Everything else is a preference, and preferences should be negotiable.

**Clear and concrete.** "Be good" means nothing. "Feet stay on the floor" is a rule a four-year-old can follow.

**Stated as what to do, not what to stop.** Brains process the noun. "Don't run" plants *run*. "Walking feet" plants *walking*.

**Consistent.** A limit enforced 8 times out of 10 is worse than one enforced 0 out of 10, because intermittent reinforcement is the most durable learning schedule there is. **Only set limits you will actually hold every time.**

**Warm.** Firm and kind are not a trade-off. The evidence favours *authoritative* parenting — high warmth *and* high expectations — over authoritarian (high demand, low warmth) or permissive (high warmth, low demand) on essentially every outcome measured.

## The limit script

> **Empathise:** "You really want to keep playing."
> **State the limit:** "And it's bath time."
> **Offer control inside it:** "Do you want to walk or be carried?"
> **Follow through:** *[if no choice is made in about five seconds, choose for them, kindly]* "I'll carry you. You can pick the bath toys."

Notice: no bargaining, no "if you don't, then…", no third warning. The choice is inside the limit, never about the limit.

## Warnings and transitions

Most defiance is actually a **transition problem**. Children live in the present; being yanked out of it feels like a loss.

- Give the warning: "Two more minutes." Then a second: "One more." Then do it.
- Use a timer the child can see — the timer becomes the authority, not you, which removes the fight from the relationship.
- Say what's coming *next*, not just what's ending: "Two more minutes, then bath, then two stories." A named next thing is much easier to move toward.
- Preview the outing before it starts: "We're getting milk and bread. We're not getting sweets today. What can you help me find?"

# Part 4: Consequences

## Natural consequences — the best kind

The world teaches it; you stay out of it.

- Won't wear a coat → is cold *(if it's safe)*
- Won't eat dinner → is hungry until the next scheduled eating time
- Breaks a toy in anger → the toy is broken
- Doesn't do homework → deals with the teacher

**Your only job is not to rescue and not to gloat.** "I told you so" converts a lesson into a relationship injury and teaches the child to hide the outcome next time. Instead: *"That's rough. What'll you do tomorrow?"*

Don't use natural consequences where the outcome is genuinely dangerous or too delayed to connect.

## Logical consequences — related, respectful, reasonable

When there's no natural consequence, make one that *fits*:

| Behaviour | Illogical (a punishment) | Logical (a teaching) |
|-----------|-------------------------|---------------------|
| Throws food | No screen time | Meal ends. Food gets cleaned up together |
| Draws on the wall | Sent to room | Helps clean it. Crayons put away for the day |
| Won't share the tablet | No dessert | Tablet goes away for both, for now |
| Late home | Grounded a week | Comes home earlier next time, for a set period |
| Leaves bike out | Extra chores | Bike is put away by you for two days |

The test: **could you explain the link in one sentence to the child?** If not, it's a punishment, and it teaches only that you have power.

## Repair beats punishment

Where possible, replace consequence with **restitution** — the child does something to fix what they broke.

- Knocked over a tower → help rebuild it
- Hurt someone → get them an ice pack, check if they're okay
- Made a mess in anger → clean it, with you helping

Repair teaches agency ("I can fix things") where punishment teaches only cost ("I got caught").

# Part 5: What Doesn't Work

## Physical punishment

This is the clearest evidence base in behavioural paediatrics, and it deserves to be stated plainly.

The AAP's 2018 policy statement, drawing on a large body of research including meta-analyses of dozens of studies (Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor, 2016), concluded that spanking, hitting, and other physical punishment:

- **does not improve behaviour** in the long term
- is associated with **increased aggression**, defiance, and antisocial behaviour
- is associated with worse mental health and poorer cognitive outcomes
- shows a **dose-response relationship** — more is worse
- and works short-term only through fear, which stops working as the child grows and does damage while it lasts

The AAP recommends that parents do not use physical punishment, in any form, at any age. Verbal humiliation and shaming are included in the same recommendation and show similar associations.

**If you were hit as a child and turned out fine, that is real and it doesn't change the population-level evidence.** Many people are fine despite something, not because of it.

**If you have hit your child:** most parents who do, do it when they are at the end of their own regulation, and most feel terrible afterwards. That feeling is useful information. Repair it directly with your child (see [Module 21, Part 5](../21-building-eq/README.md#part-5-repair--the-most-underrated-skill)), and then treat the *trigger* as the thing to solve — sleep, support, the specific recurring situation. If it's happening repeatedly and you can't stop, that is a reason to get support, not a reason for shame.

## Other things that don't work as well as they feel like they should

| Approach | Why it fails |
|----------|-------------|
| **Yelling** | Escalates arousal, so the child's ability to comply goes *down*. Habituates fast — you have to keep escalating |
| **Shaming** ("big boys don't…", "you're being a baby") | Attacks identity rather than behaviour. Shame produces hiding, not change |
| **Threats you won't carry out** | Teaches that your words are noise |
| **Counting to three** | Works, but teaches the child to comply at 2½. Nothing wrong with it — just know it's a compliance tool, not a teaching one |
| **Bribing in the moment** ("if you stop crying you can have…") | Reinforces the exact behaviour you're trying to end |
| **Long explanations during dysregulation** | Nothing is going in. Save it for later — it works well *later* |
| **Removing something unrelated** | No link, so no learning; just resentment |
| **Time-out used as banishment** | Isolation at peak distress teaches that big feelings make you unlovable |

### Time-out vs time-in

Time-out isn't inherently harmful — it's a well-studied component of behavioural programmes and, done correctly, is a *break from stimulation*, not a punishment by isolation. The distinction:

| Time-out done badly | Time-in / calm break |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| "Go to your room!" | "Let's go sit somewhere quiet." |
| Child alone at peak distress | Adult nearby or present |
| Duration as punishment | Ends when they're calm |
| Framed as exclusion | Framed as regulation |

For under-5s, **time-in is the better default.** For older children who *want* space, respect that — many children over 6 genuinely regulate better alone, and forcing connection then is its own error. Ask them.

# Part 6: The Playbook

## Tantrums (peak 18 months – 3 years)

**What's happening:** the emotional system is fully online and the regulatory system is years away. This is not manipulation — a genuine tantrum is a loss of control, not a strategy.

**During:**
- Safety first — move them if they could get hurt
- Say almost nothing. Stay nearby
- Don't reason, bargain, threaten, or leave
- Lower your voice below theirs

**After:**
- Reconnect first, teach second: "That was hard. I'm here."
- Later, when fully calm: "What could we try next time you feel that big?"

**Do not change the answer because of the tantrum.** If no was right before the screaming, it's right after. But you can absolutely be warm about it.

**Prevention beats management** — and prevention is 80% of the solution. Track when they happen (the [Meltdown Pattern Tracker](../parent-toolkit/worksheets.md) is designed for this). Almost always you'll find a pattern: 5pm, hungry, after screen time, at transitions, in shops. Fix the pattern.

### The public tantrum

The problem is usually the audience, not the child.

Practical: get low, get quiet, ignore everyone else, and if needed leave — calmly, without shame, without lecturing on the way out. Strangers' opinions are not a factor in your child's development. *"We're going to go sit in the car until you feel better"* is a complete and adequate plan.

## Hitting, biting, pushing

**Under 2:** almost always sensory, exploratory, or communicative. There's no aggression in it. Block it, say "gentle," redirect. Don't lecture.

**2–4:** frustration plus no language plus no impulse control. Block, name the feeling, teach the replacement, practise it when calm.

**5+:** if it persists at this age it's worth looking harder — check for a skill gap, a specific trigger, something happening at school, or an unmet sensory need. Persistent aggression past 5, across settings, warrants professional input.

**Biting specifically:** attend to the bitten child first, warmly and visibly. This removes the dramatic reaction that sometimes maintains the behaviour, and it models the empathy you're trying to teach.

## Whining

**What it is:** a voice a child uses when they've learned it works, or when they're depleted and can't do better.

**What works:**
1. Name it neutrally: "That's your whiny voice. I can't understand it well."
2. Ask for the replacement: "Try your regular voice."
3. **Respond fast and warmly the instant they switch** — this is the part that matters. If the regular voice doesn't work better than the whiny one, the whiny one wins.
4. Check whether they're just exhausted. A depleted child whines. That's a state problem, not a habit.

## "They don't listen"

Nine times out of ten it's one of these:

| Cause | Fix |
|-------|-----|
| Instruction shouted from another room | Go to them. Get down. Get eye contact. Then speak |
| Too many instructions at once | One at a time, for under-6s |
| No transition warning | Warn twice, then act |
| Phrased as a question when it isn't one | "Time to put shoes on," not "shall we put shoes on?" |
| They're absorbed in something | Wait, or connect first: sit down, be interested for 30 seconds, *then* ask |
| Hearing | Get it checked. Genuinely — recurrent ear infections are common and under-diagnosed |

## Lying (typically starts ~3–4)

The first lies are a **cognitive milestone** — they require understanding that your mind holds different information from theirs. Unnerving, but developmentally it's good news.

**What works:**
- Don't set traps. Don't ask "did you break this?" when you know they did. That forces a lie and then punishes it. Say: "I see the cup is broken. Let's clean it up together."
- Make truth-telling pay: *"If you tell me the truth, the consequence gets smaller. Always."* Then hold to it, exactly, every time — or you have taught them the opposite.
- Separate the act from the identity. "That wasn't true" — never "you're a liar."
- Ask what they were afraid would happen. Usually the lie is protecting them from your reaction, which is information about your reaction.

## Backchat and rudeness (6+)

Often a clumsy first attempt at autonomy or at expressing a real disagreement.

- Don't take the bait or escalate: "I'm not going to talk about it while we're both like this. Let's come back to it."
- Teach the acceptable version: "You can absolutely tell me you think that's unfair. You can't tell me I'm stupid. Try again."
- Distinguish *disagreement* (allow it, encourage it) from *contempt* (address it).
- Notice if it's constant. Constant hostility usually means something bigger — school, friends, mood — and the rudeness is the presenting symptom.

## Sibling conflict

- **Don't adjudicate.** You didn't see it, and being the judge teaches both children to build cases against each other.
- Describe both sides, then hand it back: "You both want the same thing. That's a real problem. Tell me when you've got a plan."
- Intervene absolutely for physical harm, always: "Bodies are safe in this house."
- Don't force sharing. Use turn-taking with a timer instead — "when he's finished" is unbearable, "when the timer goes" is bearable.
- Never compare children, in either direction. "Why can't you be more like your sister" and "you're the sensible one" both do damage.
- **The actual intervention:** 10 minutes of one-on-one time with each child, separately, daily. Sibling rivalry is largely a competition for a scarce resource. Make the resource less scarce.

# Part 7: Food & Mealtime Behaviour

Nutrition — *what* and *when* to feed — is covered in [Module 15](../15-nutrition-brain-development/README.md) and each age module. This part is about **behaviour**: how to teach a child to eat, sit, and self-regulate around food.

## The one rule that governs everything

**The Division of Responsibility** (Ellyn Satter):

> **You decide** *what* food is offered, *when* it's offered, and *where*.
> **Your child decides** *whether* they eat, and *how much*.

If you cross into their half, you break their appetite regulation. If they cross into yours, you get chaos. Almost every feeding battle is a boundary violation in one of these two directions.

**Crossing into their half looks like:** one more bite, finish your plate, three more peas, you can't leave until, eat this and you can have that, aeroplane spoons, distracting them with a screen so they eat more.

**What that produces**, consistently across the research: more picky eating, worse fullness recognition, and a worse long-term relationship with food. **Pressure is the single most reliably counterproductive feeding practice there is.**

## Why "just one bite" backfires

It feels reasonable, and it's the most common mistake.

1. It turns the food into a **negotiation**, so the food now carries conflict.
2. It teaches that eating is done to satisfy *you*, which overrides the child's own hunger and fullness signals.
3. It makes the disliked food *more* aversive through association.
4. It rewards refusal with attention — often the most attention the child gets all meal.

What works instead is dull: put the food on the table, say nothing about it, eat it yourself visibly, and let them not eat it. **Do this ten or fifteen times.** Acceptance comes from repeated neutral exposure, and the neutrality is doing as much work as the repetition.

## Mealtime behaviour by age

| Age | What's realistic | What to teach | How |
|-----|-----------------|---------------|-----|
| 6–12 mo | Mess, playing, throwing, 5–10 min sitting | Food is fun. Sitting to eat | Let them touch everything. Don't wipe constantly — it interrupts learning |
| 1–2 yr | 5–10 min at the table. Refusing what they ate yesterday | Sit to eat. "All done" | Teach a sign or word for finished, so they don't need to throw |
| 2–3 yr | 10–15 min. Peak pickiness. Strong opinions | Stay until excused. Say "no thank you" instead of yuck | Small portions; let them serve themselves |
| 3–4 yr | 15–20 min. Can pass things, help serve | Serving others. Using cutlery. Waiting for everyone | Give a real job: pouring water, passing bread |
| 4–5 yr | 20 min. Real conversation | Table conversation. Trying without pressure | A daily round-the-table question |
| 5–7 yr | 20–30 min. Helps prepare and clear | Manners with reasons, not rules. Clearing their place | "We wait so nobody eats alone" beats "it's rude" |
| 7–12 yr | Full meal. Cooking | Eating at other people's homes. Packing own lunch | Rehearse the guest script before you go |
| 12+ | Independent | Feeding themselves well. Noticing their own hunger | Cook together; talk about how food makes them feel |

## Specific situations

### Throwing food (1–2 years)

Usually experimentation, or "I'm finished and have no other way to say so."

- Teach and accept the exit signal: a sign, "all done," pushing the bowl away
- One calm warning: "Food stays on the tray. If it goes on the floor, I'll know you're finished."
- Then follow through — meal ends, no drama, no lecture
- Serve less at a time so there's less ammunition

### Getting up mid-meal (2–4 years)

- "You can get down, and then the meal is finished."
- Mean it. Food goes away. No snacks until the next scheduled eating time.
- This is only fair if snacks are on a **schedule** — a child who can graze all afternoon has no reason to sit
- Keep meals age-realistic in length. A 3-year-old sitting for 35 minutes is being set up to fail

### Refusing everything on the plate

- Always include **one thing you know they'll eat**, at every meal. This is the single most useful anti-battle tactic there is: it makes the "no thank you" survivable for both of you
- Serve family-style and let them take their own where practical
- Don't cook a second dinner. Do offer a boring, permanently available default (bread and butter, plain yoghurt) so nobody goes to bed hungry and nobody gets a reward for refusing
- Do not comment on how much they ate — in either direction, including praise

### Eating at other people's houses

Rehearse the guest script before you arrive:

> "At Nani's house, we eat what's offered. If you don't like something, you can leave it. You say 'no thank you' quietly — you don't say 'yuck' out loud, because that's about her feelings, not the food."

That framing — *manners are about the other person's feelings* — generalises far better than a rule, because it gives the child the reason and lets them apply it somewhere new.

### Restaurants

Set it up for success rather than testing endurance: go early, bring something to do, order first, expect 30 minutes not 90, and be willing to walk out with them for a lap around the block. Practise in cheap, loud, forgiving places before expensive quiet ones.

### Food as reward or punishment

Avoid both. "Eat your vegetables and you can have dessert" reliably produces two results: vegetables become the price, and dessert becomes more desirable. Both are the opposite of what you want.

Better: serve dessert as part of the meal sometimes, in a normal portion, with no conditions attached. Removing its status removes most of its power.

Also avoid food as comfort ("you fell over — have a biscuit"). It's an easy habit to install and a hard one to remove, and it's the foundation of emotional eating.

### Teaching a child to notice their own fullness

This capacity is present at birth and gets trained out. Protect it:

- "Are you still hungry, or are you full?" — asked genuinely, with either answer accepted
- Never "just three more bites"
- Let them serve themselves and take too much sometimes. Then: "You can leave what you don't want."
- Slow the meal down. Fullness signals lag intake by about 20 minutes
- No screens at meals — screen-distracted eating measurably overrides fullness cues

### The child who eats very little variety

Distinguish **picky eating** (normal, peaks around 2–3, gradually widens with neutral exposure) from **avoidant/restrictive eating** (very few accepted foods, distress at new foods, gagging, growth or nutrition affected).

The second is worth a referral — to your paediatrician, and often to a feeding therapist or dietitian. Standard picky-eating advice can make ARFID-type presentations worse, so this distinction matters.

# Part 8: Manners — Reasons, Not Rules

Manners taught as arbitrary rules produce compliance in front of adults and nothing else. Manners taught as **the effect you have on other people** produce actual social skill — and they're EQ training in disguise.

| Instead of | Say |
|-----------|-----|
| "Say thank you." | "Nani made that especially for you. How could you let her know you liked it?" |
| "That's rude." | "When you said that, did you see her face change?" |
| "Say hello to Uncle." | "Uncle just said hello to you and you didn't answer. He might think you don't like him. What could you do?" |
| "Don't interrupt." | "I'm in the middle of talking to Amma. Put your hand on my arm and I'll know you're waiting." |

**Age expectations:** please and thank you with prompting from 2, without prompting from around 4–5. Greeting adults from 4–5 with support. Genuine social awareness — noticing another person's state and adjusting — from about 6–7, and unevenly for years after.

**Don't force greetings from a shy child.** Give an alternative: a wave, a nod, standing near you. Forcing a frightened child to hug or speak teaches that their discomfort doesn't count, which is directly counter to what [Module 23, Part 7](../23-life-skills-critical-moments/README.md#part-7-body-safety--consent) is trying to build.

# Part 9: When It Isn't Working

## Run this checklist before concluding anything

1. **Is it a capacity problem?** Am I expecting something developmentally unavailable?
2. **Is it a state problem?** Sleep, food, movement, illness, constipation, over-scheduling?
3. **Is it a connection problem?** Has this child had unhurried, undirected attention from me lately?
4. **Is it a consistency problem?** Am I holding this limit every time, or most times?
5. **Is it a skill problem?** Have I actually taught and *practised* the replacement, when calm?
6. **Is it a me problem?** Am I regulated when this happens? Am I depleted?
7. **Is something changed?** New sibling, new school, house move, illness, loss, conflict at home?

Item 3 is the one most often missed, and it is often the whole answer. Behaviour deteriorates when connection thins — and connection thins invisibly, during busy periods, without anyone deciding it should.

## The ten-minute intervention

If you try one thing from this module, try this. **Ten minutes a day, per child, of one-on-one time where they lead.**

Rules: they choose the activity, you don't teach, you don't correct, you don't check your phone, and you don't turn it into a lesson. Name it — "special time" — so they know it's a thing that exists and is reliable.

This is the most-studied and most consistently effective component of behavioural parenting programmes. It costs ten minutes and it changes more behaviour than any consequence system.

## When to get professional help

- Behaviour is severe, persistent, and present across **home and school/daycare**
- Aggression toward people or animals that doesn't respond to consistent support
- Any behaviour that endangers your child or others
- You feel unable to manage, or frightened of your own reactions
- The behaviour changed suddenly and markedly — this can signal something has happened
- Your gut says something is wrong

Good options: your paediatrician first, then a child psychologist, a parent-behaviour programme (Triple P, Incredible Years, PCIT and similar have solid evidence behind them), or your child's school.

**Asking for help is a parenting skill, not a parenting failure.** These programmes work, and they work faster the earlier you start.

## Research Notes

- **Physical punishment:** AAP policy statement, *Effective Discipline to Raise Healthy Children* (2018). Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor (2016) meta-analysed 75 studies covering 160,000+ children and found spanking associated with 13 of 17 measured outcomes in the undesired direction, and none in the desired one.
- **Parenting styles:** Baumrind's typology and the large subsequent literature consistently favour authoritative (warm + high expectations) over authoritarian and permissive across academic, behavioural, and mental-health outcomes.
- **Behavioural parent training:** Programmes including Parent–Child Interaction Therapy, Incredible Years, and Triple P have strong randomised-trial evidence for reducing disruptive behaviour. Child-led one-on-one play time is a core component of nearly all of them.
- **Feeding and pressure:** Studies of parental pressure to eat consistently find associations with *more* food fussiness, lower intake, and poorer self-regulation — the opposite of the intended effect. Satter's Division of Responsibility is the standard clinical framework.
- **Repeated exposure:** Wardle, Cooke and colleagues showed repeated taste exposure — without reward or pressure — increases acceptance of previously refused vegetables in preschoolers.
- **Emotion coaching:** Gottman's meta-emotion work found parents who validate and coach negative emotion have children with better regulation and fewer behaviour problems than parents who dismiss or punish it.

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Pick your one hardest recurring behaviour. Then, for that one behaviour only:**

1. Write down what usually happens right before it. *(State? Transition? Time of day?)*
2. Write down the **replacement behaviour** you want instead.
3. Teach and practise that replacement once today, while everyone is calm and happy — as a game, not a correction.

That's it. Not the whole system. One behaviour, one replacement, practised once when nobody is upset.
