# Module 18: Screen Time & Technology

> Time: 2 hours | Difficulty: Beginner | Focus: digital media, attention, language, family habits, healthy boundaries

---

## Key Takeaways

- In early childhood, the main cost of screens is often what they replace: sleep, conversation, movement, and play.
- For babies and toddlers, live interaction is dramatically more valuable than screen exposure for language and social development.
- Not all screen use is equal; content, context, timing, and co-viewing matter.
- Family media habits shape development as much as the child’s own screen time.
- The goal is not panic or perfection. The goal is intentional use.

---

## The Evidence in Plain Language

Research consistently shows that heavy or poorly timed screen use in early childhood is associated with weaker language, attention, sleep, and self-regulation outcomes, especially when screens displace direct human interaction.

The strongest concerns in the 0 to 5 period are:
- reduced serve-and-return interaction
- less active play
- poorer sleep
- more dysregulation around transitions
- passive overstimulation

Video chatting is different because it can involve real-time social connection.

---

## Practical Guidance by Age

### 0 to 18 Months

Best practice:
- avoid routine screen media
- exception: limited video chatting with responsive adults

Why:
- babies learn best from live, contingent interaction
- passive screen exposure does not match how infant brains learn language and social cues

### 18 to 24 Months

If screens are used:
- choose high-quality content
- co-view whenever possible
- keep sessions brief and purposeful
- talk about what is happening on screen

### 2 to 5 Years

Best practice:
- keep use limited and intentional
- prioritize high-quality content
- co-view when possible
- avoid screens during meals, before bed, and as the default calming tool

### Older Children and Teens

Shift from pure time limits to broader media habits:
- sleep protection
- digital boundaries
- online safety
- emotional effects of social comparison
- content quality and timing

---

## Why Co-Viewing Matters

When an adult watches with the child and talks about the content, the child gets more language, comprehension, and emotional processing than from passive viewing alone.

Helpful parent behaviors:
- name what is happening
- connect it to real life
- ask simple questions
- stop when attention or regulation starts dropping

---

## Common Screen-Related Problems

### Meltdowns When Screens End

What helps:
- set expectations before starting
- use a consistent ending ritual
- transition into movement or connection, not just “screen off now”
- avoid using screens as the primary emotional pacifier

### Screens During Meals

Often reduces hunger/fullness awareness and conversation.

What helps:
- keep meals screen-free most of the time
- use conversation prompts or simple table rituals instead

### Bedtime Screen Habits

What helps:
- create a screen cutoff before bedtime
- replace with reading, music, or calm connection
- charge devices outside the bedroom for older children and teens where possible

### Parent Phone Distraction

This matters. Children notice when attention is repeatedly broken.

What helps:
- designate protected no-phone interaction windows
- put the phone out of hand during play, feeding, and bedtime routines

---

## A Healthy Family Media Plan

Define:
- when screens are allowed
- where screens are used
- what content is okay
- how transitions happen
- where phones sleep at night

Examples:
- no personal screens at meals
- no screens in the final part of bedtime routine
- outdoor play before entertainment media
- co-viewing for young children

---

## Research Notes

- AAP guidance recommends no routine screen media under 18 months except video chatting, limited high-quality co-viewed content for 18 to 24 months, and intentional limits for ages 2 to 5.
- Parent device distraction reduces verbal interaction and responsiveness.
- Screen use near bedtime is associated with poorer sleep.
- High-quality educational content is still less developmentally rich than live interaction in the early years.

---

## 5-Minute Action Plan

Pick one protected daily screen-free window for connection, such as breakfast, bath, or the first 20 minutes after daycare. Protecting one reliable interaction window does more for development than making a hundred vague promises about “less screen time.”
