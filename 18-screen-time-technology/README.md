## Key Takeaways

- The evidence here is **much more mixed than either side of the argument admits.** This chapter separates the strong claims from the weak ones.
- **Strong:** babies and toddlers learn far less from screens than from people. Screens near bedtime harm sleep. Screens displace things that matter.
- **Weak:** the idea that total screen hours meaningfully predict teenage mental health. The largest careful analysis found the association explains **at most 0.4%** of the variation in wellbeing.
- So: **worry about what screens replace, and about sleep. Worry less about the number on the weekly report.**
- Your own phone use is part of your child's screen environment, and it's the part you control most directly.

## What the Evidence Actually Supports

Screen time generates more heat than almost any parenting topic. The loudest voices on both sides overstate their case.

Here's the honest split.

### Strong: young children don't learn well from screens

Babies and toddlers learn markedly less from a screen than from the same thing presented by a person in the room. This is one of the more replicated findings in infancy research.

The reason appears to be **contingency**. Early learning depends on it: you do something, and a person responds to *that specific thing*, right now.

A screen can't do that. See [Module 01](../01-brain-science-fundamentals/README.md#serve-and-return-the-single-most-important-thing).

The AAP's policy statement for ages 0–5 reviews this literature. It emphasises that what matters is not only content and time limits, but **shared parent–child engagement** with the media ([Council on Communications and Media, 2016, *Pediatrics*](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27940793)).

**Video calls are different.** A grandparent on a video call is responding to your child in real time — that's contingent interaction, and it doesn't carry the same problem.

### Strong: screens near bedtime harm sleep

The light matters. The content matters more. Both raise arousal at exactly the point you need it falling.

And a device in the bedroom extends the night indefinitely.

So much of child behaviour, mood, and learning runs through sleep (see [Module 16](../16-sleep-and-development/README.md)). That makes this arguably the most important screen rule there is.

### Strong: displacement

For young children, the clearest cost of screens is **what they replace**: back-and-forth conversation, active play, movement, sleep, and boredom.

An hour on a screen isn't harmful in itself. An hour that would otherwise have been outside, talking, or bored is a real trade.

**This is why the same hour can be fine or not fine**, depending entirely on what else is in the day.

### Weak: total hours and teenage mental health

You will read confident claims that screen time is driving a teenage mental health crisis. The most careful analysis of the correlational evidence does not support that as stated.

Researchers used **specification curve analysis**. It's a method built to stop analysts cherry-picking the one combination of variables that gives a striking result.

They ran it across three large datasets totalling **355,358 people**.

The finding: the link between digital technology use and adolescent wellbeing is **negative but small — explaining at most 0.4% of the variation in wellbeing.** They concluded the effects were too small to warrant policy change ([Orben & Przybylski, 2019, *Nature Human Behaviour*](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30944443)).

**What to take from this:**

- The debate is genuinely unsettled, and confident claims in either direction are running ahead of the evidence
- **Aggregate hours is probably the wrong measure.** What they're doing, with whom, instead of what, and how it makes them feel are better questions
- **This does not mean "screens are fine, relax."** The strong findings above still stand — they're just about specific mechanisms rather than a total
- If a specific app reliably makes your teenager feel worse, that's real and worth acting on, regardless of what the population averages say

## Practical Guidance by Age

### Under 18 months

**Avoid screen media**, apart from video calls with people who respond to your child.

**Why:** they learn very little from it, and this is the period where back-and-forth interaction is doing the heaviest lifting.

### 18–24 months

If you use screens: **choose good content, and watch it with them.** Talk about what's happening. Connect it to real life. Keep it short.

**Media at this age works when an adult is mediating it.** Alone, it does much less.

### 2–5 years

Keep it limited and intentional. Prioritise quality. Co-view when you can.

**The rules that matter most:**
- Not during meals
- Not in the hour before bed
- **Not as the default way to calm a child down**

That last one is worth dwelling on. A screen is very good at stopping a meltdown. That's exactly why it's a trap.

A child who gets regulated by a screen isn't practising being regulated by you, or by themselves.

### 6–12 years

Shift from counting minutes to building habits:

- Devices charge **outside the bedroom** overnight
- No screens in the last hour before bed
- Screen-free meals
- Outdoor play and movement before entertainment media
- Start talking about content, ads, and what's real

### Teenagers

Time limits stop working here, and start costing you connection. Move to:

- **Sleep protection** — phones out of bedrooms is the single highest-value rule
- **Talking about design, not just content.** "Why does this app want you to keep scrolling?" is a better conversation than "you're on it too much"
- **Watching mood, not minutes.** Does a specific app reliably leave them feeling worse? That's the useful signal
- **Online safety, privacy, and permanence** — see [Module 23](../23-life-skills-critical-moments/README.md#part-11-digital-independence)

**And the rule that keeps you informed:** never make telling you about a problem cost them their device. Say it out loud:

> "You will never lose your phone for telling me something that happened on it."

If disclosure costs access, you have guaranteed non-disclosure.

## Why Co-Viewing Changes Things

When an adult watches with a child and talks about it, the child gets language, comprehension, and emotional processing that the screen alone doesn't deliver.

**What to actually do:**

- Name what's happening: "He's hiding. I think he's scared."
- Connect it to their life: "That's like when you were nervous about swimming."
- Ask a question and wait
- **Pause it.** You're allowed to pause it
- Stop when their attention or regulation starts sliding

This is the same technique as dialogic reading, applied to a different medium — see [Module 22](../22-building-iq/README.md#reading-what-actually-matters).

## Your Phone

This is the part of the screen conversation that gets least attention and is most under your control.

When a caregiver is absorbed in a device, exchanges with the child get fewer and thinner. Children then make more attempts to get the attention back, often escalating to do it.

**Practical, not perfectionist:**

- **Protect specific windows**, rather than trying to be off your phone generally. Meals, the first twenty minutes after school or nursery, bath, bedtime
- **Put it out of sight**, not face-down on the table. Visible phones pull attention even when unused
- **Narrate when you do need it:** "I'm answering one message from work, then I'm putting it away." That's honest, and it models managed use rather than furtive use
- **Notice the pick-up reflex.** Most of it isn't deliberate

**You will not do this perfectly.** The aim is a few protected windows, reliably — not a phone-free life.

## A Family Media Plan

Agree it when nothing is going wrong, and write it down. Involve older children in setting it — a rule they helped write gets followed.

**Decide:**

| Question | Example |
|----------|---------|
| **When** are screens okay? | After school once bags are unpacked; weekend mornings |
| **Where**? | Living room. Never bedrooms |
| **What** content? | Agreed together; new apps discussed first |
| **How do they end?** | Timer, warned twice, then off. Same every time |
| **Where do devices sleep?** | Charging in the kitchen, all of them, including yours |
| **What's always screen-free?** | Meals. The hour before bed. Car journeys under 20 minutes |

The AAP has a family media plan tool if you want a structured version ([HealthyChildren](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/fmp/Pages/MediaPlan.aspx)).

## Common Problems

### Meltdowns when screens end

The most common screen complaint, and it's largely a transition problem rather than a screen problem.

**What helps:**

- **Set the end before it starts.** "Two episodes, then we're going out." Agreed in advance, not announced mid-programme
- **Use a timer they can see.** The timer becomes the authority instead of you
- **Warn twice** — five minutes, then one
- **Transition *into* something.** "Screen off, then we're making pancakes." Moving toward something is far easier than being deprived of something
- **End at a natural break** — end of an episode, end of a level. Stopping mid-thing is genuinely worse, and it isn't unreasonable of them to mind

**And don't use screens as the emotional off-switch.** If the screen is what calms them down, every hard feeling now requires a screen.

### Screens at meals

Reduces conversation, and screen-distracted eating overrides fullness signals — children eat past full without noticing.

Keep meals screen-free most of the time. Yours too.

### "They only want screens"

Usually the screen is just the easiest thing to start. So make the alternative easier — leave something out, half-built.

And cut the negotiation loop by making screen time predictable, rather than something to be argued for each time.

### Content you didn't expect

It will happen. **Your reaction determines whether they tell you next time.**

> "Thanks for telling me. That sounds like it was horrible to see. You're not in trouble. Tell me what happened."

Then deal with the settings, quietly, afterwards.

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Pick one protected screen-free window and defend it.**

Not a screen time policy. One window: breakfast, bath, the first twenty minutes after school, or the last half hour before bed.

**Yours as well as theirs.**

One reliable protected window does more for your child's development than a hundred vague resolutions about cutting down — because it's the interaction you're protecting, and that's the thing the evidence is actually about.
