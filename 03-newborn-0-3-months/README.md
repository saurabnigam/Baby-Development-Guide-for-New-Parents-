# Module 03: Newborn — 0 to 3 Months

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **Focus**: safe sleep, surviving the crying, and how a newborn actually learns
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md). If your baby was born early, use their **corrected age** — count from the due date, not the birth date.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Two things on this page matter more than everything else: **safe sleep**, and **what to do when the crying won't stop**. Both are near the top for a reason.
- Your newborn learns from repeated experience, not teaching. Your face, voice, smell, and touch are the whole curriculum.
- Secure attachment starts now, built out of thousands of ordinary moments where they signalled and you came.
- You cannot spoil a newborn. Responding quickly is not a bad habit — it is the mechanism.
- More stimulation is not better. Calm, well-timed attention beats constant activity.

# Read This First: Safe Sleep

This is the highest-risk window in a child's life for sudden unexpected infant death. Safe sleep takes thirty seconds to set up and it applies to **every** sleep — naps, nights, at home, at your mother's, in a hotel, anywhere, by every person who ever puts your baby down.

## The ABCs

- **A — Alone.** Nothing else in the sleep space. No pillows, blankets, bumpers, positioners, wedges, or soft toys.
- **B — Back.** On the back, every sleep, until they can roll both ways by themselves. Once they can roll both ways reliably you don't have to flip them back all night — but you always *place* them on their back.
- **C — Cot.** A firm, **flat, non-inclined** surface with a fitted sheet and nothing else.

## The numbers that make the case

These are the ones worth knowing, because they're much starker than most people expect:

| Situation | Risk |
|-----------|------|
| Sleeping on a **sofa or armchair** with a baby | Up to **67 times** higher risk of sleep-related death |
| **Bed-sharing** with a baby under 4 months | Roughly **5 to 10 times** higher risk |

*(AAP, 2022 — [parent guide](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx))*

**The sofa number is the one to remember.** Falling asleep on the sofa while feeding at 3am feels like the safer, more careful option compared with the bed. It is by far the most dangerous thing on this page.

## The rest of it

- **Room-share, don't bed-share.** Your baby sleeps in your room, on their own separate surface, for **at least the first 6 months**. Room-sharing lowers risk. The AAP does not recommend bed-sharing under any circumstances.
- **If bed-sharing is going to happen anyway** — and for a lot of exhausted families it does — prepare the bed *in advance* rather than deciding at 4am on a sofa. Firm mattress, no duvet or pillows near the baby, no gap between mattress and wall or headboard, no other children or pets, baby on their back. And know that the risk is much higher if anyone in the bed smokes, has drunk alcohol or taken anything sedating, is extremely sleep-deprived, or if your baby was premature or small at birth. Talk to your midwife, health visitor, or doctor about your actual situation rather than working it out alone at night.
- **Never on a sofa or armchair.** See above.
- **Don't overheat.** One more layer than you'd want yourself. No hats indoors. Head uncovered.
- **Move them if they fall asleep elsewhere.** Car seats, swings, bouncers, and your arms are not safe sleep surfaces. Transfer when you can.
- **Stop swaddling at the first sign of rolling.** A swaddled baby who rolls face-down cannot get back. Swaddling is fine before that, but it doesn't reduce SIDS risk — it's for settling.
- **A dummy at sleep** is associated with lower risk. Offer it once feeding is established, if you want to. Don't force it, and don't put it back once they're asleep.
- **Smoke-free**, in pregnancy and after. One of the biggest things you can change.
- **Breastfeeding**, if it works for you, is associated with lower risk — and any amount helps.
- **Vaccinations up to date** is also associated with lower risk.

## Products to avoid, whatever the packaging says

**Weighted blankets, weighted sleep sacks, and weighted swaddles** — the AAP says don't use them. **Inclined sleepers, sleep positioners, and cot bumpers** (including "breathable" ones). **Hammock-style sleepers.**

Several of these categories have been recalled after infant deaths. The test is simple: **if a product adds something to the sleep surface or changes your baby's position, the answer is no.**

**Home breathing or oxygen monitors** are not medical devices and there's no evidence they reduce SIDS risk. They produce both false alarms and false reassurance. They are not a substitute for any of the above.

## If your baby has reflux or was premature

Ask your doctor before changing anything. The answer is almost always still back-sleeping on a flat surface. **Reflux is not a reason for inclined or side sleeping.**

# Read This First: When the Crying Won't Stop

Please read this before you need it, not during.

## Normal crying is worse than anyone tells you

There is a well-documented pattern to newborn crying, sometimes called the **Period of PURPLE Crying**. The letters describe what normal crying is actually like:

| | |
|---|---|
| **P** | **Peak** — crying increases from about 2 weeks, peaks around **6–8 weeks**, then eases |
| **U** | **Unexpected** — it starts and stops for no reason you can find |
| **R** | **Resists soothing** — nothing works, and that doesn't mean you're doing it wrong |
| **P** | **Pain-like face** — they look like they're in pain when they're not |
| **L** | **Long lasting** — can go on for hours |
| **E** | **Evening** — clusters in the late afternoon and evening |

*([National Center on Shaken Baby Syndrome](https://dontshake.org/purple-crying) · [Seattle Children's](https://www.seattlechildrens.org/health-safety/parenting/purple-education-crying/))*

Every letter of that is **normal**, and it ends. Knowing it's a phase with a peak and a downslope makes it survivable in a way that "why won't my baby stop" does not.

## The thing nobody says out loud

Here is why this section exists.

**Abusive head trauma peaks at 6–8 weeks of age — exactly the same weeks as the crying peak.** Prolonged crying is the number one trigger. This does not happen to monstrous people. It happens to exhausted, loving parents and carers who hit a wall they didn't know was there.

## So: it is always safe to put your baby down and walk away

If you feel yourself getting angry, panicky, or out of control:

1. **Put the baby down on their back in the cot.** A crying baby, alone in a safe cot, is completely fine.
2. **Leave the room. Close the door.** Set a timer for 5 or 10 minutes.
3. Breathe. Drink water. Step outside. Phone someone.
4. Go back when you're calmer.

Nothing bad happens to a baby who cries in a safe space for ten minutes. Something catastrophic can happen to a baby who is shaken.

**Never shake a baby.** Even briefly. Even out of desperation rather than anger. Shaking can cause bleeding in the brain, permanent brain injury, blindness, or death. There is no safe amount.

### Practical Soothing Tools: The "5 S's" & Critical Safety Boundaries

Pediatrician Dr. Harvey Karp (*The Happiest Baby on the Block*) popularized the concept of the **"4th Trimester"**, observing that newborns enter the world sensory-underprepared and retain an innate **Calming Reflex** triggered by sensations replicating the womb:

| The "S" | How It Works | **Critical Pediatric Safety Boundary (AAP 2022)** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Swaddle** | Snug wrapping provides boundaries, dampening the startling Moro reflex. | **Stop immediately when rolling starts:** The moment your baby shows any attempt to roll (~8–12 weeks), swaddling must stop completely. Hands must be free. Never use weighted swaddles. |
| **2. Side or Stomach** | Holding baby on their side or tummy across your forearm activates the calming reflex. | **ONLY while held awake in an adult's arms:** Side/stomach positioning is **never safe in a cot or bassinet**. Sleep must ALWAYS be exclusively on the back on a flat, firm surface. |
| **3. Shush** | White noise mimics the rushing sound of blood through the uterine arteries. | Keep sound machines $\le 65\text{ dB}$, placed at least 7 feet (2 metres) away from the baby's ears. |
| **4. Swing** | Gentle, rhythmic bobbing mimics uterine motion when the parent walked. | Support the heavy newborn head at all times. Never shake, jar, or use aggressive movement. |
| **5. Suck** | Non-nutritive sucking releases oxytocin and lowers infant heart rate. | Offer a clean dummy/pacifier. If breastfeeding, you may wait 3–4 weeks until latch is established, though early pacifier use has no proven harm once feeding is progressing. |

## Tell everyone else, out loud

This is the part people skip. Say it explicitly to every single person who will ever be alone with your baby — partner, grandparents, babysitter, anyone:

> *"If she won't stop crying and you're at the end of your rope — put her down in the cot, walk out, and call me. Never shake her. I will never be annoyed at you for putting her down."*

That last sentence matters. People shake babies partly because they feel they're failing and can't admit it.

**This works.** When one Canadian region rolled out a programme teaching parents exactly this, hospital admissions for abusive head trauma in under-2s fell by about **35%**.

## If it's you

If you're having thoughts of harming your baby or yourself, or you feel numb and nothing toward your baby at all — that is a **medical situation, and it is treatable.** It is not a character verdict.

Call your doctor, midwife, or health visitor **today**. In the US you can also call or text **988**.

## What's Happening in Those First 12 Weeks

Your newborn's brain is organising its senses — sight, sound, touch, smell, movement — and starting to find patterns.

They aren't passive. They are collecting data: your face, your smell, the sound of your voice, the rhythm of feeding, what it feels like to be settled.

**The goal at this stage is not more input. It's repeated, predictable, emotionally safe input.** A baby who experiences *"I got upset → someone came → I settled"* several hundred times is building the earliest wiring for managing stress.

---

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Prefers faces to anything else, especially close up
- Briefly follows a face or object with their eyes
- Interested in strong patterns and contrast
- Goes quiet at a familiar voice
- Starts to recognise the shape of the day — feed, sleep, settle
- Beginning of cause and effect: calms when picked up

**Why this counts as thinking.** A baby staring at your mouth while you talk is doing serious work. That's the foundation of attention, memory, pattern recognition, and language, all at once.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- Calms with touch, rocking, feeding, or your voice
- Increasingly prefers the people who care for them
- **Social smile**, often around 6–8 weeks — a real one, aimed at you
- Signals distress by crying, looking away, hiccupping, or going stiff
- Very short back-and-forth exchanges when calm and alert

**Why this counts.** Newborns can't calm themselves. They borrow yours. Every time you settle them, you're not just fixing today — you're installing the machinery they'll use later to settle themselves.

---

## Reading Your Baby's State

This is the most useful skill in the first three months, and almost nobody teaches it. The same activity is brilliant or too much depending on what state they're in.

| State | What you see | What to do |
|-------|-------------|-----------|
| **Quiet alert** | Eyes open, body relaxed, looking at you | **This is the golden window.** Talk, make faces, show them things |
| **Active alert** | Moving, wriggling, making sounds | Gentle play, but watch for it tipping over |
| **Overstimulated** | Turning away, splaying fingers, hiccupping, arching, sudden crying | Stop. Reduce everything. Hold still and quiet |
| **Tired** | Yawning, red around the eyes, jerky movements, gaze sliding away | Stop playing. Start the sleep routine |
| **Hungry** | Rooting, hands to mouth, fussing, getting frantic | Feed first. Play later |

### A worked example — the overstimulation cycle

This happens in almost every household and is almost always misread.

> **4pm.** Grandma arrives. Baby is in that lovely quiet alert state. Everyone crowds round, talking, passing him about.
>
> **4:06.** He's smiling and making sounds. Everyone gets more excited and does more. *This is the moment it goes wrong — the excitement is a signal to slow down, not speed up.*
>
> **4:09.** He turns his head away. **This is the first brake.** It is not rudeness or tiredness. It means: *I need a second.*
>
> **4:10.** Nobody notices. Someone moves into his eyeline to get his attention back. He turns away again, splays his fingers, hiccups.
>
> **4:12.** He goes from fine to screaming with no warning. Someone says "oh, he must be hungry" — but he fed at 3:30.
>
> **What was actually happening:** he ran out of capacity and gave three clear signals before he crashed. He isn't hungry. He's *done*.
>
> **What to do instead, at 4:09:** take him to a quiet room. Hold him upright against your chest, facing in. Say very little. Move slowly. Two or three minutes of nothing at all.
>
> Often he'll come back and be sociable again. That's a baby who was helped to regulate, and the whole cycle just taught him something.

**The rule: turning away is a request for a break, not a rejection of you.** Once you can see it, you'll see it ten times a day.

## Six Things That Actually Help

### 1. Skin-to-skin

Baby on your bare chest, chest to chest.

**Why:** supports bonding, helps steady their temperature, heart rate, and stress response, and helps breastfeeding get established.

**Do:** 10–20 minutes when you can, especially in the early weeks and during fussy stretches. Fathers and partners too — the benefits aren't exclusive to whoever gave birth.

### 2. Face time at the right distance

Hold your baby **20–30 cm (8–12 inches)** from your face and talk slowly.

**Why:** that's roughly where newborn vision works best. Any closer or further and you're a blur.

**Do:** use nappy changes, feeds, and the alert window after a nap.

### 3. Narrate everything

Talk through what you're doing, in a warm ordinary voice.

> "Right, up we come. Let's get this off. One popper, two, three. Ooh, better. Now a clean one. Legs up… and down. All done."

**Why:** builds familiarity with language long before words mean anything, and makes the world predictable, which is calming.

### 4. Tummy time

Short and often, while awake and watched.

**Why:** builds neck, shoulder, and trunk strength, helps their vision and movement work together, and prevents flat spots.

**Do:** start with **1–3 minutes, several times a day**, and build. On your chest counts. After a nappy change works better than after a feed.

**If they hate it:** 30 seconds more often beats 3 minutes of screaming. Try lying down and putting them on your chest so they can see your face.

### 5. Slow tracking

Move your face, or a simple high-contrast card, slowly side to side.

**Why:** builds visual tracking and early attention control.

**Do:** slowly. Much more slowly than feels natural. Stop when they look away.

### 6. Pause-and-answer

When your baby makes a sound or a face, **stop and answer it.**

> *Baby: "aah."*
> You: "Aah! Is that right?"
> *[wait 3–5 full seconds — this is the whole technique]*
> *Baby: kicks, makes another sound.*
> You: "You've got a lot to say today."

**Why:** this is the first conversation your baby ever has. It builds communication timing, social reward, and — over thousands of repetitions — attachment.

**The pause is the hard bit.** Count silently to five. It will feel absurdly long. Do it anyway.

## Toys: You Barely Need Any

At this stage the best "toys" are faces, voices, textures, and movement.

| Thing | What it's for |
|-------|--------------|
| Unbreakable mirror | Visual tracking, early self-interest |
| High-contrast black and white cards | Easiest thing for a newborn to actually see |
| Soft rattle or wrist rattle | Links sound to movement |
| Play mat with things overhead | Something to track and reach toward |
| Soft cloth book with textures | Multiple senses at once; starts the reading habit |
| Silicone or cloth teether | Mouth exploration |

### Safety notes for that list

- **A mobile must be out of reach and removed by about 5 months**, or as soon as your baby can push up on hands and knees — whichever is first. Anything hanging over a cot becomes a strangulation risk once they can reach.
- **Nothing on this list goes in the cot for sleep.** All of it is for supervised awake time. The sleep space stays empty.
- Check everything for small parts, loose threads, long ribbons, and cords. Nothing with a cord longer than about 20 cm goes near a baby.

## Feeding: 0 to 3 Months

**Breast milk or iron-fortified formula is the whole diet.** Nothing else — no water, no juice, no solids — unless your doctor specifically says otherwise.

### Vitamin D — this one applies to nearly everyone

**400 IU (10 micrograms) per day, from the first days, for every baby** up to age one.

The exception: babies drinking **more than about 27 ounces (800ml) a day of fortified formula** are getting enough already.

If you're breastfeeding or mixed feeding, your baby needs drops. This is easy to forget and easy to fix. ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/feeding-nutrition/Pages/Vitamin-Iron-Supplements.aspx))

### Iron — ask at the 4-month check

Formula contains iron, so formula-fed babies are covered.

For breastfed and mixed-fed babies, AAP recommends **1 mg per kg per day of oral iron starting at 4 months**, continuing until iron-rich solids are established. Babies who were premature, small at birth, or had complications may need iron earlier — sometimes in the first month.

> **An honest note.** This particular recommendation is not unanimous. When AAP published it, its own Section on Breastfeeding formally objected, arguing the evidence for universal supplementation of healthy breastfed babies wasn't strong enough. That disagreement is still live.
>
> **What that means for you:** don't self-prescribe iron, and don't feel you must. **Ask at the 4-month visit** and decide with your doctor based on your baby. Separately, AAP recommends screening everyone for iron deficiency at 12 months. ([Baker & Greer, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20923825/))

### What to expect

| Marker | Typical |
|--------|---------|
| Back to birth weight | By 10–14 days |
| Weight gain | Around 150–200g a week through month 3 |
| Breastfeeds | 8–12 in 24 hours |
| Formula feeds | Every 3–4 hours; roughly 60–90ml a feed early on |
| Wet nappies after day 5 | 6 or more a day |

**Call your doctor about:** poor weight gain, fewer than 6 wet nappies a day after the first week, very long gaps between feeds, a very sleepy baby who's hard to wake for feeds, or persistent pain when feeding.

## Your Own Head Matters — Clinically

Parental mental health is a developmental factor, not a side issue. Untreated depression makes it genuinely harder to do the responsive back-and-forth that builds a baby's brain — and it is very treatable.

**This is common.** Somewhere around **11–18% of women report postpartum depressive symptoms.** Partners get it too.

**Your baby's doctor should be asking you about it.** AAP recommends screening parents for depression at the **1, 2, 4, and 6-month** visits. If nobody asks, say something anyway. ([Earls et al., 2019](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/perinatal-mental-health-and-social-support/))

**Ask for help if** you feel persistently sad, numb, flat, anxious, panicky, or detached from your baby — or if you're frightened by your own thoughts. Say it out loud to a professional. You will not lose your baby for admitting you're struggling; that fear stops far too many people asking.

### Things that actually help

- Sleep in blocks where you can, even if it means someone else does one feed
- Ask for **practical** help — food, laundry, an hour out — not just sympathy
- Get outside daily, even briefly
- Say the true version to one person: "this is much harder than I expected"

## Other Safety Essentials

- **Car seat.** Rear-facing, correctly fitted, every journey including the one home. Straps flat and snug — two fingers under the strap at the collarbone. **No bulky coats under the harness.** A large share of seats are fitted wrong; get it checked if there's a service near you.
- **Learn infant CPR and choking response.** Take an in-person class before you need it. It is one of the highest-value hours a new parent can spend.
- **Never leave them on a raised surface** — changing table, bed, sofa, counter. One hand on them, always.
- **Bath: never leave them, not for a second, not for the doorbell.** A baby can drown in a few centimetres of water. Take them with you.
- **Test water with your elbow or wrist**, not your hand.
- **Smoke alarms** tested and working.
- Nobody carries hot drinks while holding the baby.

## Common Worries

### "My baby cries a lot. Am I doing something wrong?"

Almost certainly not — see the PURPLE section above. Run the basics: hungry, wind, too hot or cold, tired, needs contact, too much going on.

**Call the doctor if** the crying is unusual for them, high-pitched, or comes with fever, poor feeding, vomiting, or poor weight gain. Trust yourself on "this cry is different."

### Day-night confusion

Very common in the early weeks, and it sorts itself out.

Bright light and normal household noise in the day. Low light and quiet at night. Be sociable during day feeds and boring during night ones — you're teaching the difference.

### "They only sleep on me"

Extremely common and developmentally sensible — you're warm, you smell right, and you move. It is not a habit you've created.

Follow safe sleep for actual sleep. Contact naps while you are awake and holding them are a different thing. If you might doze off, put them down first.

## Red Flags: Call a Doctor If

- Rarely responds to sound
- Doesn't focus on anything visually by the end of this stage
- Consistently floppy, or consistently stiff
- **No social smile by around 3 months**
- Feeding is persistently poor, or weight gain is a worry
- **Loses something they were doing before**
- Very little alert engagement with anyone

**And in any of these, go to emergency care now:** trouble breathing, blue or grey colour, unresponsiveness, a seizure, or **a fever in a baby under 3 months**.

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**At your next nappy change, do exactly three things:**

1. Get your face about 25cm from theirs and make eye contact
2. Narrate what you're doing, out loud, in a warm voice
3. **After each sentence, stop and count to five.** Watch what they do. Answer it

That's it. Attention, attachment, and early language, in the time it was already going to take.
