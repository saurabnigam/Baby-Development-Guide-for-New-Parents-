# Module 22: Building IQ — Curiosity, Focus & Thinking

> Time: 4 hours | Difficulty: Beginner to Intermediate | Focus: the cognitive habits that are actually buildable, and how to build them — birth to 18
>
> *Companion to [Module 21: Building EQ](../21-building-eq/README.md). Read that one first if your child struggles to stay regulated — a dysregulated brain cannot use the cognitive skills it has.*

---

## Key Takeaways

- You cannot raise "IQ" as a number. You **can** build the habits that determine whether your child's cognitive ability actually gets used: curiosity, attention, working memory, persistence, language, and metacognition.
- The highest-leverage tool is not a toy, an app, or a programme. It is **how you talk** — specifically, conversational turns and the kind of questions you ask.
- Attention span is trained by finishing things, not by being told to focus.
- Early academic drilling produces short-term gains that wash out; play-based and language-rich approaches produce gains that don't.
- **Struggle is the mechanism.** A child who is never allowed to be stuck never learns to get unstuck — which is the actual skill.

---

## What Can and Cannot Be Changed

Let's be straight about this, because the honest version is more actionable than the marketing version.

**What is largely not under your control:** raw processing speed and the general cognitive ability that IQ tests measure are substantially heritable, and heritability *increases* with age. No parenting programme reliably raises adult IQ scores by a large margin.

**What is very much under your control — and matters more day to day:**

| Buildable | Why it decides outcomes |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Curiosity** | Determines how much learning happens when nobody is teaching |
| **Attention** | Determines how much of any lesson actually lands |
| **Working memory** | The workspace for all thinking; trainable through use |
| **Persistence** | Determines whether hard problems get solved or abandoned |
| **Language** | The strongest single environmental correlate of later cognitive outcomes |
| **Knowledge base** | Comprehension is mostly knowledge; you cannot understand what you have no hooks for |
| **Metacognition** | Knowing how you learn multiplies everything else |

Think of general ability as engine size and these seven as the transmission, the fuel, and the driver. Most children never use anything close to their engine.

**And the biggest lever of all:** protect the floor. Iron deficiency, chronic sleep loss, untreated hearing loss, chronic hunger, and toxic stress all reduce measurable cognitive performance — and all are addressable. Fixing a floor problem beats any enrichment programme ever devised. See [Module 15](../15-nutrition-brain-development/README.md) and [Module 16](../16-sleep-and-development/README.md).

# Part 1: Language — The Highest-Leverage Habit

## It's turns, not words

The old advice was "talk more to your baby." The better-supported version: **conversational turns matter more than word count.**

Ferjan Ramírez, Lytle & Kuhl (PNAS, 2020) coached parents to increase back-and-forth exchanges and found increases in infant language production that persisted at follow-up. Romeo et al. (2018) found that the number of conversational turns — not the number of adult words — predicted both language scores *and* brain activation in language regions.

A monologue is not an interaction. A pause is.

### The turn-taking rule at every age

| Age | What a "turn" from them looks like | What you do |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-------------|
| 0–6 mo | A coo, a look, a kick, an eyebrow | Respond as if it were a sentence. Pause 3–5 sec |
| 6–12 mo | Babble, point, reach, hand you something | Name what they're attending to. Wait |
| 1–2 yr | A word, a gesture, a sound | Expand: they say "dog" → "A big dog! He's running" |
| 2–4 yr | A sentence, a question | Answer, then ask one back. Aim for 5 exchanges |
| 4–7 yr | A story, a theory | Ask what happened next, or why they think that |
| 7–12 yr | An opinion | Disagree respectfully. Ask for their reasoning |
| 12–18 yr | A guarded half-answer | Ask, then be quiet long enough for the real answer |

### The five-turn goal

Most parent–child exchanges die after two turns:

> Parent: "How was school?"
> Child: "Fine."
> *[end]*

Aim for five. This requires questions that cannot be closed:

> Parent: "What was the most annoying part of today?"
> Child: "Lunch."
> Parent: "What happened at lunch?"
> Child: "Ravi took my seat."
> Parent: "Huh. What did you do?"
> Child: "Sat somewhere else. It was fine actually, I sat with Meera."
> Parent: "Was that better or worse?"
> Child: "Better. Meera's funnier."

Six turns. You now know something real, and you did it by asking a *specific, slightly negative* question rather than a general positive one. "What was annoying/hard/weird/funny today?" outperforms "how was your day?" almost every time.

### Expansion — the single best language move for ages 1–4

Take what they said, and give it back one step more complex.

| Child says | Weak response | Expansion |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| "Car." | "Yes, car." | "A red car! It's going fast." |
| "Doggy run." | "Good talking!" | "The doggy is running. He's chasing the ball." |
| "Me do it." | "Okay." | "You want to do it yourself. You want to put your own shoes on." |
| "I falled." | "You *fell*." | "You fell! That was a big bump. Are you okay?" |

Note the last row. **Don't correct grammar directly** — just model the correct form in your reply. Correction reduces how much a child says; modelling increases it, and the grammar arrives anyway.

### Decontextualised talk — the underrated one

From about age 3, the strongest language predictor shifts from naming things present to talking about things **not present**: the past, the future, explanations, hypotheticals.

> "Remember when we went to the beach and the wave knocked you over?"
> "What do you think will happen if we leave the ice cream out?"
> "Why do you think leaves fall off in autumn?"
> "If you could make one rule for the whole family, what would it be?"

This is what builds the language of school — narrative, causal, and abstract — and it costs nothing but attention.

## Reading: what actually matters

**0–2:** the point is not comprehension. It is the association of books with warmth and attention, and exposure to book language, which is more complex than speech. Let them chew the book. Let them turn pages out of order. Read the same one 400 times — repetition is how the words get in.

**2–5: dialogic reading.** This is the evidence-backed technique, and the shift is simple: **the child tells the story, you ask the questions.**

The PEER sequence, applied to any page:

- **P**rompt — "What's he doing?"
- **E**valuate — "That's right, he's climbing."
- **E**xpand — "He's climbing the tall tree to get the kite."
- **R**epeat — "Can you say it? He's climbing the tall tree."

Studies of dialogic reading consistently show larger vocabulary gains than ordinary read-aloud with the same books and the same time.

**5–8:** keep reading aloud *above* their independent reading level. Listening comprehension outruns reading comprehension until around age 13 — so read-alouds are where the vocabulary and ideas come from, while their own reading builds fluency.

**8–18:** the goal shifts to **volume and choice.** A child who reads a lot of what they love outperforms a child who reads a little of what you approve of. Comics, fan fiction, sports writing, and manga all count. The intervention that works here is usually just access plus visible adult reading.

# Part 2: Attention — Trained by Finishing

Attention span is not fixed and it is not primarily about willpower. It is built by repeated experiences of **starting something and completing it without interruption**.

## What destroys it

1. **Interrupting deep play.** The most common and least noticed. A child absorbed in something is doing exactly the training you want. Calling them for a snack, a photo, or "come look at this" is deleting the rep.
2. **Too many toys visible.** Choice overload causes shallow switching. Rotate; keep 5–8 things out.
3. **Fast-cut media.** Content that changes scene every 2–4 seconds trains the brain that novelty is free.
4. **Every gap filled.** A child handed a screen for every queue, car ride, and wait never practises sustaining their own attention.

## What builds it

**Protect deep play, ruthlessly.** If your child is absorbed, do not interrupt. Delay the meal. Skip the photograph. This is the single most valuable attention training available and it requires you to do nothing.

**Finish things.** The puzzle gets completed. The tower gets built. The chapter gets finished. Not rigidly — but a household where things get finished teaches a different lesson than one where things get abandoned.

**One thing at a time.** Meals without screens. Homework without a phone in view. Music without a video. This is modelling more than rule-making.

**Match the demand to the age.** Expecting a 3-year-old to sit still for 30 minutes guarantees failure and teaches them they are bad at focusing.

| Age | Realistic sustained focus on a self-chosen task |
|-----|------------------------------------------------|
| 1 yr | 2–5 min |
| 2 yr | 4–10 min |
| 3 yr | 6–15 min |
| 4–5 yr | 10–20 min |
| 6–8 yr | 15–30 min |
| 9–12 yr | 25–45 min |
| 13+ | 40–60+ min |

Roughly 2–5 minutes per year of age, and **substantially longer for things they chose themselves** than for things you chose. A 6-year-old who cannot sit through 10 minutes of handwriting practice but builds for 45 minutes does not have an attention problem.

## The daily "focus block"

From about age 4, one protected block per day: one activity, no screens, no interruptions, adult nearby but not directing. Start at 10 minutes and let it grow. This is the cognitive equivalent of a daily walk — unglamorous, cumulative, and it works.

# Part 3: Curiosity — Protecting the Question Habit

Children ask a startling number of questions in the preschool years, and the rate declines sharply once formal schooling starts. Whatever the exact figures, the direction is well documented and it is not inevitable. Curiosity dies from three causes, and you control all three:

1. Questions get treated as an interruption
2. Answers arrive instantly and completely, so wondering has no function
3. School rewards having the answer, not having the question

## The four responses to "why?"

Rotate them. Don't only ever do #1.

| Response | Example | Use when |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **Answer it** | "Because the earth spins and turns away from the sun." | They asked something factual and you know it |
| **Turn it back** | "Huh. What do *you* think?" | Their theory would be interesting — which is most of the time |
| **Admit you don't know** | "I have no idea. That's a great question." | Genuinely. Say it often. It's the most valuable one |
| **Find out together** | "Let's look it up." *[then actually do it]* | Once or twice a week |

The third one is the important one. A parent who never says "I don't know" teaches that not knowing is shameful. A parent who says it happily teaches that the boundary of knowledge is an interesting place to stand.

### Worked example — age 5

> Child: "Why is the sky blue?"
> Parent: "Ooh. What do you think?"
> Child: "Because the sea is blue and it reflects up."
> Parent: "That's a really good theory. It would explain it. But — what colour is the sky in the desert, where there's no sea?"
> Child: *[pause]* "...Blue."
> Parent: "So it can't just be the sea. Now we've got a mystery. Want to look it up after dinner?"

You did not deliver the answer about light scattering. You did something better: you took their theory seriously, tested it against evidence, and let them feel the pleasure of a hypothesis failing. That is science, and it took ninety seconds.

## The "I wonder" habit

Model curiosity rather than only rewarding it:

> "I wonder why they built the bridge there and not further up."
> "I wonder how they get the stripes into toothpaste."
> "I wonder what this tastes like."

Say these to no one in particular, several times a week. Children copy wondering far more readily than they respond to being told to wonder.

## Protecting curiosity through school years

School will teach your child that questions have right answers and that having the answer is what's rewarded. Counterbalance at home:

- Ask questions **you** don't know the answer to
- Discuss things with no right answer — ethics, "would you rather," what-ifs
- When they're wrong about something, ask how they concluded it rather than correcting immediately
- Praise good questions out loud: *"That's a really good question. I hadn't thought about that."*

# Part 4: Working Memory & Executive Function

Executive function is the set of three: **working memory** (holding information while using it), **inhibitory control** (stopping an automatic response), and **cognitive flexibility** (switching rules). It predicts academic achievement roughly as well as IQ does, and unlike IQ it responds reliably to practice.

The best-supported way to train it is not an app. It's games — specifically games that require holding a rule in mind and inhibiting an automatic response.

## Games by age — and what each one trains

| Age | Game | Trains |
|-----|------|--------|
| 1–2 | Peekaboo, hide-and-find under cloths | Holding an absent object in mind |
| 2–3 | Simple "Simon Says" (one rule), freeze dance | Inhibition |
| 2–3 | Sorting by one feature | Holding a category |
| 3–4 | "Do the opposite" (I say up, you touch down) | Inhibition + rule-holding |
| 3–4 | Memory / matching pairs | Working memory |
| 4–5 | Sorting the same set two ways (by colour, then shape) | Cognitive flexibility |
| 4–5 | Red Light Green Light; musical statues | Inhibition under excitement |
| 5–7 | Card games with rules; simple strategy games | All three at once |
| 5–7 | Multi-step instructions from memory ("get your shoes, put the cup away, then come back") | Working memory |
| 7–12 | Chess, complex card games, coding, an instrument | Planning, sequencing, error correction |
| 7–12 | Cooking from a recipe alone | Sequencing, monitoring, adjusting |
| 12–18 | Managing their own schedule, money, and commitments | Real-world EF, with real consequences |

**Two rules that make the difference:**

1. **Increase difficulty as they improve.** Executive function training works when it stays just beyond current capacity. A game that is easy is no longer training.
2. **Let them lose.** Losing a game is simultaneously EF practice and emotional-regulation practice. Playing to let them win removes both.

## Working memory in the ordinary day

- **Multi-step instructions, deliberately.** "Please get your shoes, put your cup in the sink, and then meet me at the door." Increase the number of steps as they get older. This is genuine training disguised as a request.
- **Recall games in the car.** "What did we buy at the shop? See if you can get all seven."
- **Retelling.** "Tell Dad what happened at the park." Retelling holds a sequence in mind and orders it — this is the same operation as reading comprehension.

# Part 5: Problem-Solving & Persistence

## The productive struggle zone

Learning happens at the edge of ability, with support — Vygotsky's zone of proximal development. Below it, no learning. Above it, shutdown.

Your job is to keep your child in the zone, which means **calibrating rather than helping**.

Use the escalation ladder from [Module 21, Part 3](../21-building-eq/README.md#the-10-second-rule). The short version: wait 10 seconds, narrate the effort, narrate the problem, ask a question, hint, do part, do it. Move one step at a time and stop as soon as they're moving again.

## Praise the process, precisely

The finding here is more nuanced than the popular version. Praising effort helps, but generic effort praise ("good try!") after failure can backfire — it reads as consolation for lacking ability. What works is **specific, strategy-focused feedback**.

| Weak | Better |
|------|--------|
| "You're so smart!" | "You figured out a way to make the base wider so it wouldn't fall." |
| "Good job!" | "You tried three different ways before that one worked." |
| "Good try!" *(after failure)* | "That approach didn't work. What's a different way in?" |
| "You're a natural." | "You've been practising that for weeks and it shows." |

The distinction that matters: **attribute outcomes to strategies and effort, which are changeable, rather than to traits, which aren't.**

## Teaching the "get unstuck" routine

By age 6 or 7, teach an explicit routine so being stuck has a procedure instead of a feeling:

1. **What am I actually trying to do?** (Restate the problem in your own words.)
2. **What do I already know?**
3. **Have I seen anything like this before?**
4. **What's the smallest first step?**
5. **Try it. Did it get me closer?**
6. **If not — different approach, not harder effort.**

Say this out loud yourself when *you* get stuck — assembling furniture, debugging the wifi, following a recipe. Children learn problem-solving from watching adults be visibly stuck and visibly systematic.

## Metacognition: thinking about thinking

From about age 7, the highest-return cognitive skill:

> "How did you work that out?"
> "What made it click?"
> "What would you do differently next time?"
> "Which part is still fuzzy?"
> "How do you know when you actually understand something versus when you've just read it?"

Also teach the two evidence-backed study facts, because most children (and adults) get them backwards:

- **Re-reading feels effective and mostly isn't.** Testing yourself feels harder and works far better (retrieval practice).
- **Spacing beats cramming.** Same total time, split across days, produces much more durable learning.

Teaching a 10-year-old these two things is worth more than years of homework supervision.

# Part 6: Number Sense & Everyday Math

Early math ability is one of the strongest predictors of later academic outcomes — stronger, in some analyses, than early reading. And "math talk" at home varies enormously between families in a way that shows up in school.

None of this requires worksheets.

| Age | What to build | How, in the ordinary day |
|-----|---------------|--------------------------|
| 1–2 | Number words as sounds; more/less | Count stairs, count fingers, "one more" |
| 2–3 | Counting objects with one-to-one correspondence | Touch each item as you count. Count the same set twice |
| 3–4 | **Cardinality** — the last number *is* the quantity | "How many? …So there are three." Ask "how many" after counting |
| 3–4 | Comparison, shapes, patterns | "Who has more?" "Make a pattern: red, blue, red, blue…" |
| 4–5 | Subitising (seeing 3 without counting); adding by one | Dice and dominoes. "You had 2, I gave you 1, now?" |
| 5–7 | Number bonds, part-whole, estimation | "How many do you think? Let's count and see how close you were" |
| 5–7 | Money, time, measurement | Let them pay and count change. Cook with measuring cups |
| 7–12 | Fractions, proportion, mental strategies | Halving recipes. Splitting a bill. "Is that a good deal?" |
| 12–18 | Real budgeting, percentages, statistics, risk | Their own money. Reading a news statistic critically |

**The two highest-value habits:**

1. **Estimate before counting.** "How many grapes do you think are in there?" Then count. This builds number sense in a way that counting alone doesn't.
2. **Ask "how do you know?"** rather than checking the answer. The reasoning is the thing you're building.

**And one thing to avoid:** saying "I was never good at maths." The evidence on parents transmitting math anxiety — particularly to daughters — is reasonably strong. Even said as a joke, it hands your child a permission slip to quit.

# Part 7: What Doesn't Work

Honest accounting, because the market for this is enormous and mostly evidence-free.

| Claim | Reality |
|-------|---------|
| **Baby videos / "educational" DVDs** | No language benefit under 2; some studies find negative associations. AAP recommends no routine screen media under 18 months. Babies do not learn language from screens — the "video deficit" is one of the most replicated findings in infancy research |
| **Flashcards for infants and toddlers** | Produce memorised responses, not concepts. Time is far better spent in conversation |
| **Mozart effect** | Meta-analysis (Pietschnig et al., 2010) found no meaningful IQ effect from passive listening. *Learning* an instrument is a different matter and does have benefits |
| **Commercial brain-training apps** | Improvements are largely task-specific and don't transfer to general ability. Broadly the same conclusion for children and adults |
| **Early academic drilling (ages 3–5)** | Produces early gains that typically wash out by around age 8, sometimes with worse motivation and social outcomes. Play-based approaches match or beat it on academics and win clearly elsewhere |
| **Omega-3 supplements in well-nourished children** | Trials in already well-nourished children are largely null. Correcting an actual deficiency is a completely different matter and does help |
| **"Learning styles"** | No good evidence that matching teaching to a visual/auditory/kinesthetic "style" improves learning. The construct has failed repeatedly |

**What actually has evidence behind it:** conversational turns, dialogic reading, sufficient sleep, adequate iron, play, learning an instrument, physical activity, low toxic stress, and a home where books and thinking are visibly valued.

## Building the Knowledge Base

An underrated point: **reading comprehension is mostly knowledge, not technique.** A child who knows nothing about the sea will not understand a passage about tides, however good their decoding is.

So one of the most useful things you can do is give your child a wide, shallow map of the world:

- Museums, zoos, factories, farms, building sites, kitchens, workshops
- Documentaries and non-fiction books, on anything at all
- Talk about your own work in real detail
- Maps and globes; where things come from; how things are made
- Let them meet adults who do different things and ask them questions

Breadth beats depth here. You are not producing an expert. You are producing a child who has a hook to hang new information on, in as many domains as possible.

## Common Challenges

### "My child gives up immediately"

Usually one of three causes: the task is genuinely above their level; they've been rescued too often; or failure has been treated as a problem in your household. Diagnose which. Then lower the difficulty until they can succeed, and rebuild upward — confidence comes from a track record, not from encouragement.

### "My child can't focus"

Check the demand first. A 5-year-old asked to sit for 30 minutes doesn't have a focus problem — they have an expectation problem. Then check sleep, iron, hearing, and screen habits, in that order. Then check whether they *can* focus deeply on things they chose: if yes, this is motivation and demand-matching, not attention capacity. If your child cannot sustain attention on anything, including things they love, across settings, raise it with your paediatrician.

### "My child is behind in reading"

Move fast, not gently. Reading difficulties respond dramatically better to early intervention than late. Get hearing and vision checked, talk to the school, and ask specifically about phonological awareness screening. Meanwhile, keep reading aloud to them well above their own level so the vocabulary and the love of story don't stall while decoding catches up.

### "My child is 'gifted' and bored"

Boredom in a bright child is usually not a need for more content — it's a need for harder problems and more autonomy. Depth beats acceleration for most children: going deeper into something they love builds persistence, while racing ahead often builds only the expectation of ease, which is a liability later.

### "How much should I push?"

The test: **does the effort come with warmth?** Pushing plus warmth builds capability. Pushing minus warmth builds anxiety and, eventually, avoidance. If your child is hiding their mistakes from you, you have crossed the line — that is the clearest single signal.

## Research Notes

- **Conversational turns:** Romeo et al. (2018, *Psychological Science*) found conversational turns — not adult word count — predicted both language scores and Broca's-area activation, and did so independent of socioeconomic status. Ferjan Ramírez, Lytle & Kuhl (2020, *PNAS*) showed parent coaching increased turns and infant language.
- **The "word gap":** Hart & Risley (1995) is widely cited but has failed to replicate (Sperry, Sperry & Miller, 2019) and rested on 42 families. Treat the turn-taking evidence as the stronger claim.
- **Dialogic reading:** Whitehurst and colleagues' interactive shared-reading technique shows consistent vocabulary advantages over standard read-aloud in randomised studies.
- **Executive function:** Diamond & Lee (2011, *Science*) reviewed interventions and found EF improves with practice that continually increases in difficulty — and that gains are largest for children who start lowest.
- **Praise:** Mueller & Dweck (1998) established the intelligence-praise problem; later work (e.g. Gunderson et al., 2013) refined it to process- and strategy-focused praise specifically.
- **Retrieval practice and spacing:** Roediger & Karpicke (2006) and the broader distributed-practice literature are among the most robust findings in cognitive psychology.
- **Early math:** Duncan et al. (2007) found early math skills the strongest school-entry predictor of later achievement across six longitudinal datasets.
- **Math anxiety transmission:** Maloney et al. (2015) found math-anxious parents helping with homework predicted children's math anxiety and lower achievement.
- **Video deficit:** infants learn markedly less from screen presentation than from equivalent live presentation — replicated across many labs and tasks.

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Do one of these today, and only one:**

- **Under 2?** Pause for five full seconds after every sound your baby makes, and answer it like a sentence.
- **2–5?** At bedtime, read one page and let *them* tell you what's happening. Ask three questions. Don't read the words.
- **5–10?** At dinner, ask "what was the most annoying thing today?" and get to five turns.
- **10+?** Ask them to teach you something they know that you don't. Take it seriously. Ask follow-up questions.

Then do it again tomorrow. The compounding here is real, and it is almost entirely in the repetition.
