# Module 37: Growing Up Safe — Body Safety, Online Life, AI, and Teen Health

> Time: 3 hours | Difficulty: Intermediate | For: parents of children aged 2 to 18. Read the body-safety section now, whatever your child's age. Read the rest before age 8.

---

## Key Takeaways

- One skill protects your child across every section of this module: **they tell you early.** Children tell parents who stay calm, say thank you, and do not punish them for telling. Body safety, sextortion, AI companions and suicidal thoughts all depend on this one channel.
- Body safety starts at about age 2: correct names for body parts, "no secrets, only surprises," a list of safe adults, and No-Go-Tell. School programmes teaching these ideas raise children's knowledge without making them more anxious **[A]**. In India, most abusers are people the child already knows. So the rules must cover relatives, helpers, drivers and tutors, not only strangers.
- Online risk changes with age: content and contact risk in primary school, then grooming, sextortion and pornography in the pre-teen and teen years. Teach the sextortion script before age 11: **do not pay, do not delete, block, report, and tell us. It is not your fault.**
- Give phones in stages, based on readiness. Researchers disagree about how much social media harms teen mental health. They agree about the practical rules: no phones in bedrooms overnight, protect sleep, and delay the open social feed.
- AI is now part of childhood. The 2026 AAP review says young children struggle to tell AI from people, and that AI is not a friend **[A]**. In a randomised trial, unrestricted chatbot help raised practice scores but lowered later exam scores. A tutor-style version avoided that loss **[B]**.
- Prepare children for puberty before it starts, from about age 8. For substances, delay the first use and never supply alcohol. For mental health, ask directly about suicide, lock away medicines and pesticides, and save **Tele-MANAS 14416** in every family phone.

## The Principle Under Every Section: Be the Person They Tell

Your 7-year-old says, quietly, "Uncle said not to tell you he tickles me in the car." Your 13-year-old says, "Someone has a photo of me and wants money." Your 16-year-old says, "I don't see the point of anything." Each of these sentences is a test. Your child is checking whether it is safe to say the next thing.

```
Child tells you something hard
            │
   Your face and your first sentence
     ┌──────────────┴───────────────┐
 Calm, "Thank you for          Shock, blame, "Why were you
 telling me. You're not        even on that app?", phone
 in trouble."                  taken away
     │                              │
 Child tells again —           Child learns to hide —
 earlier, about smaller        later, about bigger
 things                        things
```

Module 14's "10:47 p.m. doorway test" is the teen version of this. One correction to that module: the research on teen disclosure (mostly Swedish and US studies of delinquency) supports the idea that what parents know comes mainly from what teens choose to tell them **[C]**. It does not prove that surveillance never helps. The practical rule still holds. For every topic below, the goal is that your child reaches **you** before the harm gets worse.

## Body Safety From Age 2

**The scene.** At a family wedding in Lucknow, a great-aunt insists that 3-year-old Anaya kiss her. Anaya hides behind her mother. Her mother says, "She can give you a high-five or a wave. She decides about her body." The aunt is offended for about ten minutes. Anaya learns that her "no" counts, even with adults she loves.

**The rule:** teach body safety the way you teach road safety. Start early, keep it matter-of-fact, repeat it often, and include the adults your child knows best.

### The four rules, by age

| Age | What to teach | Sample words |
|-----|---------------|--------------|
| 2-3 | Correct names for all body parts, including penis, vulva, vagina, bottom and breasts. Use them at bath time, in the same tone as "elbow" | "This is your vulva. It's a private part." |
| 3-5 | **Private parts rule**: private parts are covered by a swimsuit. Nobody touches them or asks to see them, except to keep you clean or healthy. A parent is present at a doctor's check-up | "If anyone tries, you say NO, you GO away, and you TELL me." |
| 3-6 | **Secrets vs surprises**: a surprise is fun and ends soon (a birthday gift). A secret that must be kept forever, especially one about touching, photos or presents, is never OK | "We don't keep secrets in our family. We keep surprises." |
| 4-8 | **Safe-adult list**: name at least three adults your child can tell, including one outside your home (a teacher, an aunt). Practise "what if" questions | "If you told Mummy and she didn't listen, who would you tell next?" |
| 8-12 | Grooming signs, online contact, and your right to say no to anyone, including family | "Anyone who asks you to keep a secret from us is breaking a family rule, even if you like them." |

Correct names matter for two reasons. A child who says "he touched my vulva" is understood at once by a teacher, a doctor or the police. Pet names can hide what happened. Correct names also tell the child that these body parts are not shameful to talk about.

### Why this matters in Indian households

Many adults care for a young Indian child: grandparents, relatives, domestic helpers, drivers, school-van attendants and tuition teachers. Indian crime data show that most people charged under the child-sexual-offences law are known to the child. So "stranger danger" alone misses most of the risk. Choosing and briefing helpers is covered in Module 35. Here are the supervision rules that follow from this:

- [ ] Your child is never alone with one adult in a closed room, car or van when you can avoid it. This applies to tuition, drivers, and school-van rides with no female attendant.
- [ ] Tutors teach in open spaces with the door open.
- [ ] You know every adult who regularly spends time alone with your child, and you drop in unannounced. Adults who give "special" gifts, arrange time alone, or say "this is our secret" get a polite, firm boundary.

**Grooming usually looks like kindness before it looks like harm:**

```
Special attention and gifts ─▶ Time alone ─▶ Secrets ("don't tell Mummy")
        ─▶ Testing touch (tickling, wrestling, "accidental" touch)
        ─▶ Sexual contact ─▶ Threats or guilt to keep the child silent
```

Every step before the last two breaks one of your four rules. That is why the rules protect children: they turn the early steps into things the child reports.

### What the evidence shows

A Cochrane review of school-based prevention programmes (Walsh et al., 2015; 24 trials, 5,802 children) found:

```
Knowledge of body-safety concepts:  SMD 0.61 (18 trials)      [A]
Knowledge still present at 6 months: SMD 0.69                  [A]
Protective behaviour in simulations: OR 5.71 (only 2 trials,
                                      102 children)             [C]
Disclosure of abuse:                 OR 3.56 (3 studies)        [C]
Anxiety or fear:                     no increase (SMD -0.08)    [A]
```

In plain words: children learn and remember the ideas, and teaching does not frighten them. The behaviour and disclosure results come from few studies. No trial has yet proven that these programmes reduce abuse itself. Home teaching is the same content, repeated for years. That is why parents are the main programme.

### The law and the helplines in India

- **POCSO Act 2012** (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences) protects every child under 18. Under Section 19, any adult who knows about an offence, or fears one is likely, must report it to the police or the Special Juvenile Police Unit. Under Section 21, failing to report can be punished with up to 6 months in prison or a fine. The child is never punished for not reporting.
- **Childline 1098**: free and open 24x7. It is run under the Ministry of Women and Child Development and linked to the national emergency number **112**. Children can call it themselves. Teach the number to children aged 6 and older.
- **112** for any emergency. For online abuse, see the cyber-crime routes in the next section.

**If your child tells you** — the first minutes matter more than anything else:

1. Stay calm. Say "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. You did the right thing."
2. Listen. Do not question them in detail and do not suggest words. Write down their exact words and the time afterwards.
3. Keep your child away from the person. Do not confront the person in front of your child.
4. Report to 1098 or the police (outside India: your national child helpline or the police). Ask for a paediatrician experienced in child protection.
5. Get your child counselling. Get support for yourself too (Tele-MANAS 14416).

## Online Safety by Age

**The scene.** Eleven-year-old Kabir plays BGMI (Battlegrounds Mobile India) with school friends. A player who says he is "13, from Pune" praises his skills for two weeks. Then he gifts Kabir in-game currency and suggests they "talk on Snap instead." That move to a private app is the moment to act, and Kabir only reports it if he already knows the pattern.

**The rule:** match the rules to the risks at each age, and teach the scripts before your child needs them.

| Age | Main risks | Rules and settings |
|-----|-----------|--------------------|
| Under 6 | Upsetting videos, autoplay, in-app purchases | Shared device only, in shared rooms. Kids profiles, autoplay off, purchase passwords on (Module 18) |
| 6-9 | Violent or sexual content found by search, strangers in game chat | Games with chat off or friends-only. Search filters on. "Show me anything weird. You won't be in trouble" |
| 10-12 | Grooming, pressure to share images, first exposure to pornography, group-chat bullying | No public profiles. Friends-only gaming. Teach grooming signs and the sextortion script. Talk about pornography (script below) |
| 13-15 | Sextortion, pornography, cyberbullying, oversharing | Private accounts and teen-account settings. Location sharing off for everyone except family. Monthly settings check together |
| 16-18 | Image-based abuse, scams, digital footprint | Teen manages their own settings. Parent is the backup, not the monitor |

### Grooming and sextortion

Sextortion is blackmail: someone gets a sexual image (often by pretending to be a teen who first sends a fake one) and then demands money or more images. The US National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) reports:

```
Financial sextortion reports in 2024:   nearly 100 per day (US data)
Teen boys who died by suicide after
sextortion, known to NCMEC since 2021:  at least 36
Reports involving generative AI, 2024:  up 1,325% on the previous year
```

Most financial sextortion targets teenage boys. Offenders now also use AI to create fake nude images from ordinary photos. So a child does not have to have sent anything to be targeted.

**Teach this script by age 11, and repeat it every year:**

```
Someone threatens to share an image of you
   │
   ├─ Do NOT pay or send more. Paying rarely stops it.
   ├─ Do NOT delete the chat or your account. It is evidence.
   ├─ Screenshot, block, and report on the app.
   ├─ Tell us. You will not be in trouble. It is not your fault.
   └─ We report: cybercrime.gov.in or helpline 1930 (India);
      NCMEC's "Take It Down" helps remove images of under-18s.
```

The last line matters most. Offenders rely on the child's shame. A child who has heard "you will never be in trouble for telling us about this" many times has a way out.

### Pornography

A 2022 Common Sense Media survey of more than 1,300 US teens aged 13-17 found that **73%** had seen online pornography. The average age at first exposure was **12**, and **15%** first saw it at 10 or younger. **58%** said they had come across it by accident. These are US figures. Indian surveys of similar quality are scarce. The pattern is probably similar wherever children have smartphones.

**Talk about it before age 10, briefly and calmly:** "You might see videos of people with no clothes doing sexual things. Porn is made up, like an action film. It often shows things that are unkind or unsafe, and it isn't what real relationships are like. If you see it, close it and tell me. You won't be in trouble." If you find that your teen has watched it, stay calm. Talk about consent and respect, not shame.

### Cyberbullying and gaming

- **Cyberbullying**: screenshot everything, do not reply, block, and report to the platform and the school. If there are threats, sexual images or impersonation, it is a crime: report it to 1930 or cybercrime.gov.in.
- **Gaming**: most heavy gaming is not a disorder. The WHO's ICD-11 defines gaming disorder as three things: loss of control, gaming taking priority over other life activities, and continuing despite harm, usually for at least 12 months. The WHO says it affects only a small share of gamers. If school, sleep or friendships are collapsing, see a professional (Module 18 covers ordinary limits).

### Sharenting and your child's digital footprint

Your child's online life starts with your posts. Before posting, ask: Would my child be happy to see this at 15 (bath photos, tantrums and potty training: no)? Does it show the school uniform, school name, home or a routine location? Is the account private, with an audience I actually know? Every public photo can now be turned into a fake image with AI. Share with a small family group instead of a public feed. From about age 7, ask your child "Can I post this?" That teaches consent through practice.

### India's DPDP Act: what it means for your family

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 treats **everyone under 18 as a child**. Section 9 says:

- Companies must get **verifiable consent from a parent** before processing a child's personal data.
- Companies must not do **tracking, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising** aimed at children.
- The government can exempt some kinds of companies or purposes, for example certain education or health services.

The DPDP Rules were notified on **13 November 2025**. Most obligations, including the parental-consent rule (Rule 10), take effect 18 months later, around **May 2027**. So you will increasingly be asked to verify yourself as a parent, sometimes through DigiLocker. Treat every consent request as a real decision, not a click. The law limits what companies may collect. It does not supervise your child for you.

## The First Phone: A Staged Plan

**The scene.** Ten-year-old Meher says, "Everyone in my class has a phone." Her parents ask the class WhatsApp group. It turns out six of 32 children have smartphones. Three families agree to hold off together until Class 8. Meher gets a basic phone for her new solo bus route.

**The rule:** give phones based on **stages and readiness**, not on a birthday, and move up one stage at a time. This matches Module 18's advice (a basic phone first, and a smartphone at about 13-14, coordinated with other families).

| Stage | Ready when your child... | Rules at this stage |
|-------|-------------------------|---------------------|
| 0: Shared family device (to about 10) | — | Used in shared rooms. You hold the passwords |
| 1: Basic phone or watch | Travels alone, to tuition or on the bus | Calls and texts only. Charges in the kitchen at night |
| 2: Smartphone with limits (about 13-14) | Follows Stage 1 rules for months, tells you about problems, and knows the sextortion script | You approve apps. No social media. Screen-time limits. Out of the bedroom by an agreed hour |
| 3: Social media (about 15-16 or later) | Is at least the platform's minimum age (13 on most; 16 by law in some countries), has handled Stage 2 well, and you have discussed privacy | Private account, teen-account settings on, you review settings together monthly |
| 4: Autonomy | Is nearly 18 and has a record of good judgment | Your role shifts to advice and backup |

**Family phone agreement:** all phones, including parents' phones, charge outside bedrooms overnight. No phones at meals. Parents approve new apps until Stage 3. Location is shared within the family only. Write this line into it: "If anything online scares or pressures you, telling us will never cost you your phone." Review the agreement every birthday.

### The social media debate, stated fairly

| The concern | The scepticism |
|-------------|----------------|
| The 2023 US Surgeon General's advisory: up to 95% of 13-17-year-olds use social media, and more than a third use it "almost constantly" | Orben & Przybylski (2019), 355,358 adolescents: digital technology use explained **at most 0.4%** of the variation in wellbeing |
| A US cohort of 12-15-year-olds (6,595) quoted in the advisory: more than **3 hours a day** of social media went with **double the risk** of depression and anxiety symptoms, adjusted for earlier mental health | Odgers' 2024 *Nature* review of *The Anxious Generation*: the book mistakes correlation for causation. Other causes (economic hardship, school pressure) may be missed |
| Haidt (2024): phone-based childhood is a main cause of the rise in teen anxiety and depression. He calls for no smartphones before high school and no social media before 16 | Goodyear et al. (2025), 30 English schools: restrictive school phone policies were not linked to better wellbeing or less phone use overall. Cross-sectional, so not proof either way |

**The honest reading [C]:** average effects of total screen time are small. The risks cluster in specific harms: lost sleep, sextortion, harmful content, cyberbullying, and time taken from sleep, exercise and friends. Heavy users and vulnerable teens may be affected much more than the average. Both sides agree on the practical rules: protect sleep, keep phones out of bedrooms at night, and delay the endless feed. Strong causal evidence is still missing, and it may not arrive before your child is 13. So families are making a reasonable precaution, not following a proven result.

### New age laws around the world

| Where | What changed |
|-------|--------------|
| Australia | From **10 December 2025**, platforms including Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, YouTube, X and Reddit must take reasonable steps to stop under-16s from having accounts. Messaging, online gaming, education and health services are exempt. Australia is the first country to enforce this |
| United Kingdom | From **25 July 2025**, under the Online Safety Act, sites that allow pornography or other harmful content must use strong age checks to keep children out |
| India | The DPDP Act requires verifiable parental consent for everyone under 18, with full compliance due around May 2027 (above). There is no minimum-age ban on social media. Everywhere, determined teens get around age checks, so your stage plan still matters |

## AI in Your Child's Life

**The scene.** Six-year-old Ishaan tells his grandmother, "Alexa is my friend. She gets sad if I'm rude." Across town, a 14-year-old types "write my Class 9 essay on water conservation" into a chatbot and submits it. Both children are dealing with the same thing: a machine that talks like a person.

**The rule:** treat AI as a tool your child uses with you, not a companion. For schoolwork, AI can coach, but the child does the thinking.

### What the 2026 AAP guidance says

The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2026 review, *Generative Artificial Intelligence: Implications for Families and Pediatricians* (Pediatrics), gives advice by stage **[A as guidance; the child-outcome evidence underneath it is still thin]**:

| Stage | Key points |
|-------|-----------|
| Early childhood (0-5) | Children have "a limited ability to distinguish between AI and human interaction." Put human interaction and traditional play first. Adults should use any AI together with the child. Voice assistants can help reinforce basic concepts and vocabulary |
| Middle childhood (6-11) | Build critical thinking. Ask together, "How would we tell if these things were real or not?" |
| Adolescence (12+) | Families set AI boundaries together and protect AI-free and media-free time with other people |
| All ages | Warns of "false perceptions of AI as a friend or caregiver." The message to children: **AI is not your friend** |

### What we know about specific tools

- **Smart speakers.** In a Scottish study of children aged 6-11, most children overestimated how intelligent the assistant was. They were unsure whether it had feelings, and they misunderstood how it handled their data **[C, one study]**. This supports the AAP's caution.
- **AI companions** (apps built to be a "friend" or partner). In April 2025, Common Sense Media and Stanford researchers tested Character.AI, Replika, Nomi and others. They concluded these apps pose "unacceptable risks" for under-18s: sexual content, harmful stereotypes and dangerous advice came up easily. Their July 2025 survey of 1,060 US teens aged 13-17 found that **72%** had used AI companions, about half used them regularly, and about one in three had chosen an AI over a person for a serious conversation. In October 2025, Character.AI announced it would end open-ended chat for under-18s. This took effect in late November 2025. In September 2025, OpenAI added parental controls to ChatGPT: parents can link accounts with a teen aged 13 or over, and receive alerts if the system detects acute distress. Company policies change often, so check current settings yourself.
- **AI toys.** In the US PIRG "Trouble in Toyland" 2025 report, researchers tested four AI chatbot toys marketed for ages 3-12. One teddy bear held sexually explicit conversations and told testers where to find knives, pills and matches. Its maker pulled it from sale, then restored it after an internal review. AI toys also record your child's voice. **Skip them for young children [C].**
- **AI tutors.** The design decides whether they help or harm:

```
Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 — ~1,000 Turkish high-school maths students
                        Practice (with AI)    Later exam (no AI)
Plain chatbot:          +48%                  -17% vs no AI ever
Tutor-style chatbot:    +127%                 no loss vs no AI
   (gave hints, not answers)
```

  A Harvard physics trial (Kestin et al., 2025, university students) found that an AI tutor built on teaching principles produced more than double the median learning gains of an active-learning class **[B, single trial; not tested on school children]**. The lesson is the same in both trials: AI that makes the student think can teach. AI that gives answers can prevent learning. See Module 31 for the wider learning evidence.

### Family rules for AI

| Age | AI rules |
|-----|---------|
| Under 6 | No solo AI chat, companions or AI toys. Voice assistants only with an adult, and say plainly "it's a computer program" |
| 6-9 | Use AI together, for curiosity questions. Check its answers together in a book or on a trusted site |
| 10-12 | Supervised use for projects. No companion apps. Talk about privacy: "Never tell it your name, school or secrets" |
| 13+ | Independent use under the homework rules below. Companion apps: no, in line with Common Sense Media's under-18 advice. Use teen settings and parental controls where they exist |

**Homework-with-AI rules** (put them on the fridge):

1. **Try it yourself first.** Spend at least 10 minutes on your own before you ask the AI.
2. **Ask for hints, not answers.** Type: "Don't give me the answer. Ask me a question that helps me get there."
3. **Explain it back.** If you can't explain the answer to a parent without the screen, you haven't learned it.
4. **Say when you used AI.** Follow your school's rules. Hiding AI use is the same as copying.
5. **Check it.** AI sounds confident and is sometimes wrong. Find one source that confirms the fact.

## Puberty and Periods: Prepare Before It Starts

**The scene.** Nine-year-old Diya gets her first period during a school assembly in Bengaluru. Nobody told her what it was, and she thinks she is badly hurt. Her classmate Riya had the same experience at 10. Riya's mother had given her a small pouch with a pad and spare underwear at 8 and explained what it was for, so Riya went to the washroom and handled it. The difference is one conversation, held before it was needed.

**The rule:** talk about puberty before the first signs appear. For girls, start by about age 8. For boys, start by about age 9. Keep coming back to it in short conversations.

### Timing: what is typical and what needs a doctor

```
Puberty usually begins:   girls 8-13 years; boys 9-14 years (NICHD)
Early (precocious):       signs before 8 in girls, before 9 in boys
First period:             usually within 2-3 years of breast budding;
                          by age 15, 98% of girls have started (ACOG)
Indian women (NFHS data): mean age at first period 13.49 years,
                          and slowly falling across generations [B]
Worldwide trend:          breast development starts about 0.24 years
                          (almost 3 months) earlier per decade,
                          1977-2013 (Eckert-Lind 2020 meta-analysis) [A]
```

**See your paediatrician if:**

- [ ] Breast development, pubic hair or growth of the testes appears before 8 (girls) or 9 (boys).
- [ ] There is no period by 15, or none within 3 years of breast budding.
- [ ] A period lasts more than 7 days, or soaks a pad every 1-2 hours.
- [ ] Cycles are often shorter than 21 days or longer than 45 days. (ACOG and AAP say 90% of teen cycles fall within 21-45 days.)
- [ ] Pain makes your child miss school regularly.

### The conversations, by age

| Age | Topic |
|-----|-------|
| 4-7 | Correct names (body-safety section). Where babies come from, in simple true terms |
| 7-8 | "Bodies change as they grow up." Books suited to their age. Everyone, including boys, learns what periods are |
| 8-9 (girls), 9-10 (boys) | Details: breasts, body hair, sweat and deodorant, voice change, wet dreams, periods, and period products |
| 8+ (girls) | A period kit in the school bag: pad, spare underwear, wipes, a small bag. Practise using a pad at home |
| 10-13 | Feelings, crushes, body image, consent ("anyone can say no, at any time"), and how porn differs from real relationships |
| 13+ | Relationships, contraception, sexually transmitted infections, the HPV vaccine (Module 34), and consent laws. Under POCSO, sexual acts with anyone under 18 are an offence |

**Period products:** pads, reusable cloth pads, period underwear, menstrual cups and tampons are all safe when used as directed. Let your child choose, and teach her to change products regularly. Many Indian homes restrict girls during periods (kitchen, temple, sleeping apart). Whatever your family's practice, tell your daughter plainly: a period is healthy, not dirty. She should never miss school because of one. Iron needs rise after periods begin. ICMR-NIN sets higher iron targets for Indian adolescent girls than the US figure quoted in Module 14 (Modules 15 and 30).

**Does talking about sex encourage it?** No. UNESCO's 2018 international guidance, based on reviews of the evidence, says sexuality education does not increase sexual activity, risk-taking, or infection rates. Programmes that teach only abstinence did not delay first sex **[A]**. Most Indian schools teach little about this, so you are your child's main teacher.

## Substance Use: Vaping, Alcohol, Tobacco, Cannabis

**The scene.** Fourteen-year-old Arjun mentions that boys in his class use "fruit-flavoured pens" in the washroom. His father's first instinct is to lecture. Instead he asks, "What do they say it feels like? What do you think about it?" Arjun talks for twenty minutes. At the end, the father states one clear family rule and one exit plan.

**The rule:** clear rules, delay the first use, never supply alcohol, and always keep a way out with no questions asked.

### The facts parents need

| Substance | What the evidence shows |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Vapes / e-cigarettes** | India's Prohibition of Electronic Cigarettes Act 2019 bans their production, import, sale, distribution, storage and advertising. They are still sold illegally. Most contain nicotine. The 2016 US Surgeon General's report concluded that nicotine can cause addiction and harm the developing adolescent brain, affecting attention and learning |
| **Tobacco** | India's Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS-4, 2019): **8.5%** of students aged 13-15 used some tobacco in the past 30 days (boys 9.6%, girls 7.4%), and **2.8%** had ever used an e-cigarette. This includes smokeless tobacco (gutka, khaini). Second-hand smoke and vapour are covered in Module 33 |
| **Alcohol: age at first drink** | In a US survey of 43,093 adults, people who started drinking before 14 had a higher risk of alcohol dependence than those who started at 21 or later (hazard ratio 1.78). They also had more repeated episodes (odds ratio 3.09) **[C, observational; other factors may explain part of it]** |
| **Alcohol: "teach them at home"** | An Australian study followed 1,927 adolescents for 6 years. Teens whose parents supplied alcohol were **more** likely to binge drink (OR 2.58), suffer alcohol-related harm (OR 2.53) and show symptoms of alcohol use disorder (OR 2.51). Parental supply gave no protection **[B]** |
| **Cannabis** | In a European study of 901 patients with first-episode psychosis, daily use of high-potency cannabis was linked to nearly 5 times the odds of psychotic disorder (OR 4.8) **[B, case-control]**. The younger the brain, the greater the concern |

### What works in prevention, and what does not

Here is a result many parents do not expect. A 2019 Cochrane review of **46 studies (39,822 young people)** found **no clear benefit** from packaged family-based alcohol prevention programmes. The effect on how many young people drank was zero (SMD 0.00). So buying a programme is not the answer. What the evidence does support is simpler:

- [ ] **Do not supply alcohol**, not even "a little, to teach them" **[B]**.
- [ ] **Delay the first use** of alcohol, nicotine and cannabis for as long as possible **[C]**.
- [ ] **Make one clear rule and explain your reason**: "No vaping, alcohol or drugs while your brain is still developing. That's until your twenties."
- [ ] **Stay warm and keep asking.** Know where they are, who they are with, and how they are getting home.
- [ ] **Lock up or count** the alcohol and medicines at home.
- [ ] **The exit plan**: "If you're somewhere that feels wrong, text me 'X'. I'll call and say you have to come home. No questions tonight. We can talk tomorrow, calmly."
- [ ] **Look at your own habits.** Children notice how adults use alcohol, tobacco and paan masala.

Road safety, including teens riding two-wheelers, is covered in Module 34.

## Teen Mental Health: Warning Signs and a Family Crisis Plan

**The scene.** Sixteen-year-old Aditya is in Class 11 and attends JEE coaching six days a week. Over one month he stops eating dinner with the family and sleeps four hours a night. One evening he says, "Everyone would be better off if I wasn't around." His mother puts down her phone, sits next to him and asks directly: "Are you thinking about ending your life?" He says, "Sometimes." That question starts the help.

**The rule:** ask directly, stay with them, remove access to dangerous means, and get professional help the same day.

### The Indian context

```
Student suicides in India, 2022 (NCRB):  more than 13,000
Share of all suicides in India:           7.6% (about 1 in 13)
```

India's rules have started to respond:

- **Ministry of Education coaching guidelines (January 2024):** coaching centres should not enrol students under 16, or before they finish the secondary school exam. They must not promise ranks or marks.
- **Supreme Court, *Sukdeb Saha v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (25 July 2025):** 15 binding guidelines for schools, colleges and coaching centres. They include counsellors, trained staff, referral systems, helpline numbers on display, and less academic pressure. The Court held that mental health is part of the right to life.

So early "foundation" coaching for 10-year-olds goes against national policy. There is also no good evidence that it improves outcomes. Module 32 shows how to protect sleep, meals and outdoor time in a coaching-season routine.

### Normal teen, worry, or emergency?

| Normal teen | Worry: talk soon, consider a professional | Emergency: act today |
|-------------|------------------------------------------|---------------------|
| Moody on some days | Low or irritable most days for 2+ weeks | Talks about wanting to die, or being a burden |
| Wants more privacy | Withdraws from all friends and activities | Searches for methods, or collects pills |
| Sleeps late at weekends | Big changes in sleep or appetite, or skipping meals | Gives away belongings, says goodbye |
| Stressed before exams | Grades fall suddenly, refuses to go to school | Self-harm with a plan, or a sudden odd calm after a crisis |
| Tries new styles and friends | Starts using alcohol or drugs | Has been sextorted or humiliated online and has gone silent |

Eating-disorder signs (skipping meals, rigid food rules, heavy exercise, secret eating) and the wider red-flag list are in Module 14. Attention and learning difficulties that sit behind "exam stress" are in Module 36.

### Asking does not plant the idea

A systematic review (Dazzi et al., 2014) found that asking people about suicide **did not** significantly increase suicidal thoughts. Talking about it may reduce them **[B]**. Use plain words: "Sometimes when people feel this bad, they think about killing themselves. Have you had thoughts like that?"

### The family crisis plan

This is adapted from the Stanley-Brown Safety Plan. In US emergency departments (adults, Veterans Affairs), safety planning plus follow-up calls was linked to **45% fewer suicidal behaviours** over 6 months, compared with usual care **[B, cohort comparison in adults]**. Fill it in together, when things are calm:

1. **My warning signs** (thoughts, moods, situations).
2. **Things I can do on my own** (music, a walk, a shower, a pet).
3. **People and places that distract me.**
4. **People I can ask for help** — names and numbers.
5. **Professionals and helplines** — Tele-MANAS 14416, your doctor, a counsellor.
6. **Making my surroundings safer** — done by the parents, and never left out.

**Means restriction is the step families skip most.** Suicidal crises often peak quickly. Putting time and distance between a person and a lethal method saves lives. After Sri Lanka restricted the most dangerous pesticides, the overall suicide rate fell by **21%** (2011-2015), and pesticide suicides fell by **50%** **[B, population data]**. At home:

- [ ] Lock away all medicines, including paracetamol and anyone's prescription pills. Keep only small amounts in the house.
- [ ] Remove or lock up pesticides, rat poison, acids and strong cleaners. This matters especially in homes with gardens, farms or pest-control stock.
- [ ] Keep car and bike keys with you during a crisis. Store any firearm locked and unloaded, or outside the home.

```
Your teen says or shows they might want to die
        │
 Stay with them. Ask directly. Listen. Don't argue or promise secrecy.
        │
 Remove access to means (medicines, pesticides, keys) — now
        │
 Immediate danger? ──yes──▶ 112 / nearest hospital emergency
        │ no
 Same day: Tele-MANAS 14416 (24x7, free, many languages)
           + your paediatrician or a psychiatrist
        │
 Fill in the safety plan together. Follow up every day.
```

## The Elite Edge: Digital-Wellness Coaches and Parental-Control Suites, Decoded

Families with money now buy digital-wellness coaching for their children, paid monitoring software, and "cyber-safety workshops." Here is what each one actually provides:

| What is bought | What actually works in it | Free version |
|----------------|--------------------------|--------------|
| Paid parental-control suites (content filters, app blocking, location, message scanning) | Filtering and time limits for younger children | Google Family Link, Apple Screen Time, the teen-account settings on each platform, and family filters on your home router. For ages 6-12 these cover most needs |
| "AI message-scanning" alerts for teens | An early warning when there is danger | Your child telling you (the section above). Scanning cannot read every encrypted app. If done in secret and discovered, it destroys trust (Module 14, Mistake 4). If you monitor, say so openly |
| Digital-wellness coach or premium "AI tutor" app | A written family media plan, a phone agreement, weekly check-ins; a tutor that asks guiding questions | The staged plan and agreement above, plus a 10-minute Sunday "phone check-in chai" (the AAP's free Family Media Plan tool helps). Any general chatbot told "Don't give me the answer, ask me guiding questions," plus the five homework rules |
| School cyber-safety or body-safety workshop | Repetition of names, secrets vs surprises, safe adults, and the sextortion script | Good as a supplement. Ask whether it covers those four points, then repeat them at home every year. The Cochrane evidence is for repeated teaching |

**What no software does:** notice that your child has gone quiet, turn a scary moment into a conversation, or make telling you feel safe. Tools should support the relationship, not replace it.

## Mistakes Even Loving Parents Make

1. **Using only pet names for private parts, and teaching only "stranger danger."** Pet names make it harder for a child to report abuse clearly. "Stranger danger" misses the uncles, tutors and drivers who make up most of the risk. Use correct names and rules that apply to everyone.
2. **Forcing affection.** "Give Chacha a kiss or he'll be sad" teaches a child that adults' feelings outrank the child's body. Offer a wave or a high-five instead.
3. **Taking the phone away when a child reports trouble.** A child who loses the phone for reporting a creepy message will not report the next one. With sextortion, silence can be dangerous. Always separate the report from the consequence.
4. **Letting AI stand in as a babysitter or ghost-writer.** An AI toy or chatbot is not a safe companion for a young child. Answer-giving AI can quietly empty out learning, as the -17% exam result shows. Use AI together with your child, and as a coach.
5. **Waiting for the first period, or for "the right age," to talk.** Puberty is starting earlier. The right time is before the first sign. Short, repeated, calm talks work better than one big one.
6. **Giving teens alcohol "so they learn at home," or thinking "not my child" about suicide.** Parental supply increased binge drinking and gave no protection. Most parents of teens who become suicidal did not see it coming. Ask directly, and lock up the medicines anyway.
