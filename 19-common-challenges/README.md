## Key Takeaways

- **Run the state check before you do anything else.** A large share of "behaviour problems" are a hungry, tired, or unwell child.
- Most problems have a home elsewhere in this guide. The table below gets you there in one hop.
- **Parental burnout is a real, measured thing** — not a character flaw, and not the same as ordinary tiredness. Four warning signs are below.
- Compare your child to your child last month. Nothing else is informative.
- **Ask for help sooner than feels justified.** The most common regret parents report is having waited.

## First: The State Check

Before you conclude anything about your child's behaviour, character, or development, check these.

### HALTS+

- **H**ungry
- **A**ngry or overwhelmed
- **L**onely or disconnected
- **T**ired
- **S**ick, or in pain — including teeth and ears
- **+** Constipated · over-stimulated · under-moved · mid-transition

**Three of these are worth pulling out**, because they're the most often missed:

**Sleep.** An under-slept child doesn't look sleepy. They look inattentive, impulsive, irritable, and difficult. Count backwards from the wake time — most families find bedtime is an hour later than they thought. See [Module 16](../16-sleep-and-development/README.md).

**Constipation.** It affects mood, sleep, appetite, concentration, and toilet learning at once, and almost nobody connects it. Worth ruling out whenever behaviour changes for no obvious reason.

**Connection.** Has this child had unhurried, undirected attention from you lately? Behaviour deteriorates when connection thins — and connection thins invisibly, during busy periods, without anyone deciding it should.

## The Things That Live Here

### Sibling conflict

**Don't adjudicate.** You didn't see it, and being the judge teaches both children to build cases against each other.

- Describe both sides, then hand it back: *"You both want the same thing. That's a real problem. Tell me when you've got a plan."*
- **Intervene absolutely for physical harm**, every time: *"Bodies are safe in this house."*
- Don't force sharing. Use turn-taking with a timer
- **Never compare them** — in either direction. "Why can't you be more like your sister" and "you're the sensible one" both do damage, and the second is the one people don't notice
- Don't make one child responsible for another's feelings

**The actual intervention:** ten minutes of one-on-one time with each child, separately, most days. Sibling rivalry is largely competition for a scarce resource. Make the resource less scarce and a surprising amount of it evaporates.

### The new baby

Regression is near-universal — sleep, toileting, babyish speech, clinginess. Step back a rung without comment or consequence and let them climb back up.

- Give the older child a real role, not a ceremonial one
- **Let them be angry about it.** "You wish she wasn't here" is a legitimate feeling and saying it out loud is much better than acting it out
- Protect one-to-one time fiercely, even in short bursts
- Don't blame the baby for things you were going to say no to anyway

### Comparison

The single most reliable source of unnecessary parental misery.

- **Compare your child to your child last month.** That's the only informative comparison there is
- Milestone ranges are far wider than charts suggest — a nine-month spread on walking alone, both ends normal. See [Module 00](../00-prerequisites/README.md#the-normal-range-is-enormous)
- What you see online is a highlight reel. Nobody posts the eight-month-old who isn't sitting
- **Early doesn't predict much.** A child who walks at 10 months and one who walks at 17 end up in the same place

### When your child's behaviour embarrasses you in public

The audience is the problem, not the child.

Get low, get quiet, ignore everyone else, and leave if you need to — calmly, without shaming them on the way out. Strangers' opinions are not an input to your child's development.

*"We're going to sit in the car until you feel better"* is a complete and adequate plan.

## Parental Burnout

This gets treated as a soft topic. It isn't — it's a measured construct with a validated instrument, developed and tested across thousands of parents ([Roskam, Raes & Mikolajczak, 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28232811)).

**It is not the same as ordinary tiredness**, and it isn't depression, though they can overlap.

### The four signs

The assessment used in this research measures four dimensions. Read them as a checklist:

| | What it feels like |
|---|---|
| **Exhaustion in your parental role** | Not general tiredness — specifically, being depleted *by parenting* |
| **Contrast with the parent you used to be** | "I'm not the parent I was. I don't recognise myself" |
| **Feeling fed up** | Being unable to stand it any more, not just finding it hard |
| **Emotional distancing** | Going through the motions. Doing the tasks without being present. **This is the one people notice last and the one that matters most** |

**If several of those land, that's information, not a verdict on you.**

### What actually helps

Not "self-care" in the bubble-bath sense.

- **Ask for practical help, not emotional support.** Someone to take the children for two hours beats someone telling you you're doing great. Be specific: name the task and the time
- **Lower standards deliberately and temporarily.** Pick the things that genuinely don't matter for the next month and stop doing them. Say so out loud, so it's a decision rather than a failure
- **Get one non-negotiable block of sleep**, even if it costs something else
- **Reduce the number of decisions.** Same meals, same routine, fewer options
- **Talk to your doctor.** If you're persistently irritable, numb, hopeless, or frightened by your own reactions, that's a medical conversation. Burnout and depression respond to treatment

### If you're frightened of your own anger

More parents experience this than admit it, and admitting it is the thing that keeps everyone safe.

**It is always safe to put your child down somewhere safe and walk away for five minutes.** Say it to yourself now, before you need it.

- With a baby: cot, close the door, set a timer. A crying baby in a safe cot is completely fine. **Never shake a baby** — see [Module 03](../03-newborn-0-3-months/README.md#read-this-first-when-the-crying-wont-stop)
- With an older child: "I'm too angry to talk well. I'm going to the kitchen for two minutes and then I'm coming back." Then actually come back — that's regulation and repair demonstrated in one move
- **If it's happening repeatedly and you can't stop it, get help.** Not because you're a bad parent, but because this is exactly what support exists for

## When to Get Professional Help

**Reach out — to your doctor, health visitor, or your child's school — if:**

- A concern **persists over time** rather than passing
- It shows up **across settings** — home *and* nursery or school
- **Your child loses a skill they had.** Always, at any age, no waiting
- Feeding, sleep, language, movement, or social engagement is significantly affected
- Family stress is becoming unmanageable
- **Your gut keeps returning to it** even after reasonable attempts to help

### Say it properly

Most parents leave appointments without saying the thing they came to say. Bring three things written down:

1. **What you've observed, specifically.** Not "she's behind" but: *"She's 20 months, has four words, doesn't point to show me things, and doesn't turn when I call her from across the room."*
2. **When you first noticed, and whether it's changing**
3. **The direct request:** *"I'd like her assessed. What's the process?"*

And if you're told to wait and see and you're still worried:

> "I hear you. I'd still like a referral so we're on the list. If she catches up, we can cancel it."

That is entirely reasonable, and no good clinician will object. Waiting lists are long. Getting on one early costs nothing and can be undone.

### Don't wait

Early support produces better outcomes than late support across essentially every developmental area. **The waiting itself is the risk.**

In the US, you can refer your own child to early intervention without a doctor, and the evaluation is free by law. See [Module 00](../00-prerequisites/README.md#early-help-works--and-some-of-it-is-free).

## Two Things Worth Believing

**Most of what feels like failure is development.** Tantrums, refusal, clinginess, regression, and rudeness are all stage-appropriate at some point. They usually mean the stage arrived, not that you got something wrong.

**Repair matters more than getting it right.** You will shout, be unfair, and get it wrong. Every parent does. What predicts a secure child is not the absence of rupture — it's the presence of repair. See [Module 21, Part 5](../21-building-eq/README.md#part-5-repair--the-most-underrated-skill).

## 2026 Research Update

> Added in the 2026 research refresh. Grades in brackets rate the evidence: **[A]** strong · **[B]** good · **[C]** mixed or observational · **[D]** early or weak.

### Evidence Box: Smacking and Time-Out

**Smacking (physical punishment).** No good study shows it helps. Researchers still argue about how much it harms.
- A 2016 meta-analysis (Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor; 111 effect sizes, about 161,000 children) found 13 of 17 outcomes significantly linked with spanking. Every one pointed to harm, and none to benefit **[A]**.
- A 2021 *Lancet* review of 69 long-term studies found that physical punishment predicted more behaviour problems over time and was not linked with any positive outcome **[A]**.
- Critics (Larzelere 2024) argue the effects are small once a child's earlier behaviour is counted **[B]**. Even on that reading, smacking adds risk and no benefit.
- The AAP (2018) advises against spanking, hitting, slapping, threatening, insulting, humiliating, or shaming. It recommends praise for wanted behaviour, clear limits, and calm consequences instead. In India, the Right to Education Act (2009) bans physical punishment in schools.

**Time-out.** Done well, time-out is a short, calm, boring pause with the parent nearby, followed by reconnection. It is not banishment. It is a standard part of the parenting programs with the strongest trials (see the Elite Edge below).
- In US Early Head Start data, parents' use of time-out at age 3 was not linked with emotional or behaviour problems up to fifth grade **[C]**.
- An Australian survey of 474 families found that time-out done well went with better child mental health and attachment, while harsh or inconsistent time-out went with worse **[C]**. Its authors co-developed a program that uses time-out.
- If time-out turns into yelling, shaming, or long isolation, it stops helping. For a child who panics when alone, sit close by instead ("time-in").

If a pattern looks like more than a phase (speech that stays behind, high activity and impulsivity in every setting, reading that will not click, or a child far ahead of age peers), Module 36 explains autism, ADHD, dyslexia, speech and language delay, and giftedness, and how assessment works.

## A Day in Real Life: Solving "Bath Time Hell" Like a Behavior Analyst

Every night for three weeks, bath time has been a war. The parents have tried bribes, sternness, skipping baths, and a new toy boat. Nothing works, because nobody knows what the problem *is*. So they borrow the professional's most basic tool — a one-week **ABC log** (Antecedent → Behavior → Consequence): three columns, filled in after each bath, 60 seconds a night.

```
Day | What happened BEFORE      | Behavior          | What happened AFTER
----|---------------------------|-------------------|--------------------
Mon | Tablet ended → straight   | Screaming at door | Bath delayed 20 min
    | to bathroom               |                   | (more cartoon won)
Tue | Same                      | Same              | Same
Wed | Played blocks before bath | Mild protest only | Bath fine
Thu | Tablet → bathroom         | Full meltdown     | Dad did bath angry
Fri | Blocks → bath             | No protest        | Fine
```

The log convicts nobody's character and one transition: **tablet directly to bath** is the trigger (hardest possible switch: high-stimulation to demand), and Monday's consequence — screaming earned more cartoon — has been quietly *paying* the behavior. The fix writes itself: screens end 30 minutes before bath with blocks in between, and screaming never again purchases screen time. Bath war over in four days.

**The transferable lesson:** you cannot fix a pattern you haven't seen. One week of three-column honesty outperforms a month of theories about whether the child is "being difficult."

## Tips & Tricks: Troubleshooting Any Challenge

1. **Fix one battle at a time.** Families who overhaul bedtime, meals, and screens simultaneously fix nothing — enforcement attention is finite. Triage: pick the battle costing the most family goodwill, fix it fully (2–3 weeks), then take the next. Sequential wins compound; parallel campaigns collapse.
2. **Audit your ratio.** Relationship research (Gottman) popularized the 5:1 ratio — five positive interactions per negative one — from studies of couples, not parents and children. No study has tested that exact number in parenting, so use it as a rule of thumb: warmth should clearly outweigh correction. A hard phase quietly drives the ratio to 1:1 (all corrections, no play). Before adding discipline, restore deposits: ten minutes of child-led special time (Module 08's PRIDE) moves more behavior than any new consequence.
3. **Write your three scripts down.** For your current top challenge, literally script it: the warning line, the limit line, the empathy line. Scripted parents stay calm because they're not composing under fire — the same reason pilots use checklists.
4. **Agree on a tag-out signal.** With a partner: a word or gesture meaning "I'm about to lose it — take over." Swapping the adult mid-conflict isn't weakness; it's the co-regulation the child needs arriving from a fresher source.
5. **Correlate behavior with sleep before psychology.** Two weeks of "sudden defiance" is, remarkably often, two weeks of a 40-minutes-later bedtime. Check the boring ledgers (sleep, food timing, schedule chaos, new stressors) before consulting the deep theories.
6. **Repair out loud after your own bad moments.** "I yelled. That was too big. I'm sorry — I was frustrated about the mess, and I'll ask calmly next time." You cannot model never-failing; you can model repairing — which is the actually usable life skill, and it's learned by watching you.
7. **Say "yet," and mean the phase.** "He doesn't sleep through *yet*." "She can't share *yet*." Every challenge in this module is version-specific software; most uninstalls itself with development plus your consistency. "Always" and "never" are the vocabulary of burnout, not of accuracy.

## The Elite Edge: What the Child Psychologist Would Actually Do First

Affluent families book private child psychologists ($150–300/session) for garden-variety challenges — and for run-of-the-mill tantrums, bedtime battles, and defiance, the first-line playbook is remarkably standard, public, and mostly runnable at home:

1. **Track before treating** — the ABC log above; often the "assessment phase" parents pay four sessions for.
2. **Differential attention** — enthusiastic, specific attention to wanted behavior; flat, minimal response to attention-seeking misbehavior (safety excepted). Attention is toddler currency; audit what you're paying for.
3. **Special time** — the daily 5–10 minutes of child-led PRIDE play (Module 08). Prescribed in virtually every evidence-based program because it works.
4. **Consequence hygiene** — few, small, instant, boring, utterly predictable. A calm 2-minute consequence delivered every time beats a dramatic one delivered sometimes (the slot-machine rule from Module 08 again).

**The named programs worth knowing:** the interventions with strong trial evidence — *Incredible Years* and PCIT, plus the widely used *Triple P (Positive Parenting Program)*, whose evidence is more disputed (an independent 2012 review found most of its trials were run by its developers, with a high risk of bias) — are structured versions of exactly these tools. Group and self-directed versions exist at a fraction of private rates, and several health systems offer Triple P free online. The research finding that should reassure you: parent-delivered versions of these programs work — the parent, not the professional's office, is the active ingredient.

**When to genuinely pay:** when a pattern is intense, persists months despite consistent use of the tools above, shows up across settings (home *and* school), involves regression, self-harm, or safety — or when *your* mood, anxiety, or conflict as parents is the stuck part. Early professional help for the right problem is money brilliantly spent; the skill is knowing it's the second line, not the first.

## Mistakes Even Loving Parents Make

1. **Strategy-hopping every 48 hours.** Most behavior methods show results in 1–3 *weeks*, and many trigger an "extinction burst" — behavior gets briefly *worse* when it stops paying out (the child escalates the old strategy before abandoning it). Families who quit during the burst accidentally teach that escalation works. Pick a sound approach, hold it for two honest weeks.
2. **Consequence inflation.** "No TV for a month!" declared at peak anger is unenforceable, so it gets quietly rolled back, teaching that consequences are negotiable theater. Small and certain beats large and hollow, every time.
3. **Case-conferencing the child in their hearing.** Discussing "the biting problem" or "how bad this week was" over the child's head installs the identity you're trying to prevent. Children become the stories told about them; tell those stories after bedtime.
4. **Treating the phase as the personality.** The 2-year-old who bites is not "aggressive"; the 4-year-old who lies about the marker on the wall is not "dishonest" (lying at 4 is a theory-of-mind milestone, honestly). Label the behavior's stage, not the child's character.
5. **Running yourself to empty and calling it devotion.** The burnout section above is not decoration: a depleted parent cannot execute *any* strategy in this guide, because every strategy runs on adult regulation. Sleep, help, and lowered temporary standards aren't self-indulgence — they're the power supply.

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Take your single worst recurring moment and write down its pattern.**

Three columns: **what happened just before · what happened · what helped, if anything.**

Do it for a week. Don't change anything yet.

Almost everyone finds the same thing: it clusters. 5pm. Hungry. After a screen. At transitions. In shops. On Sunday nights.

**Then fix the pattern, not the moment.** That's a completely different and much more winnable problem — and you can't see the pattern until you've written it down, which is why the week of just watching is the whole technique.
