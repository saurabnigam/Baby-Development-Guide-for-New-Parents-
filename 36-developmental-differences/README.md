# Module 36: When Development Needs a Closer Look — Autism, ADHD, Dyslexia, Speech, and Giftedness

> Time: 3 hours | Difficulty: Intermediate | For: every parent — read once in the first year, return the day a teacher, grandparent, or your own gut raises a flag

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Screening is cheap; waiting is expensive.** A 20-item questionnaire costs you ten minutes. A missed year of early help is a year of the fastest-growing period used without support (judgment). A positive screen is a reason to look closer. It is not a diagnosis.
- **Follow a schedule, not a mood.** Developmental checks at every visit, a formal screen at 9–12, 18–24 months and school entry, and an autism screen (M-CHAT-R/F) at 18 and 24 months. India has its own validated tools and a free public pathway (RBSK and District Early Intervention Centres).
- **Autism help can start before the label.** Indian and US guidelines both say intervention should begin as soon as a concern is clear. One well-supported approach is coaching parents in everyday interaction. A UK trial found lower symptom severity about six years later, and a version has been tried in India. More hours is not proven to be better.
- **Vaccines do not cause autism. The 2025 paracetamol (Tylenol) headlines were not backed by the strongest studies.** Sibling-comparison studies of millions of children find no link.
- **ADHD, dyslexia and speech delay have clear referral points and first-line treatments.** Parent training comes before medicine for 4–5-year-olds. Omega-3 capsules do not treat ADHD. Phonics-based teaching is the dyslexia treatment with the best trial evidence.
- **"Gifted" and "struggling" can live in the same child.** Twice-exceptional children get missed in both directions. A certified disability in India (autism, specific learning disability, or a permanent speech-language disability) unlocks legal rights under the RPwD Act 2016, including board-exam accommodations. A typical toddler speech delay is not a disability under the Act. The Act's speech and language disability means a permanent condition with an organic or neurological cause, such as aphasia, not ordinary speech delay.

## The Principle: Screening Is Cheap, Waiting Is Expensive

Meera is 20 months old and lives in Bengaluru. She has four words. She loves spinning the wheels of her toy bus. She does not look up much when her name is called. Her grandmother says, "Her father talked late too. Einstein talked late. Don't make her into a patient." Her father is not sure. The pediatrician is booked for three weeks.

Here is the honest arithmetic. Meera's parents can fill in a free 20-question autism screen tonight in about ten minutes. It is the M-CHAT-R/F, free at mchatscreen.com, meant for children aged 16–30 months; they answer the questions, then take the form to their pediatrician, who scores it and asks the follow-up questions. The official site also has versions in several Indian languages. If the screen is clear, they have lost ten minutes. But a clear screen does not settle the question if the worries continue: in a large US primary-care network that screened 25,999 toddlers and followed them for years, the M-CHAT with follow-up caught only 38.8% of the children later diagnosed with autism (Guthrie et al. 2019) [B]. So they still tell the pediatrician what they have seen. If it is not clear, a follow-up interview sorts out most false alarms. If a concern remains, a full evaluation follows, and help can start **before** any final label. The Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) says plainly that "a definitive diagnosis is not necessary for commencing intervention" [A].

The cost of waiting runs the other way. The years from birth to five are when language, social attention and learning habits build fastest (Module 01). A "wait and see" year is a year of that window used without support.

Three rules follow:

1. **A flag is not a diagnosis.** Screeners are built to catch as many children as possible, so they also catch children who turn out fine. In the main US validation study of 16,071 toddlers, children who stayed positive after the follow-up interview had a 47.5% chance of an autism diagnosis, and a 94.6% chance of some developmental delay or concern [B]. In everyday practice the autism figure is lower: in a US network that screened 25,999 toddlers, 14.6% of screen-positive children were later diagnosed with autism (Guthrie et al. 2019) [B]. So a positive screen almost always means "this child needs something", but it often does not mean autism.
2. **A clear screen is not a guarantee.** If you are still worried, ask for an evaluation anyway. The M-CHAT's own instructions say a parent's concern is reason enough, whatever the score. Otherwise, screen again at the next checkpoint (Module 02, Mistake 4).
3. **You are the data source.** Specialists see your child for an hour. You see the whole week. Write down what you see, with dates and short videos.

```
 You notice something ──► Screen (free, 10-15 min) ──► Clear? ──► Re-screen at next checkpoint
                                   │
                                   ▼ Not clear
                         Follow-up interview / pediatrician review
                                   │
                                   ▼ Still a concern
       ┌───────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                       ▼
 Start help NOW                                     Full evaluation
 (parent coaching, speech therapy,                  (developmental pediatrician,
  hearing & vision checks)                           psychologist, therapists)
```

## The Screening Schedule: Which Tool, Which Age

Two words first. **Surveillance** is the doctor asking about and watching development at every visit. **Screening** is a short, validated questionnaire or test given at set ages. You want both.

| Age | What should happen | Tools you may hear named | Source |

| 9 months (IAP: 9–12) | Formal developmental screen | ASQ-3, Trivandrum Developmental Screening Chart (TDSC) | AAP; IAP 2022 |
| 18 months | Developmental screen **and** autism screen | ASQ-3 or TDSC; **M-CHAT-R/F** | AAP; IAP 2022 |
| 24 months | Autism screen | **M-CHAT-R/F** | AAP |

**What each tool is:**

- **ASQ-3 (Ages & Stages Questionnaires).** A parent-completed questionnaire covering communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem solving and personal-social skills. It spans 1–66 months with 21 age-specific forms, takes parents 10–15 minutes, and takes a professional 1–3 minutes to score. It is a paid product, so ask whether your clinic uses it (Module 02).
- **M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised, with Follow-Up).** Twenty yes/no questions for parents, free to download for clinical use. Score 0–2: low risk (if under 24 months, screen again after the second birthday). Score 3–7: the clinician asks structured follow-up questions; if the score stays at 2 or more, refer. Score 8–20: refer straight away for evaluation and early intervention.
- **Trivandrum Developmental Screening Chart (TDSC).** Built in Kerala in 1991 from Indian norms. The original 17-item chart for under-2s had 66.7% sensitivity and 78.8% specificity against the Denver Developmental Screening Test, itself a screening tool [B]. A 51-item version for ages 0–6, tested in 1,183 children, reported 84.62% sensitivity and 90.8% specificity [B]. A Hindi version was validated in 2025. It is quick and suited to community health workers. (*Sensitivity* is the share of children with a real delay that the tool catches. *Specificity* is the share of children without a delay that it correctly clears.)
- **CDC milestone checklists (revised 2022).** Free, with photos and videos.

### What changed in the CDC milestones in 2022

The old checklists were mostly based on clinical opinion, and many milestone lists give the average age, when only about **half** of children have the skill. That meant a child missing one was often normal, and parents and doctors learned to shrug. The 2022 revision (Zubler et al., with the AAP) lists skills that **75% or more** of children reach by each visit age [A]. The panel also:

- added checklists at **15 and 30 months**, so every well-child visit from 2 months to 5 years has one;
- cut the number of milestones by 26.4% and replaced 40.9% of the old ones;
- moved one third of the kept milestones to a different age, and 67.7% of those moved to an **older** age.

**What this means for you:** under the new lists, missing a milestone is more meaningful than before. The flip side: many milestones moved to older ages, so a child can pass a checklist and still be behind. Speech-language experts have criticised the revision. A 2023 review found the evidence for its vocabulary milestones 'conflicting, misaligned, or missing' (Roberts et al., *JSLHR*) [C]. Treat the checklist as a floor, and also use the speech red flags later in this module. The authors' advice is that a child missing any milestone should be considered for screening, instead of "wait and see". If your child misses one, ask for a screen. Do not wait for the next birthday. This rule is for the CDC checklist items, which three in four children can already do at that age. Module 02's ranges show when children usually start a skill, and being late inside a range is still normal. Missing a CDC item is not an emergency: ask for a screen at the next visit, or book one in the next few weeks.

**Before any developmental test, check hearing and vision.** The IAP guideline on developmental delay says both should be screened first [A]. A child who cannot hear well will look "delayed" in language and "inattentive" in class.

## Autism: Signs, Early Help, Myths, and the Indian Pathway

### The scene

Back to Meera. At 20 months she rarely points to show her parents a bird. She brings her father's hand to the fridge instead of looking at him and asking. She lines up her bus, cars and spoons. None of these alone proves anything. Together, and persisting, they are exactly what the screen is designed to catch.

**Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)** is a difference in brain development that affects social communication and includes restricted or repetitive behaviours and interests. It is a spectrum: some autistic people need daily support all their lives; others are highly verbal and academically strong. The AAP says autism can be diagnosed as young as 18 months [A].

### How common is it?

```
US, 8-year-olds, 2022 surveillance year (CDC):   about 1 in 31  (3.2%)
India, 2-9-year-olds, 5 regions (Arora 2018):    any neurodevelopmental disorder
                                                  9.2% at ages 2 to <6
                                                  13.6% at ages 6-9
```

The Indian study counted seven conditions in children under 6: vision impairment, hearing impairment, epilepsy, neuromotor impairment including cerebral palsy, speech-language disorders, autism, and intellectual disability. For children aged 6–9 it counted nine, adding ADHD and learning disorders. About one in five affected children had more than one [B].

### Early signs by age (CDC)

| By this age | Watch for (social communication) |
|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 9 months | Does not respond to name; few facial expressions (happy, sad, angry, surprised) |. Move 'Avoids or does not keep eye contact' into the 'Any age' row.
| 12 months | Does not play simple back-and-forth games like pat-a-cake; few or no gestures (no waving bye-bye) |
| 15 months | Does not share interests with you |
| 18 months | Does not point to show you something interesting |
| 24 months | Does not notice when others are hurt or upset |
| 3 years | Does not notice other children or join them in play |
| 4 years | Does not pretend to be someone else (teacher, superhero) in play |
| 5 years | Does not sing, dance, or act for you |
| **Any age** | **Loss of words, babble, or social skills the child once had. Act the same week.** |

Also watch for repetitive play (lining up, spinning), strong upset at small changes, and unusual reactions to sounds, textures or lights. (Your age modules list these as red flags; this table gathers them.)

### Girls are missed more often

Many people believe autism is four times as common in boys. A 2017 meta-analysis of 54 studies and more than 13 million people found an overall ratio of 4.20 to 1, but **3.32 to 1 in the higher-quality studies**. When researchers screened whole populations, not only children already diagnosed, the ratio was 3.25 to 1 [A]. The authors concluded that girls who meet the criteria are more likely to go undiagnosed. Clinicians report that girls may copy social behaviour, or "mask", and that their intense interests (animals, books, a favourite show) can look ordinary. Research on this is still developing. If you have a daughter who is struggling socially, do not let "girls don't get autism" close the question.

### What early intervention involves, and what the evidence shows

Good early autism intervention looks less like a clinic and more like coached play. A therapist watches you play with your child, often on video, and helps you notice your child's small signals, wait for them, and respond in a way that builds back-and-forth communication. The child learns in daily routines: bath, meals, the walk to the shop.

An effect size shows how big a difference is: about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.

| Study | What was tested | Result | Grade |
|-------|-----------------|--------|-------|
| **PACT** (UK; Pickles et al., Lancet 2016) | Parent-mediated social communication therapy, children aged 2–4, 152 children | About 6 years after treatment ended (mean age 10.5), the treated group had lower autism symptom severity over the whole period (effect size 0.55). **No difference in language** at follow-up. | [B] one trial with long follow-up |
| **PASS** (Goa, India and Rawalpindi, Pakistan; Rahman et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2016) | PACT adapted for South Asia: 12 sessions delivered by **non-specialist health workers**, 65 children aged 2–9 | Better parent-child synchrony (effect size 1.61) and more communication started by the child (0.99). Time in shared attention went **down** (−0.70), an unexpected result. | [B] small trial; feasibility shown |
| **Intervention amount** (Sandbank et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2024) | 144 studies, 9,038 autistic children aged 8 or under | No link between more hours (per day, per week, or total) and bigger gains. The authors found "little robust evidence" for the common 20–40 hours a week recommendation. | [B] meta-regression of weak studies |

**What this means for you:**

1. Start early and start with the parents. A coached parent delivers "therapy" for many hours a week at no extra cost.
2. It can be delivered in India by trained non-specialists. A small trial in Goa and Rawalpindi (65 children) improved parent-child interaction, but it did not show a change in autism symptoms. You do not need a famous clinic to begin.
3. Judge a programme by its quality and fit, not its hours. A 40-hour-a-week package is not proven to beat a well-run shorter one, and it can crowd out sleep, play and family life.
4. Treat the other problems too. The AAP lists sleep and feeding problems, seizures, ADHD and anxiety as common alongside autism, and each one affects daily life [A].

The IAP's 2017 consensus says intervention should begin as early as possible, target social communication and repetitive behaviours, be structured and evidence-based, and be run by an interdisciplinary team coordinated by the pediatrician [A].

### Myths that still circulate in family WhatsApp groups

| Claim | What the strongest evidence shows | Grade |
|-------|-----------------------------------|-------|
| "The MMR vaccine causes autism." | A Danish study of 657,461 children found a hazard ratio of 0.93 (95% CI 0.85–1.02) for vaccinated vs unvaccinated children: no increased risk, including in children with an autistic sibling (Hviid 2019). Keep to the vaccine schedule (Module 34). | [A] |
| "Paracetamol (Tylenol) in pregnancy causes autism." (US government announcement, 22 September 2025) | Studies that compare **siblings** (one exposed in the womb, one not) control for family genes and circumstances. A Swedish study of 2,480,797 children found sibling-comparison hazard ratios of 0.98 for autism and 0.98 for ADHD. A Hong Kong sibling study (JAMA Internal Medicine, September 2026; sibling samples of 124,333 and 97,285 children) found 1.00 and 1.01. Pooled studies without sibling control do show a small association (one 2025 meta-analysis: autism odds ratio 1.17). Family factors, and the illnesses that made mothers need the medicine, may explain it. The US FDA's own notice acknowledged "conflicting literature". As of September 2026, reports indicated that the US label had not been changed and that the process was still pending [D]. Check for updates. ACOG continues to regard paracetamol as appropriate in pregnancy. Untreated fever in pregnancy has its own risks. Medicine decisions in pregnancy belong with your obstetrician (Module 25). | [C] on the association; strongest designs are null |
| "Leucovorin (folinic acid) treats autism." | The AAP says current evidence is insufficient to prescribe it for autism unless the child has cerebral folate deficiency, a rare condition. In March 2026 the US FDA approved leucovorin only for cerebral folate deficiency and said the evidence does not support using it for autism. All studies so far have fewer than 100 patients, and there is no FDA-cleared test for the antibodies some clinics sell tests for. | [D] |
| "My gestational diabetes caused this." | Pooled studies show an association between maternal diabetes and autism or ADHD. But a study of 3.6 million mother-child pairs used sibling comparisons, and the gestational diabetes–ADHD link disappeared within families (hazard ratio 1.05, not significant). The authors called it "unlikely to be causal". That study looked at ADHD only. No sibling comparison for autism exists yet. One 2026 hospital cohort of 115,063 deliveries found no gestational diabetes–autism link after adjusting for other factors [C]. Pre-existing diabetes kept a stronger, unresolved association. | [C] |
| "Stem cells can cure autism." | No. Stem-cell use for autism is experimental and allowed in India only inside approved clinical trials (ICMR 2017 guidelines); the Supreme Court of India ruled in January 2026 that offering it outside a trial is malpractice. | [A] |
| "Bad parenting caused this." | Autism has strong genetic roots and begins developing before birth. Parenting shapes how well an autistic child is supported, not whether they are autistic. | Judgment |

A **hazard ratio** or **odds ratio** of 1.00 means no difference in risk. Numbers below 1 mean lower risk; above 1, higher.

### The Indian pathway for autism

- **Your pediatrician** does surveillance and the M-CHAT-R/F. Ask for it by name at 18 and 24 months.
- **RBSK (Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram)**, the national child health screening programme, screens children from birth to 18 years for the "4 Ds": Defects at birth, Diseases, Deficiencies, and Developmental delays including disabilities. It covers 32 conditions, with free treatment and management. Block-level mobile health teams visit Anganwadis and schools.
- **District Early Intervention Centres (DEICs)** sit at district hospitals and focus on children from birth to 6 years. The 2014 national guidelines staff them with a pediatrician, medical officer, dentist, physiotherapist, audiologist and speech therapist, psychologist, optometrist, and an early interventionist/special educator. Parents can refer their own child; you do not need a school or RBSK referral.
- **Indian diagnostic tools** you may see on reports: **INDT-ASD** (the INCLEN tool; in its validation study of 154 children at tertiary centres it showed 98% sensitivity and 95% specificity against expert diagnosis [B]) and **ISAA** (Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism), which DEIC guidelines list for autism assessment.
- **Developmental pediatricians** and child development centres at medical colleges (for example NIMHANS in Bengaluru and the Child Development Centre in Thiruvananthapuram) handle complex cases.
- **Certification.** Autism is one of the 21 disabilities in the RPwD Act 2016. A certificate and a UDID card (see the assessment section) unlock school and exam rights.

**Global equivalents:** in the US, Early Intervention (IDEA Part C) evaluates children under 3, and public school systems evaluate from age 3, free and without a doctor's referral (Module 00).

## ADHD Versus Normal Variation in Attention

### The scene

Arjun is 4 and a half. His preschool teacher sends a note: he cannot sit through circle time, grabs toys, and runs off during the line to the bus. At home he can build Lego for 40 minutes. His parents wonder whether this is ADHD, a boy being a boy, or the school expecting too much.

Some of it is age. Four-year-olds have short attention spans, and executive function is still under construction (Module 28). The questions that separate ADHD from normal variation are about **how much, how long, how many places, and how much harm**.

| Usually normal variation | Worth an evaluation |
|--------------------------|---------------------|
| Restless in one setting (only at school, or only at home) | Problems in **two or more settings** (home and school, or school and tuition) |
| Short attention for disliked tasks, but manages ordinary tasks (getting dressed, a short worksheet) with a reminder | Trouble sustaining attention even with support, across most tasks |
| Recent change: new sibling, move, new school, illness | Pattern lasting **at least 6 months** |
| Behaviour settles with sleep, routine and clear limits | Behaviour causes real harm: injuries, falling behind, losing friends, constant conflict |
| Youngest in the class and behaving like his age | Behaviour well outside what is usual for the child's actual age |

Long focus on a favourite activity does not rule ADHD out. Many children with ADHD can focus for hours on Lego or screens. Watch the everyday tasks they do not choose.

These columns track the DSM-5 criteria: six or more symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (five from age 17), lasting at least 6 months, some present before age 12, seen in two or more settings, and clearly interfering with daily life.

### Look-alikes to rule out first

- **Poor sleep.** The AAP asks clinicians to screen for sleep apnoea [A]. Snoring, mouth-breathing and restless sleep can look like hyperactivity by day (Module 16).
- **Hearing or vision problems.** The CDC lists hearing and vision tests as part of the ADHD evaluation.
- **A learning disability, a language disorder, autism, or anxiety.** These often co-exist with ADHD and can also mimic it. The AAP guideline tells clinicians to screen for all of them [A].
- **Being the youngest in the class.** A US insurance-claims study of 407,846 children compared August-born and September-born children (about 36,000 in each group) in states where the school-entry cutoff is 1 September. The August-born children, nearly a year younger than some classmates, were diagnosed more often [B]:

```
ADHD diagnosis per 10,000 children:  August-born 85.1   vs   September-born 63.6
ADHD treatment per 10,000 children:  August-born 52.9   vs   September-born 40.4
```

If your child is among the youngest in the class (common in India when parents push early admission), ask whether the behaviour is unusual for the child's **age**, not the class's average age.

### What the guidelines say

The **AAP 2019 guideline** [A]:

- Evaluate any child from **age 4 to the 18th birthday** with academic or behaviour problems plus inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity.
- **Ages 4–5 (preschool):** first-line treatment is evidence-based **parent training in behaviour management** (PTBM) and/or behavioural classroom support. Methylphenidate may be considered only if these do not help enough and the child's functioning is still moderately to severely disturbed.
- **Ages 6–12:** approved ADHD medicines **together with** parent training and/or classroom behavioural support, preferably both.
- **Ages 12–18:** medicines with the teen's agreement, plus behavioural or training support.
- **At every age:** educational supports (class placement, instructional support, behavioural supports at school) are a necessary part of treatment.

The **IAP 2017 consensus** [A] agrees: educate the family and teachers, set target outcomes together with the child, parents and school, and treat co-existing conditions at the same time. Behavioural, medical and educational treatments can be used alone or together.

**Medication is a decision for you and your child's doctor.** This book does not advise on it.

How common is it? A pooled estimate across 41 studies in 27 countries (Polanczyk 2015):

```
ADHD, worldwide:   3.4%
```

### The omega-3 question

Omega-3 "focus" supplements are heavily marketed to Indian parents. A 2023 Cochrane review (37 trials) found **high-certainty evidence of no effect** on parent-rated ADHD symptoms in the medium term (standardised mean difference −0.08; 95% CI −0.24 to 0.07; 16 studies, 1,166 participants) [A]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 16 trials found a small, borderline effect, mostly on objective or cognitive-performance measures rather than on behaviour ratings. A 2024 umbrella review was more hopeful about EPA as an add-on, but it relied on weaker studies. Bottom line: omega-3 is not an ADHD treatment. Fish in the diet still matters for other reasons (Modules 15 and 30).

## Speech and Language Delay

### The scene

Close the scene: "Kabir has about 18 words across both languages at his second birthday. The usual late-talker line is fewer than 50 words and no two-word phrases by 24 months (ASHA). So his parents book a hearing test and a speech-language evaluation now. They use Module 26's toolkit every day while they wait, and they keep speaking Kannada." Add "fewer than 50 words (all languages counted) at 24 months" to the first row of the referral table. Cite https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/late-language-emergence/ in Research Notes. The neighbour's daughter, same age, "speaks full sentences in English". His parents panic.

**Rule 1: count across languages.** A bilingual child's vocabulary is split. Add both lists (minus the words that mean the same thing) before comparing. Bilingualism does not cause language delay (Module 20 FAQ, Module 26). Do not drop the home language on anyone's advice.

**Rule 2: understanding and gestures matter as much as talking.** A meta-analysis of 20 samples (2,134 late-talking toddlers) found three factors that predicted how well late talkers later caught up in speaking: the size of their spoken vocabulary as toddlers, how much they **understood**, and family socioeconomic status [B]. Sex, family history and whether they used phrases did not predict it. A late talker who understands a lot, points, gestures and engages is in a much better position than one who does neither.

### When to refer (and when not to wait)

| Sign | Action |
|------|--------|
| No babbling by 9–10 months; no words by 16 months; no two-word phrases by 24 months; strangers cannot understand the child at 4 (Module 26) | Speech-language evaluation **and** a formal hearing test |
| Poor understanding of simple words or instructions, or few gestures, at any age | Evaluate now; these predict persistent delay |
| Any loss of words or babble | Same week: pediatrician, hearing test, autism screen |
| Delay in **both** languages of a bilingual child | Evaluate; this is not a bilingualism effect |
| Frequent ear infections or "glue ear" with a speech concern | Hearing test first |

A hearing test comes first for any speech delay. Ask for a formal hearing assessment by an audiologist, not a clap test. For toddlers this is usually OAE and/or BERA (also called ABR); for older children, play or pure-tone audiometry. Module 34 covers re-testing after ear infections.

### Stuttering

Stuttering is common in preschoolers. In an Australian community study of 1,619 children, **11.2%** had started stuttering by age 4 [B]. The same study found that children who stuttered had, on average, **stronger** language and nonverbal skills than those who did not. Only 6.3% had recovered within 12 months of onset, so most children are still stuttering a year later [B]. The same study found that in that first year, stuttering was not linked to worse language, temperament or quality of life. That is reassuring about the child. It is not a reason to wait. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) estimates that most children recover over time, and reports that about 95% of children who stutter start before age 4, with average onset around 33 months.

**Refer to a speech-language pathologist** if the stuttering has lasted more than about 6 months, or sooner if any of these apply (ASHA risk factors for stuttering that persists):

- [ ] Family history of stuttering that persisted
- [ ] Onset at age 3½ or later
- [ ] A boy (boys persist more often)
- [ ] Slower language development or other speech concerns
- [ ] Visible tension, facial grimaces, eye-blinking, or avoiding words
- [ ] The child is upset or aware, or you are worried

Module 26's referral list uses the same 6-month line.

## Dyslexia and Specific Learning Disabilities

### The scene

Ananya is 8 and in Class 3 at a CBSE school in Bengaluru. She is quick in conversation and good at maths puzzles. She reads English slowly, guesses words from the first letter, and her Hindi dictation is full of errors. Her tuition teacher says, "She is lazy. More practice."

**Specific learning disability (SLD)** means real, lasting difficulty with reading (dyslexia), writing (dysgraphia) or maths (dyscalculia) in a child with average or higher ability, not explained by poor vision, hearing, or lack of teaching. It is not laziness, and more of the same practice rarely fixes it.

### Early signs by age

| Age | Signs worth noting | Why |
|-----|-------------------|-----|
| 2–4 | Late talking; trouble learning nursery rhymes; a parent or sibling with reading problems | Children at family risk show slower early language [A] |
| 4–6 | Cannot hear rhymes or break words into sounds; slow to learn letter names and sounds; slow at naming familiar pictures or colours quickly | These are the foundation skills of decoding: letter knowledge, phonological awareness, and rapid naming [A] |
| 6–8 | Reading much slower than classmates; guessing; avoiding reading aloud; spelling the same word differently each time | Decoding is not becoming automatic |
| 8+ | Reads accurately but very slowly; homework takes far longer than it should; strong spoken answers, weak written ones | Fluency and writing load |

The evidence behind the first two rows is a review of 95 publications on children with a family history of dyslexia (Snowling & Melby-Lervåg 2016) [A]. Family history is one of the clearest early flags, alongside slow letter learning and trouble with rhymes and sounds.

### Reading in Indian scripts

Kannada, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu and other Indian scripts use **akshara**: units that combine a consonant with a vowel sign, plus many joined forms. A study of 5–10-year-olds learning Kannada found that learning the akshara set takes longer than learning the English alphabet, and that awareness of individual sounds emerges more slowly [C, one study]. This was true in weak and strong schools alike.

What this means for you:

- **Do not judge Kannada or Hindi reading by English phonics timelines.** Slower akshara learning in Class 1–2 can be normal.
- **Compare the child with classmates in the same language and school.** A child who is behind peers in **every** script, and in English, is the one to assess.
- **Many Bengaluru children learn three scripts at once** (English, Kannada, Hindi). That is a real load, not a disorder. The RPwD Act allows exemption from second and third languages for children with a certified disability. Ask the school how it applies this in the primary years. Board-exam rules (below) start with Class IX registration.

### Treatment that works, and treatment that does not

A meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials in children and teens with reading disabilities found that **phonics instruction** (explicit, systematic teaching of how letters map to sounds). The trials were in alphabetic languages such as English and German. Teaching akshara the same explicit way is reasonable but has not been tested in trials. was the only approach with statistically confirmed benefit for reading and spelling [A]. Coloured overlays or lenses, auditory training, motor exercises and medical treatments did not reach significance. Look for a remedial educator or special educator who teaches this way, and ask how progress will be measured.

### Formal assessment

A formal SLD assessment includes an intelligence test, standardised reading, writing and maths tests, and checks of vision and hearing. In India, the **NIMHANS SLD battery** is the standard test. DEIC guidelines also list the Dyslexia Early Screening Test (ages 4–6) and the Dyslexia Screening Test–Junior (ages 6–11) for screening. A formal diagnosis needs enough teaching to show a clear gap, so in practice it is made from around age 8 or Class 3, the point India's 2024 disability-assessment guidelines (DEPwD, 14 March 2024) use for SLD screening. Earlier signs deserve early help even before a formal diagnosis.

### Rights under Indian law

The **Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act 2016** lists 21 disabilities. **Specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder, and speech and language disability are on the list. ADHD is not.** If your child has ADHD alone, board concessions under the Act are not available. Ask the school for classroom supports: a seat near the teacher, short tasks, movement breaks, and written instructions. Also ask the assessor to check for a learning disability, which is common alongside ADHD and can be certified. A "benchmark disability" means at least 40% of a listed disability, certified by the competent authority. Some benefits require benchmark level.

Section 17(i) directs the government to make "suitable modifications in the curriculum and examination system", "such as extra time for completion of examination paper, facility of scribe or amanuensis, exemption from second and third language courses".

**CBSE board exams** (from the board's 2019 consolidated circular; check the current year's circular, because rules and deadlines are updated):

| Accommodation | Detail |
|---------------|--------|
| Extra time | 60 minutes for a 3-hour paper (20 minutes per hour) |
| Scribe or reader | Allowed for listed categories, including SLD (dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, dyspraxia, developmental aphasia) and autism |
| Language load | Class X: exemption from the third language; option to study one compulsory language instead of two |
| Certificate | The 2019 circular accepted certificates from government hospitals or RCI-registered practitioners, among others. Under India's March 2024 disability-assessment guidelines, SLD certification goes through government medical boards. Confirm what your board accepts this year. |
| Process | The school enters the disability category when it registers your child with CBSE in Class IX (Class XI for Class XII exams), and applies with the certificate. Concessions are considered only if the category was entered then |

State boards, ICSE, IB and Cambridge have their own rules. Ask the school's exam coordinator in Class 8, not in Class 10.

## Giftedness and Twice-Exceptional Children

### The scene

Riya is 6. She does Class 4 maths in her head and asks about black holes at dinner. She also melts down when her handwriting looks messy, cannot sit through assembly, and has few friends. One teacher calls her gifted. Another calls her a behaviour problem. Both may be right.

**What the long-term evidence says about high ability** (Module 31 covers the wider research frontier):

- The **Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth (SMPY)** has followed more than 5,000 people identified by age 13 through above-grade-level testing, from the top 3% to the top 0.01% of reasoning ability. Even inside the top 1%, higher ability predicted more achievement: patent rates were 13.2% for the top quarter versus 4.8% for the bottom quarter [B]. This shows early ability is informative. It does **not** show that enrichment programmes create it.
- **Acceleration** (skipping a grade or a subject) has real but modest benefits. A 2015 synthesis found grade-skipping academic effect sizes of about +0.67 (another aggregation gives +0.78), with small positive effects on social adjustment (+0.34) and psychological adjustment (+0.42) [B]. The common fear that skipping a grade harms a child socially is not supported. One caveat: schools usually accelerate children who already look socially ready, which flatters the results. Module 20's FAQ reflects this.

**Twice-exceptional (2e)** children are gifted **and** have a disability such as ADHD, dyslexia or autism. The two can hide each other:

```
High ability  ──hides──►  the disability   ("She's bright, she's just not trying")
The disability ──hides──► the ability      ("His written work is weak, so he can't be advanced")
Result: average grades, a frustrated child, and no support for either side
```

What to do:

- [ ] If a bright child's written work is far weaker than their spoken reasoning, ask for an SLD assessment.
- [ ] If a bright child is constantly in trouble for behaviour, ask for an assessment of attention, anxiety and social communication, not only an IQ test.
- [ ] Ask the psychologist to report the **profile** (scores by area), not just one IQ number. A large gap between areas is itself information.
- [ ] IQ scores become stable enough to make decisions about one child only from about age 7 (Module 31). Before that, feed interests and watch.
- [ ] Do not label the child "gifted" to their face as an identity. Praise effort and strategy. This is sensible, but the evidence that mindset messages raise achievement is weak (Module 31).

## How to Get an Assessment in India

```
 Concern (you, teacher, grandparent, screen)
          │
          ▼
 Pediatrician: history, examination, hearing + vision check, screening tool
          │
          ├──► Public route: RBSK team / District Early Intervention Centre /
          │    government medical college child development clinic / NIMHANS
          │
          └──► Private route: developmental pediatrician, child psychiatrist,
               clinical psychologist (RCI-registered), speech-language pathologist,
               occupational therapist
          │
          ▼
 Multidisciplinary evaluation ──► written report ──► intervention plan (start now)
          │
          ▼
 Disability certificate + UDID card (if eligible) ──► school and board accommodations
```

### Who to see for what

| Concern | First professional | Who else may be needed |
|---------|--------------------|------------------------|
| Missed milestones, several areas | Pediatrician, then developmental pediatrician | Physiotherapist, occupational therapist, psychologist |
| Autism signs | Developmental pediatrician or child psychiatrist | Speech-language pathologist, psychologist, occupational therapist |
| Speech delay or stuttering | Audiologist (hearing test) and speech-language pathologist | Developmental pediatrician if other concerns |
| Attention and behaviour | Pediatrician or child psychiatrist | Clinical psychologist; school input |
| Reading, writing, maths | Clinical psychologist for SLD assessment | Remedial or special educator; eye check first |
| Suspected giftedness or 2e | Clinical psychologist experienced with high-ability children | Any of the above if a disability is suspected |

### What it costs

- **Public route:** RBSK screening and treatment are free. DEICs and government hospitals are the cheapest route to a full evaluation and a certificate, but waiting lists can be long.
- **Private route:** If you cannot verify a rupee range from 2–3 published fee pages (for example, government medical college or NIMHANS fee schedules), stay number-free but make the section usable. Name the lowest-cost route: RBSK screening and treatment and DEIC services are free, and the government disability board is the cheapest route to a certificate. Name what drives private cost: number of sessions, number of standardised tests, a school observation, and the written report. Add the script: "Please give me a written quote for the full assessment, including the report, and list the tests included." Cross-reference Module 02's US range. A full psychological or developmental assessment is a multi-session, multi-test process. Ask for an itemised written quote before starting. You can use the private route for speed and the public route for certification.

### What to ask before you book

- [ ] Which standardised tools will you use, and are they normed for Indian children or for our language?
- [ ] Will you check hearing and vision, or should we do that first?
- [ ] Will you collect information from the school as well as from us?
- [ ] Is the assessor registered with the Rehabilitation Council of India (for psychologists and special educators)?
- [ ] Will the report be accepted for a disability certificate and for board exam accommodations?
- [ ] How many sessions, what total cost, and when will we get the written report?

### What a good report contains

- [ ] Reason for referral and the questions it answers
- [ ] Developmental, medical, family and school history, and the sources used (parents, teacher, observation)
- [ ] Every test named, with the child's scores and what they mean in plain words
- [ ] Hearing and vision status
- [ ] A clear conclusion: a diagnosis (with DSM-5 or ICD code), "does not meet criteria", or "concerns remain, review on a set date"
- [ ] Co-existing conditions considered
- [ ] Strengths, not only weaknesses
- [ ] Specific recommendations for home, school and therapy, with who, how often, and how progress will be measured
- [ ] A re-assessment date

**Getting certified:** apply for a disability certificate and a **UDID (Unique Disability ID) card** through the national portal (swavlambancard.gov.in). The UDID system is run by the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities and is meant to give one card, accepted nationwide, for disability benefits.

## The Elite Edge: Private Assessments and Therapies, Decoded

**What families with money buy:** fast private assessments, intensive therapy packages, "brain-based" programmes, specialised lab tests, and imported supplements.

| What is sold | What the evidence says | The free or cheaper version |
|--------------|------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Private developmental screening for reassurance | A screening visit uses the same screeners you can get free or cheaply (Module 02). Speed is the real product. | M-CHAT-R/F (free), CDC checklists (free), TDSC through your pediatrician or DEIC |
| 20–40 hours a week of autism therapy | Across 144 studies, more hours were not linked to bigger gains. The authors found 'little robust evidence' for intensive programmes. The underlying studies are weak, so this means more hours are unproven, not proven useless [B]. | Parent coaching (PACT-style), tested in India with non-specialists [B]; DEIC early interventionist |
| Folate receptor antibody tests and leucovorin for autism | No FDA-cleared test; AAP: evidence insufficient without cerebral folate deficiency [D] | A standard medical workup chosen by your developmental pediatrician |
| Coloured lenses, auditory training, motor or "brain integration" exercises for dyslexia | No confirmed benefit in randomised trials. Only phonics showed a statistically confirmed effect [A]. The others were tested in few, small trials (motor exercises in just one), so they are unproven rather than proven useless [B]. 'Brain integration' programmes were not tested in this review. | Phonics-based remedial teaching; daily shared reading (Module 26) |
| Omega-3 "focus" supplements for ADHD | High-certainty null result on ADHD symptoms [A] | Sleep, exercise, parent training, classroom support |
| Stem-cell "therapy" for autism | Not an accepted treatment. ICMR's 2017 national stem-cell guidelines allow it only inside approved clinical trials, and in January 2026 the Supreme Court of India held that offering it outside a trial is medical malpractice | Nothing to buy. If a clinic offers it, ask for the trial's Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI) number and ethics approval |
| Private speech therapy for every late talker | Needed for real disorders; not every late talker has one | Module 26's stimulation toolkit, plus referral when the red flags above appear |

**The pattern:** money buys speed and convenience. The mechanisms that work (early screening, coached parent interaction, phonics teaching, behavioural parent training, school accommodations) are cheap or free. Spend where it buys time you cannot get otherwise, such as a private evaluation when the public wait is many months, and keep the public route open for certification.

## Mistakes Even Loving Parents Make

1. **Waiting because someone famous talked late.** "Einstein talked late" and "boys are slow" are the most common reasons for lost months. A ten-minute screen settles the question faster than any family story.
2. **Hearing "positive screen" as "diagnosis".** Most children who flag on a screen need something, but not always the thing the screen is named after. Book the follow-up, start the simple help, and hold the label loosely until the evaluation is done.
3. **Buying hours instead of quality.** More therapy hours are not proven to help more. A tired, over-scheduled child learns less. Ask every provider what they will measure and when.
4. **Dropping the home language.** Advice to "speak only English" to a speech-delayed child is not supported by the evidence and costs the child a relationship language (Module 26).
5. **Keeping the diagnosis secret from the school.** Parents fear the label. But without a certificate and a school plan, the child is judged as lazy or naughty, and exam accommodations come too late. Share the report with the teacher you trust most.
6. **Letting brightness close the case.** A gifted child who is struggling may have a second exceptionality. And a child who is simply the youngest in the class may be mislabelled with ADHD. Ask about the whole profile and the child's true age.
