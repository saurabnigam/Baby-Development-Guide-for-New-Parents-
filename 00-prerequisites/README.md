# Module 00: Start Here — How to Use This Guide

> **Time**: 30 minutes | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **For**: All parents and caregivers
>
> ⚕️ Please read the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md) first. It takes two minutes and tells you what to do in an emergency.

---

## Key Takeaways

- This guide is sorted by age. Find your child's stage and start there.
- Every module covers both **thinking skills (IQ)** and **feeling skills (EQ)**, because they grow together.
- Milestones are ranges, not deadlines. The normal range is much wider than most people think.
- You don't need special toys or equipment. You need time, attention, and patience.
- If something worries you, ask your doctor. Don't wait to see if it passes.

---

## What This Guide Is

A plain-language walk through how children grow, from birth to 18, with things you can actually do at each stage.

**What it is not:** a test, a schedule, or a way to build a "better" child. There is no such thing as a perfect parent, and children do not need one.

---

## Three Things to Get Straight Before You Start

### 1. Stop comparing

Your child is not in a race. What you see online is a highlight reel — nobody posts the eight-month-old who isn't sitting yet.

The only useful comparison is your child now versus your child last month.

### 2. "Good enough" is the goal, and that's not a consolation prize

In 1953, the paediatrician and psychoanalyst **Donald Winnicott** wrote about the "good enough mother" — an idea that now applies to any parent. His point was not that you should aim low. It was that *perfect* parenting would actually be bad for a child:

> "The good-enough mother starts off with an almost complete adaptation to her infant's needs, and as time proceeds adapts less and less completely, gradually, according to the infant's growing ability to deal with her failure."
> — D. W. Winnicott, 1953

Read that again, because it's the most freeing sentence in child development. **Your small failures are part of the design.** A baby whose every need is met instantly never learns to wait. A child who is never frustrated never learns to cope.

What matters is not getting it right every time. It's noticing when you got it wrong and coming back — what this guide calls **repair**.

### 3. Trust the science, and trust yourself

This guide gives you frameworks. You know your child. When those two disagree, pay attention — parents notice things before tests do.

## What You Actually Need

**A doctor you can talk to.** In the US, the American Academy of Paediatrics recommends check-ups at:

> 3–5 days · 1 month · 2 · 4 · 6 · 9 · 12 · 15 · 18 · 24 · 30 months · then once a year from age 3 through 21

Other countries run different schedules. Ask what applies where you live. ([AAP/Bright Futures schedule](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/health-management/Pages/Well-Child-Care-A-Check-Up-for-Success.aspx))

**A reasonably safe home** for whatever your child can currently do — and redo it every time they get more mobile. What was safe for a baby who stays put is not safe for one who crawls.

**The habit of watching.** Notice what your child looks at, reaches for, and reacts to. This is free, takes no equipment, and is the single most useful skill in this whole guide.

### What you don't need

- Expensive "brain-building" toys
- Flash cards for babies
- Learning apps or videos for under-2s
- A tidy house, a fixed schedule, or unlimited patience

## Milestones: What They Really Mean

A milestone is a skill that **most** children develop within a certain age range. That's all. It is not a deadline, an IQ test, or a competition.

### The normal range is enormous

Here is real data. The World Health Organization followed 816 healthy children in **Ghana, India, Norway, Oman, and the USA** and recorded when each one reached six movement milestones. These are the windows in which **99% of healthy children** got there:

| Skill | Age window (99% of healthy children) | Width |
|-------|--------------------------------------|-------|
| Sitting without support | 3.8 – 9.2 months | 5.4 months |
| Standing with help | 4.8 – 11.4 months | 6.6 months |
| Crawling on hands and knees | 5.2 – 13.5 months | 8.3 months |
| Walking with help | 5.9 – 13.7 months | 7.8 months |
| Standing alone | 6.9 – 16.9 months | 10.0 months |
| **Walking alone** | **8.2 – 17.6 months** | **9.4 months** |

*Source: [WHO Motor Development Study, 2006](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/child-growth/child-growth-standards/indicators/motor-development-milestones/who-motor-development-study-windows-of-achievement-for-six-gross-motor-development-milestones.pdf)*

Look at the walking row. A child who walks at 9 months and a child who walks at 17 months are **both completely normal**. That's a nine-month spread — and the child who walked last is not behind, slower, or less clever.

**And one more finding from that study:** 4.3% of these healthy children **never crawled on hands and knees at all.** They went straight from sitting to pulling up, or shuffled on their bottoms. Crawling is common. It is not required.

### Other common milestones

| Skill | Usual range |
|-------|-------------|
| First social smile | 6–8 weeks |
| First words | 8–15 months |
| Two words together ("more milk") | 18–30 months |

### A worked example

> Priya is 13 months old and not walking. Her cousin walked at 10 months. At a family gathering, three people mention it.
>
> **What the WHO data says:** 13 months is comfortably inside the normal window, which runs to 17.6 months. Priya isn't late. Her cousin was early.
>
> **What actually matters:** is Priya *moving toward* walking? Does she pull up on furniture? Cruise along the sofa? Stand while holding on? Does she use both sides of her body evenly? Is she communicating — pointing, babbling, responding to her name?
>
> If yes to those, there is nothing to do but wait and let her practise on the floor.
>
> If she isn't weight-bearing at all, or one side seems much weaker, or she has lost a skill she used to have — that's a phone call, today. Not because 13 months is late, but because *those* signs are different from simply not walking yet.

That's the whole skill: **look at the pattern, not the single number.**

## IQ and EQ: Why This Guide Does Both

**IQ — thinking skills.** Solving problems, remembering, reasoning, language, understanding space.

**EQ — feeling skills.** Knowing what you feel, calming yourself down, understanding other people, getting along, keeping going when it's hard.

### A claim this guide will not repeat

You have probably heard that "EQ matters more than IQ," or that it explains most of career success. That comes from a 1995 popular book, not from research, and the evidence doesn't support it.

When researchers actually measured it, they found emotional skills **add** to what thinking ability predicts, rather than beating it. Joseph and Newman's meta-analysis — a study that combines the results of many other studies — found emotional intelligence has real value **on top of** cognitive ability and personality, especially in jobs with heavy emotional demands ([Joseph & Newman, 2010](https://workplaceinsight.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/JosephNewman2010.pdf)).

The claim is also usually credited to researchers **Mayer and Salovey**, who built the scientific model of emotional intelligence and did not make it.

### The version that's actually true, and more useful

**IQ and EQ predict different things, and each one caps how much of the other your child can use.**

- A child who can't calm down can't think. The ability is there; it just isn't reachable in that moment.
- A child who gives up when things get hard never finds out what they could have solved.
- A child who can't read a room can't work with anyone, however clever they are.

And here's the practical part: **feeling skills are far more teachable than raw thinking ability.** That's why this guide spends so much time on them — not because they matter more, but because you can do more about them.

### They grow together

A baby who feels safe explores more, and exploring is how babies learn. A child who can handle frustration sticks with a puzzle longer, and sticking with it is how puzzles get solved.

You cannot really build one without the other.

## When to Ask for Help

### Call your doctor if

- Your child stays at the **late edge** of the range on several things at once
- Your child **loses a skill** they already had — at any age, this always warrants a call
- Feeding, sleeping, or connecting with people is persistently difficult
- Your gut says something is off

That last one is not a soft criterion. Parents notice things before tests do, and good clinicians know it.

### How to actually raise it — a worked example

Most parents leave appointments without saying the thing they came to say. The visit is short, the doctor is busy, and "he seems fine, doesn't he?" is an easy thing to nod at.

**What doesn't work:**

> "I'm probably being silly, but is his talking okay? Everyone says boys are slower."

That invites reassurance, and you'll get it.

**What works — bring three things written down:**

1. **What you've observed, specifically.** Not "he doesn't talk much" but: *"He's 20 months. He has four words. He doesn't point at things to show me. He doesn't turn when I say his name from across the room."*
2. **When you first noticed**, and whether it's changing.
3. **The direct question:** *"I'd like him assessed. What's the process?"*

Then, if you're told to wait and see and you're still worried:

> "I hear you. I'd still like a referral so we're on the list. If he catches up, we can cancel it."

That is a completely reasonable thing to say, and no good clinician will object. Waiting lists are long. Getting on one early costs nothing and can be undone.

### Early help works — and some of it is free

Getting support early produces better outcomes than waiting. The waiting itself is the risk.

**In the US**, under the law known as **IDEA Part C**, every state runs an early intervention programme for children from birth until their third birthday. Some important details most parents don't know:

- **You can refer your own child.** You don't need a doctor's referral.
- **The evaluation is free.** By law, child find, evaluation and assessment, service coordination, and writing the plan must be at public expense with **no fee charged** ([34 CFR 303.521](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/regs/c/f/303.521)).
- **The therapy itself may or may not be free.** States are allowed to charge sliding-scale fees for the actual services, but a family unable to pay must still receive them at no cost.

([US Dept. of Education — IDEA Part C](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/early-learning-early-childhood/))

Outside the US, ask your doctor, health visitor, or paediatrician about early intervention services in your area. Most countries have something.

## Words This Guide Uses

Every technical term, in plain English. Come back here whenever one turns up.

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Serve and return** | The back-and-forth between you and your baby. They make a sound, you answer. They point, you name it |
| **Co-regulation** | Calming down by borrowing an adult's calm. This is how all children manage feelings before they can do it alone |
| **Self-regulation** | Calming yourself down without help. This takes about eighteen years to build |
| **Attachment** | The bond between a child and the people who care for them |
| **Secure base** | Using a trusted adult as home base — going off to explore, coming back to check in |
| **Scaffolding** | Helping just enough, then backing off. Like the scaffolding on a building — temporary, removed when it holds itself up |
| **Zone of proximal development** | The gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help. This is where learning happens |
| **Object permanence** | Knowing things still exist when you can't see them. Babies learn this around 6–9 months, which is why peekaboo suddenly gets funny |
| **Theory of mind** | Understanding that other people know, want, and feel different things from you. Starts around 3–4, and is the foundation of empathy |
| **Executive function** | Three skills bundled: holding information in your head, stopping yourself doing something, and switching between rules |
| **Working memory** | Holding something in mind while you use it — like remembering three instructions while doing the first |
| **Metacognition** | Thinking about your own thinking. "How did I work that out?" Usually from about age 7 |
| **Symbolic thought** | Letting one thing stand for another — a block becomes a phone. The foundation of language and reading |
| **Sensorimotor** | Learning by touching, moving, and mouthing rather than by thinking. How babies learn for roughly the first two years |
| **Attunement** | Noticing and matching your child's emotional state, so they feel understood |
| **Neural pruning** | The brain clearing out connections it isn't using, so the ones it keeps work faster |
| **Myelination** | Insulation forming around nerve pathways, making signals travel much faster. Continues into the mid-20s |
| **Parallel play** | Playing *next to* another child rather than *with* them. Normal and healthy until about age 3 |
| **Proprioception** | Your sense of where your body is without looking |
| **Conservation** | Knowing an amount stays the same when you change its shape — the same water in a tall thin glass and a short wide one. Usually clicks around 6–7 |
| **Seriation** | Putting things in order by size or amount |
| **Repair** | Coming back after a rupture — an argument, a shouted word, a bad moment — and reconnecting. More important than avoiding the rupture |

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Watch your child for five uninterrupted minutes.** No phone. No agenda. Don't teach, don't direct, don't test.

Just notice:

- What are they looking at?
- What sounds are they making?
- What are they reaching for, or moving toward?
- What makes them smile? What makes them stop?

Write down three things you noticed.

Do this once a week. Within a month you'll see patterns you'd have missed — what they're working on, what frustrates them, what they've just started doing. That's the beginning of the most useful parenting skill there is: **seeing what's actually in front of you**, rather than what a chart says should be.
