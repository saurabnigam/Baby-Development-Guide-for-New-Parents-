# Module 00: Prerequisites & How to Use This Guide

> **Time**: 30 minutes | **Difficulty**: Beginner | **For**: All parents and caregivers

---

## Key Takeaways

- This guide is organized by age — jump to your child's current stage
- Every module covers BOTH cognitive (IQ) and emotional (EQ) development
- Milestones are ranges, not deadlines — every child develops differently
- You don't need special equipment — just presence, patience, and play
- When in doubt, consult your pediatrician

---

## What You Need Before Starting

### Mindset Prerequisites

1. **Let go of comparison** — Your child is not competing with other babies. Social media milestones are curated highlights, not reality
2. **Embrace "good enough"** — The paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott coined the idea of the "good enough" parent from decades of clinical practice: children need responsiveness and repair, not perfection. Later attachment research supports the underlying point — ordinary caregiving with reliable repair is what builds security
3. **Trust the science, trust yourself** — This guide gives you frameworks. Your instincts fill in the gaps

### Practical Prerequisites

- **Pediatrician relationship** — Regular well-child visits. AAP's Bright Futures schedule is a newborn visit at 3–5 days, then 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24, and 30 months, then annually from age 3. Schedules vary by country — ask your own clinician what applies where you live
- **Safe environment** — Basic childproofing appropriate to your child's current stage
- **Observation habit** — Start noticing what your baby looks at, reaches for, reacts to. This is your most powerful developmental tool

### What You Do NOT Need

- Expensive "brain-building" toys
- Flash cards for infants
- Educational apps or videos for children under 2
- A perfect house, perfect schedule, or perfect patience

## Understanding Milestones

### What Milestones ARE

- **Guideposts**, not finish lines
- **Ranges** — most milestones have a 3–6 month window of "normal"
- **Skills most children develop** by a certain age range

### What Milestones ARE NOT

- Deadlines your child must meet
- Intelligence tests
- Competition benchmarks
- Reasons for anxiety

### The "Range" Principle

| Milestone | Typical Range | Mean Age |
|-----------|--------------|----------|
| First social smile | 4–8 weeks | 6 weeks |
| Sitting independently | 4–9 months | 6 months |
| First words | 8–15 months | 12 months |
| Walking | 9–17 months | 12 months |
| Two-word phrases | 18–30 months | 24 months |

*Source: WHO Motor Development Study, 2006; Bayley Scales of Infant Development*

A child who walks at 16 months has the same long-term motor outcomes as one who walks at 10 months. The range is wide and normal.

## IQ vs EQ: Why Both Matter

### IQ (Cognitive Intelligence)
- Problem-solving, memory, reasoning, language, spatial awareness
- Traditionally measured by standardized tests
- Important for academic and professional success

### EQ (Emotional Intelligence)
- Self-awareness, self-regulation, empathy, social skills, motivation
- Measured through behavioral observations and social functioning
- Predicts relationship quality, mental health, and resilience better than IQ does

> **A claim we're not going to repeat.** You have probably heard that "EQ matters more than IQ" or that it accounts for most of career success. That comes from a 1995 trade book, not from the research literature, and meta-analyses don't support it — cognitive ability remains the strongest single predictor of academic and job performance, with emotional skills adding meaningful value *on top of* it rather than replacing it. The claim is also frequently attributed to Mayer & Salovey, who developed the ability model of emotional intelligence and explicitly did not make it.
>
> The accurate version is more useful anyway: **IQ and EQ predict different things, and each one limits how much of the other actually gets used.** And crucially — emotional skills are far more teachable than raw cognitive ability, which is why this guide spends so much time on them.

### The Critical Insight

**They develop together.** A baby who feels securely attached (EQ) explores more freely and learns faster (IQ). A child who can manage frustration (EQ) persists at puzzles longer (IQ). A teen who understands others' perspectives (EQ) collaborates better on complex projects (IQ).

**You cannot meaningfully develop one without the other.**

## When to Seek Professional Help

### Contact Your Pediatrician If:

- Your child consistently misses milestones at the **late end** of the range
- There is **regression** — losing skills they previously had
- You notice persistent difficulties with feeding, sleeping, or social engagement
- Your gut tells you something isn't right (parent intuition is real and valid)

### Early Intervention Works

Research consistently shows that early identification and intervention for developmental delays produces significantly better outcomes. In the US, Early Intervention services (Part C of IDEA) are available for children 0–3 at no cost. Don't wait and see — early action is always better.

## Terminology Used in This Guide

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Serve & Return** | The back-and-forth interaction between baby and caregiver |
| **Scaffolding** | Supporting a child just enough to help them reach the next level |
| **Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)** | The gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help |
| **Attachment** | The emotional bond between child and primary caregiver(s) |
| **Neural pruning** | The brain's process of eliminating unused connections (use it or lose it) |
| **Executive function** | Higher-order skills: working memory, flexible thinking, self-control |
| **Proprioception** | The sense of body position and movement in space |
| **Parallel play** | Playing alongside (not with) other children — a normal developmental stage |

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Right now, do this one thing:**

Watch your child for 5 uninterrupted minutes. No phone, no agenda. Just observe:
- What are they looking at?
- What sounds are they making?
- What are they reaching for or moving toward?
- What makes them smile or frown?

Write down what you notice. This is the beginning of developmental awareness — the single most powerful parenting skill you can build.
