# Module 12: Age 5 to 7 — School, Friends & Being Compared

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Intermediate | **Focus**: starting school, the arrival of self-comparison, and the three things that quietly wreck a school-age child
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Something new and permanent arrives this year: **your child starts comparing themselves to other people.** How you respond to that shapes a lot.
- **Check sleep before you conclude anything about behaviour.** An under-slept 6-year-old looks inattentive, impulsive, and irritable — not sleepy.
- The 20 minutes after school are worth more than any homework help. Food, movement, no questions.
- Friendship skills have to be taught explicitly. Most children are never taught them.
- **Praise the strategy, not the effort in general, and never the trait.** The popular version of this advice is stronger than the evidence — the honest version is below.

---

## What's Happening Now

School changes the shape of childhood. For the first time, your child spends most of their day somewhere you aren't, with adults you don't choose, among children who are visibly better and worse than them at things.

That last part is the big one. **Social comparison switches on around now** and doesn't switch off again.

They can also do a lot more: hold rules in mind, follow classroom routines, focus for longer stretches, and reason logically about concrete things. But the machinery is still immature, and it collapses when they're tired, hungry, or overwhelmed — which by 4pm on a Thursday, they are.

---

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Learns letters, sounds, and then whole words
- Basic number work; understanding of quantity firms up
- Follows multi-step instructions more reliably
- Sorts, categorises, and sequences
- Real logical reasoning about concrete situations
- Understands that an amount stays the same when its shape changes — the same juice in a tall thin glass and a short wide one
- Attention span and persistence both improve

**Around 7, a big shift:** they stop *learning to read* and start *reading to learn*. That's when reading becomes the main way new knowledge arrives.

## What to Expect: Feeling

- Real, more stable friendships
- Strong sense of fairness — and loud about it
- **Compares self to peers.** "I'm the worst at maths." "Everyone can do it except me."
- Recovers from disappointment, with support
- Uses words more, melts down less
- Growing conscience; genuine concern about doing right

## The Three Things That Wreck a School-Age Child

Before you conclude anything about your child's behaviour, attitude, or ability, check these. In this order.

### 1. Sleep

**6–12 year olds need 9–12 hours in 24**, per the joint AAP and American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommendation ([AASM consensus statement](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27250809) · [AAP summary](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sleep/Pages/healthy-sleep-habits-how-many-hours-does-your-child-need.aspx)).

| Age | Hours per 24 |
|-----|-------------|
| 1–2 years | 11–14 |
| 3–5 years | 10–13 |
| **6–12 years** | **9–12** |
| 13–18 years | 8–10 |

**Here is the thing most parents don't know: an under-slept child doesn't look sleepy.** They look inattentive, impulsive, irritable, emotionally fragile, and "hyperactive". Short sleep in a school-age child is routinely mistaken for a behaviour problem or an attention problem.

**Work backwards from the wake time.** If they must be up at 7, and they need 10–11 hours, bedtime is 8 or 8:30 — asleep, not starting the routine. Most families discover their bedtime is an hour later than they thought.

**If your child's behaviour got worse when school started, look here first.**

### 2. Movement

Children this age need around an hour of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day, and it is not optional decoration — physical activity in this age group is associated with better attention, mood, and school outcomes.

**Move them before homework, not after.** A child who has sat still all day cannot sit still for more sitting.

### 3. Decompression

They have been holding it together for six hours. The container is empty.

## The After-School Meltdown

Almost every family gets this and almost every family misreads it.

> **Your child is reportedly delightful at school. At 3:20pm they get in the car and fall apart over the wrong snack.**
>
> **What it means:** they used every bit of their self-control at school, all day, and you are the person safe enough to fall apart in front of. It is, irritatingly, a compliment.
>
> **What usually happens**
>
> "How was school?" *(nothing)* "What did you do?" *(nothing)* "Did you play with Ben?" *(shrug)* "Have you got homework?" *(explosion)*
>
> Four questions in ninety seconds, to a child running on empty.
>
> **What to do instead — the 20-minute rule**
>
> For the first twenty minutes: **food, movement, and no questions.**
>
> 1. **Food first, immediately.** In the bag, in the car, in your hand at the gate. Blood sugar is doing more of this than mood is
> 2. **No questions.** Not one. Not even "good day?"
> 3. **Movement if you can** — walk, scoot, run around the park on the way home
> 4. **Sit near them without an agenda.** Say nothing, or narrate something of your own: *"I had a really annoying morning. The printer broke twice."*
> 5. **Wait.** They will start talking. Usually at a moment of your choosing — never, ever at the moment of your choosing. Bath time, bedtime, in the car, sideways
>
> **The one question that works, when they're ready:** not "how was school?" but something specific and slightly negative:
>
> > "What was the most annoying thing today?"
>
> "How was school?" gets "fine" because it's unanswerable. "Most annoying thing" is specific, gives permission to complain, and almost always opens a real conversation.

## Praise: What Actually Holds Up

You've probably been told to praise effort, not ability — the "growth mindset" advice. The real picture is more nuanced, and worth knowing.

**What the evidence says.** Two large meta-analyses looked at this. The first pooled 273 studies covering over 365,000 people to test whether mindset relates to achievement. The second pooled 43 studies covering over 57,000 people to test whether mindset *interventions* improve achievement.

**Overall effects were weak in both.** But there was a real signal in one place: students who were **academically at risk or from low-income backgrounds** did appear to benefit.

([Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara, 2018, *Psychological Science*](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29505339))

### So what should you actually say?

The useful principle survives even if the packaging was oversold: **describe what they did, specifically, and attribute results to things that can change.**

| Weak | Better |
|------|--------|
| "You're so clever!" | "You worked out that you could line up the edges first." |
| "Good job!" | "You tried three different ways before that one worked." |
| "Good try!" *(after failure)* | "That approach didn't work. What's another way in?" |
| "You're a natural." | "You've been practising that for weeks and it shows." |

**Why generic effort praise after failure backfires:** "good try!" when a child has just failed reads as consolation for lacking ability. Strategy talk doesn't — it points at something they can change.

**And don't say "I was never good at maths."** Even as a joke. Parents' maths anxiety appears to transfer to children, and it hands them a permission slip to quit.

## Friendship: Still Has to Be Taught

Friendships get more real and more complicated. Children are rarely taught how to do any of it.

**Rehearse these at home, when nothing has happened:**

| Situation | The script |
|-----------|-----------|
| Joining in | "Can I play?" |
| Being refused | "Okay." *(and knowing this is survivable)* |
| Sharing time | "Can we do one turn each?" |
| Setting a limit | "I don't like that. Stop." |
| Needing space | "I want to play on my own for a bit." |
| Repairing | "I'm sorry. Can I help fix it?" |
| Disagreeing | "I don't want to do that. Can we do something else?" |

### When friendship hurts

- **Get the story before the advice.** Most parents skip to solving
- **Don't minimise.** "I'm sure they didn't mean it" ends the conversation
- **Don't over-react either.** Your face tells them how serious this is
- **Then:** "What could you try tomorrow?"

**Watch for the difference between conflict and bullying.** Conflict is between roughly equal children and changes shape over time. Bullying is repeated, targeted, and involves a power imbalance. Repeated exclusion or humiliation of the same child is a school conversation, not a resilience-building opportunity.

## Reading

**Keep reading aloud, well above their own reading level.**

Children can understand far more complex language than they can decode, and that gap lasts until roughly age 13. So read-alouds are where the vocabulary and the ideas come from, while their own reading builds fluency.

**Protect the relationship over the performance.** A child who is made to read out loud every night as a chore, corrected on every word, learns that reading is a test. A child who is read to, and who reads whatever they like, reads more.

**Comics, joke books, football annuals, and the same book fifteen times all count.**

**If reading is a struggle, move fast.** Reading difficulties respond much better to early help than late. Get hearing and vision checked, talk to the school, and ask specifically about a phonological awareness screen.

## Feeding: Age 5 to 7

**Breakfast is the one that matters now.** Protein plus a slower carbohydrate holds attention through the morning better than sugary cereal.

| | |
|---|---|
| Energy | Roughly 1,400–1,800 kcal a day |
| Iron | 10mg/day |
| Calcium | 1,000mg/day |
| Vitamin D | 600 IU a day |
| Protein | ~19g/day |
| Oily fish | 1–2 times a week where practical |

**Involve them in packing lunch.** A lunch they chose gets eaten; a lunch that arrived doesn't.

**Watch the "inattentive or irritable" list.** Hungry, under-slept, constipated, over-scheduled. Check all four before concluding anything.

## Parent Behaviours That Matter Most

- **Connection before correction**, especially in the first twenty minutes home
- Ask about **effort and difficulty**, not marks
- **Let them forget things.** The consequence of an unpacked PE kit teaches better than you can
- **Don't do it for them because it's faster.** This is the year that habit sets for a decade
- **Model mistakes out loud:** "I got that wrong. Let me try again."
- **Protect unstructured play.** It doesn't stop being essential because school started
- **Talk to the teacher before there's a problem**, not only after

## Common Worries

### "They don't want to go to school"

Predictable mornings, calm validation plus steady follow-through, connection before correction, and coordination with the teacher if it persists.

**Watch for anxiety showing up in the body** — tummy aches, headaches, sleep trouble — especially on Sunday nights and Monday mornings.

### "They say they're stupid"

**Don't argue with it.** "That's not true!" ends the conversation and teaches them not to tell you.

> "That's a horrible way to feel. Tell me what happened."

Get the story first. Later: "That sounds like disappointment talking. Disappointment says extreme things."

Occasional self-criticism is normal. Persistent, across weeks, is worth professional input.

### Perfectionism

Praise the process. Model your own mistakes visibly. Don't correct every small error. Watch for a child who avoids trying anything they might not be good at — that's the real cost.

### "They're behind"

Ask the teacher directly what they see, and ask what would help. Check hearing and vision. Then focus on the specific gap rather than a general sense of behind-ness.

**The comparison to make is with your child last term**, not with the child next to them.

---

## Red Flags: Talk to Someone If

- School distress is severe or sustained
- Emotional or behavioural difficulty is present **across home and school**, not just one
- Persistent social isolation
- Learning, attention, or language concerns are becoming clearer
- Marked change in sleep, appetite, mood, or functioning
- Any talk of self-harm or not wanting to be alive — **take seriously at any age**; see [Module 14](../14-age-12-18/README.md#if-you-are-worried-about-suicide-or-self-harm)

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Change one question.**

Stop asking "how was school?" It gets "fine" because it's unanswerable.

Ask instead, once a day, after they've eaten:

> "What was the most annoying thing today?"

It's specific, it gives permission to complain, and it almost always gets a real answer. Follow it with "what did you do?" and you're three turns into a conversation you weren't having last week.
