# Module 13: Age 7 to 12 — Competence, Friends & Being Someone

> **Time**: 3 hours | **Difficulty**: Intermediate | **Focus**: the drive to be good at something, the arrival of the peer world, and the years when self-concept sets
>
> ⚕️ Parent education, not medical advice. See the [Disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

---

## Key Takeaways

- The question your child is working on now is **"am I any good at anything?"** Almost everything they do is an attempt to answer it.
- Peers become the main audience. This is normal, necessary, and not a rejection of you.
- **They get better at hiding feelings — which is not the same as managing them.** Middle childhood is where children learn to look fine.
- This is a great age for real responsibility, real skills, and long projects. It's the first time they can stick with something for months.
- **Stay in the boat.** Parents often step back around now because children seem more self-sufficient. This is exactly when the groundwork for adolescence is laid.

---

## What's Happening Now

Middle childhood gets described as the quiet stretch between toddlerhood and teenage chaos. It isn't quiet — it's just less loud. A great deal is being decided.

**Thinking gets systematic.** They can classify, compare, plan several steps ahead, and reason properly about concrete things. They start thinking about their own thinking: *how did I work that out?*

**Self-concept forms.** They build a story about who they are — sporty, clever, funny, bad at maths, the kind of person who gives up. That story becomes remarkably sticky, and much of it is written this decade.

**The peer world takes over as the main stage.** Fairness, status, belonging, and being included become enormously important. Your approval still matters — but it stops being the only currency.

---

## The Engine: Competence

There's a well-supported account of what motivates people, developed over decades of research. It says humans need three things to be self-motivated:

| Need | What it means | What it looks like at this age |
|------|--------------|-------------------------------|
| **Competence** | "I'm getting good at something" | Wanting to practise, to improve, to show you |
| **Autonomy** | "I chose this" | Fierce resistance to being made to do things |
| **Relatedness** | "I belong with these people" | Friendship groups, teams, in-jokes, clubs |

*(Ryan & Deci — [self-determination theory](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11392867))*

**Why this matters at 7 to 12:** competence is the loud one now. A child in this window wants, badly, to be genuinely good at something — and if they can't find something they're good at, they will often decide they are someone who isn't good at things.

### What to do with that

**Help them find one thing.** It doesn't matter what. Football, drawing, coding, cooking, drums, chess, birds, a video game they get seriously skilled at. What matters is the experience of *going from bad at something to good at something through practice*, because that generalises.

**Let them choose it.** A skill you chose satisfies competence but starves autonomy, and the motivation doesn't stick.

**Don't take it over.** The moment your child's hobby becomes a thing you manage, chase, and measure, it becomes yours. The most useful sentence you have is: *"Tell me about it — I don't really understand it."*

**Watch for the child who is good at nothing they value.** A child who's told they're "clever" but wants to be sporty is not getting their competence need met. Widen what counts in your house.

## What to Expect: Thinking

- Real logical reasoning about concrete situations
- Better organisation and planning
- Reading comprehension and writing take off
- More sophisticated number sense and problem-solving
- **Metacognition** — thinking about their own thinking, reflecting on strategies and mistakes
- Understands sarcasm, irony, and jokes that turn on double meanings
- Can hold and defend an argument

## What to Expect: Feeling

- More stable friendship groups, with hierarchies
- Intense concern with fairness, status, and belonging
- **Can mask emotions** — which looks like regulation and often isn't
- Stronger conscience and moral reasoning
- Wants to feel capable, useful, and respected
- Increasingly private

**On masking:** a 9-year-old who says "I'm fine" is a genuinely new thing. They now can, and often do, keep it in. It comes out sideways — at bedtime, in the car, as irritability, as stomach aches, or in a sudden disproportionate reaction to something small.

**Which is why side-by-side time matters more than face-to-face questioning now.** Driving, walking, washing up, kicking a ball. Nobody has to make eye contact, and the pressure is off.

## Puberty Starts Here — Earlier Than Most Parents Expect

Puberty commonly begins during this window: **from around 8–13 in girls, and 9–14 in boys.** That means it starts, for many children, in primary school.

**Talk about it before it happens, not after.** A child who gets their first period or their first erection with no warning experiences it as something going wrong.

- **Keep it matter-of-fact and repeated.** Little and often, not one big talk
- **Both parents, both children.** Boys need to know about periods; girls need to know about erections. Ignorance produces cruelty
- **Name body changes without commentary on appearance.** This is exactly the window where remarks about weight land permanently
- **They will be mortified.** That's fine. Say it anyway, briefly, and don't require a conversation in return

## A Worked Example: The Social Problem

> **Your 9-year-old comes home furious. "Maya told everyone I cheated at the game and now nobody will play with me."**
>
> **What usually happens**
>
> "Did you cheat?" · "Well, you shouldn't have..." · "I'll email the teacher." · "Just ignore her, she's jealous."
>
> All four end the conversation. Two are investigations, one takes over, one dismisses.
>
> **What to do instead — five questions, in order**
>
> **1. Get the story, all of it, without reacting.**
> > "Tell me what happened."
>
> Let them run. Don't correct the parts that are obviously biased. You are not conducting an inquiry.
>
> **2. Name the feeling before touching the facts.**
> > "That sounds humiliating."
>
> Say the actual feeling. Humiliated, left out, betrayed. This is the step that makes them keep talking.
>
> **3. Then, and only then, widen it.**
> > "What do you think was going on for Maya?"
>
> Not *"maybe she didn't mean it"* — that's you supplying the answer. Ask, and let the answer be "she's just horrible" the first time. Ask again another day.
>
> **4. Options, not instructions.**
> > "What are your options here?"
> > "What happens if you do that?"
>
> Let them generate bad ideas. Ask what would happen next. That's the reasoning being trained.
>
> **5. Their choice, your backing.**
> > "What are you going to try?"
> > "How do you want me to help — do you want me to do something, or just know about it?"
>
> **That last question is the important one.** Children this age frequently want to be heard and specifically do not want you to act. Acting anyway — emailing the school without asking — is the fastest way to stop being told things.
>
> **When to override that:** if it's repeated, targeted, involves a power imbalance, or is physical or online and sustained, you act — but tell them you're going to and why. *"This one I do have to take to school. Here's what I'm going to say."*

## Conflict, or Bullying?

| | Conflict | Bullying |
|---|---------|----------|
| Power | Roughly equal | Imbalanced |
| Pattern | One-off or changes shape | Repeated, targeted |
| Intent | Usually not to harm | To harm, humiliate, or exclude |
| Response | Coach them through it | **Adults must act** |

**Don't treat bullying as a resilience opportunity.** Repeated targeted humiliation is not character-building, and telling a child to handle it themselves teaches them that you won't help.

**Watch the digital version.** From about 9 or 10 it moves into group chats and games, where it runs 24 hours and leaves a record. Ask about it specifically — "is anything happening in the chats?" — because they won't volunteer it.

**The one rule that keeps you informed:** never make telling you about a problem cost them their device. If disclosure costs access, you have guaranteed non-disclosure.

## Low Confidence: A Worked Example

> **"I'm rubbish at maths. I'm the worst in the class."**
>
> **What doesn't work**
>
> "No you're not!" — they know their own position in the class better than you do, so this reads as either not listening or not understanding.
>
> **What to do**
>
> **1. Don't argue with the feeling.**
> > "That sounds rubbish. Tell me what happened today."
>
> **2. Find the actual size of it.** Usually it's one specific thing, not maths.
> > "Is it all of maths, or a particular bit?"
>
> Often: "it's the timed tests." That's a completely different, solvable problem.
>
> **3. Separate the skill from the identity.**
> > "You're not bad at maths. You haven't learned this bit yet. Those are different."
>
> **4. Find evidence they'll accept.** Not compliments — evidence.
> > "You couldn't do division at all in September. Remember? Now you can."
>
> **5. Make a small plan they own.**
> > "Do you want to do ten minutes of times tables with me before school, for a fortnight, and see if the tests feel different?"
>
> **6. Then tell the teacher** — after telling your child you're going to.

## Responsibility: The Underrated Intervention

Children this age want to be useful, and are almost never asked to be. Household contribution is associated with later self-efficacy — and even setting the research aside, a child who is genuinely needed learns they are genuinely capable.

| Age | Real jobs |
|-----|-----------|
| 7–8 | Load/unload dishwasher · take out rubbish · make their own breakfast · feed a pet |
| 8–10 | Own laundry with supervision · cook simple food · sweep · look after a pet properly |
| 10–12 | Own laundry fully · cook a meal · clean a room properly · help with younger siblings |

**The rule that decides whether this works: don't redo their work in front of them.** The badly made bed stays badly made. Redoing it says the standard matters more than the person, and the person stops volunteering.

**Keep family chores unpaid.** You do them because you live here. Extra jobs can be paid. Blurring the two teaches that helping is a transaction.

## Sleep, Movement, and Screens

**Sleep: 9–12 hours** for 6–12 year olds. Still the first thing to check when behaviour, mood, or school performance dips. See [Module 12](../12-age-5-7/README.md#1-sleep).

**Movement: aim for an average of at least 60 minutes a day** of moderate or vigorous activity across the week, plus activities that strengthen muscles and bones, and break up long periods of sitting ([NHS guidelines](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-children-and-young-people/)).

Moderate intensity is easy to spot: **they can still talk, but not sing.**

**Screens** shift from time limits to habits — devices out of bedrooms at night, no screens in the last hour before bed, and conversations about what they're seeing rather than only how long. See [Module 18](../18-screen-time-technology/README.md).

## Feeding: Age 7 to 12

Habits set here and travel into adulthood, so family food culture matters more than any individual meal.

| | |
|---|---|
| Energy | Roughly 1,400–2,200 kcal a day — wide range, driven by growth spurts, sex, and activity |
| Iron | 8–10mg/day; demand rises with pre-pubertal growth |
| Calcium | 1,000mg/day ages 4–8; **1,300mg/day from age 9** (US RDA — some national bodies set lower targets) |
| Vitamin D | 600 IU a day |
| Protein | Around 34g/day at 9–13 |
| Oily fish | 1–2 times a week where practical |

**Calcium steps up at 9** because this is a peak window for building bone mass, and much of adult bone density is set before 20.

**Growth spurts make hunger unpredictable.** A child who eats nothing for a week and then everything for three days is normal. Respond to appetite rather than fixed portions.

**Don't comment on their body, ever** — not their weight, not anyone else's, not your own. This is the window where disordered eating starts to become possible, and adult commentary is one of the reliable ingredients.

## Common Worries

### "They've gone quiet"

Increasing privacy is developmentally normal. Test whether it's privacy or withdrawal: are they still doing the things they enjoy? Still seeing friends? Eating and sleeping normally?

**Privacy plus normal functioning = fine. Withdrawal plus loss of interest = look closer.**

Use side-by-side time. Ask specific questions rather than general ones.

### "They only care about their friends"

Correct and healthy. Your influence becomes indirect rather than absent — it runs through the relationship, the values you model, and being someone they'd come to in trouble.

**Stay in the boat.** Keep the routines, the meals, the lifts, the shared things. Presence at 11 is what makes conversation at 15 possible.

### "School stress"

Break work into steps. Protect sleep and downtime before protecting homework. Coordinate with teachers early.

Watch for anxiety showing up as perfectionism, avoidance, or physical symptoms rather than as worry they can name.

### "They lie about small things"

Common. Usually protecting themselves from your reaction rather than pursuing gain.

Make truth-telling reliably reduce the consequence, and then hold to that exactly. And ask yourself what they were afraid would happen — the answer is usually information about you.

## Red Flags: Talk to a Professional If

- Sadness, anxiety, anger, or school refusal that persists
- **Withdrawal from things they used to enjoy**
- Social isolation that's increasing
- Attention, learning, or behaviour concerns interfering significantly
- Marked change in sleep, appetite, weight, or functioning
- Any self-harm, or talk of not wanting to be alive — **take seriously at any age.** See [Module 14](../14-age-12-18/README.md#if-you-are-worried-about-suicide-or-self-harm)
- Signs of being bullied: reluctance to go to school, unexplained lost items, changed routes home, secrecy about devices

## 5-Minute Action Plan

**Ask them to teach you something.**

Something they know and you don't — a game, an app, a football rule, a YouTuber, how their favourite thing works.

Then actually listen. Ask follow-up questions. Get it wrong and let them correct you.

**Why this one:** it hits all three motivational needs at once. Competence (they're the expert), autonomy (they chose the subject), and relatedness (you're interested in them). It also quietly re-establishes that you're someone who wants to hear from them — which is the thing you'll need in three years.
